  Revision notes
  • The specific: credit-linked notes (S1). A CLN is a funded credit derivative: the investor pays the note
  principal upfront, earns a coupon that includes the credit premium, and is repaid less any credit loss, so the
  note investor is the protection seller and bears the credit risk, while the issuer (protection buyer) already
  holds the cash (outside-source: the CLN mechanics are not set out in the GARP excerpts). Choudhry (CR-
  23): CLNs issued through an SPV “do not have any credit-linkage to the sponsoring entity”; the note is
  linked to the assets sold to the SPV and its performance depends on those assets. A CLN issued directly by
  an originator also carries the issuer’s credit risk.
  • CDS mechanics (CR-9 g, CR-13 a; S3): the buyer pays the CDS spread (quarterly in arrears is standard)
  until maturity or a credit event (failure to pay, restructuring, bankruptcy); the seller pays L(1 − R). Physical
  settlement: deliver bonds for face value; now usually cash settlement, with an ISDA auction setting the
  cheapest-to-deliver bond price. Hull: 90 bps on USD 100 million is USD 225,000 per quarter; auction price
  35 gives a USD 65 million payoff plus a final accrual (about USD 150,000). Unlike insurance, the buyer
  need not own the underlying (Lehman: about USD 400 billion of CDS against USD 155 billion of debt).
  Restructuring is sometimes excluded in North America.
  • CDS and bond yields: buying a 7% bond and 200 bps protection approximates a 5% risk-free bond, so the
  CDS spread ≈ bond yield spread. CDS-bond basis = CDS spread − bond yield spread; positive pre-2007,
  negative in the crisis, mixed since. See also A15 for hazard rates from CDS spreads.
  • Valuing a CDS (CR-13 b, c): equate PV of expected spread payments plus accrual to PV of expected payoff.
  Hull: hazard 2%, r = 5%, R = 40%: 4.0728s + 0.0422s = 4.1150s = 0.0506, so s = 123 bps. An
  old deal at 150 bps is worth 4.1150 × 0.015 − 0.0506 = 0.0111 to the seller. Default probabilities must
  be risk-neutral, implied from bond prices or CDS quotes (100 bps implies a 1.63% hazard). The value is
  insensitive to R when the same R is used to imply PDs (PDs ∝ 1/(1 − R), payoff ∝ 1 − R); a binary CDS
  (fixed payoff) is not, priced at 205 bps.
  • Credit indices and fixed coupons (CR-13 d): CDX NA IG and iTraxx Europe each hold 125 investment
  grade names, rolled on 20 March and 20 September. Index 66 bps on USD 800,000 per name costs USD
  660,000 a year; a default cuts it by USD 5,280. The index is slightly below the average spread. Fixed coupons:
  price P = 100 − 100 × D × (s − c) with D the CDS duration; the buyer pays 100 − P upfront, then the
  coupon. iTraxx example: 34 bps quote, 40 bps coupon, D = 4.447, P = 100.27, so the seller pays the buyer
  0.27%.
  • CDS forwards and options (CR-13 e): a forward CDS is an obligation, a CDS option a right (premium
  upfront), to buy or sell protection at a set spread at time T ; both cease to exist if the reference entity defaults
  before T . A call (right to buy protection at 280 bps) is exercised if the spread is above 280 bps.
  • Basket CDS: add-up pays on any default; first-, second-, kth-to-default pay only on the kth default, then
  terminate.

  • Total return swaps (CR-13 a; S2): the payer passes the total return on a bond (coupons plus gain or loss) to
  the receiver against floating plus a spread. Used for financing: the payer buys and retains ownership of the
  bond; the receiver is in the position of having borrowed at floating plus spread. On default the swap usually
  ends with the receiver paying the excess of notional over the bond’s market value. The spread compensates
  the payer for the risk that the receiver defaults when the bond has fallen, so it depends on both credit qualities
  and their correlation. Similar to a repo.
  • Cash and synthetic CDOs (CR-13 a): a cash CDO funds a bond portfolio and tranche holders invest upfront;
  a synthetic CDO’s originator sells CDS protection on each name, and tranches absorb payouts in order (Hull:
  equity 0 to 5% at 1,000 bps, mezzanine 5 to 20% at 100 bps, senior above 20% at 10 bps; USD 2 million
  of payouts cut equity principal to USD 3 million). Tranche holders post the tranche principal as collateral.
  Single-tranche trading uses standard tranches: iTraxx 0 to 3, 3 to 6, 6 to 9, 9 to 12, 12 to 22 and 22 to 100%;
  CDX 0 to 3, 3 to 7, 7 to 10, 10 to 15, 15 to 30 and 30 to 100%.
  • Correlation and tranche risk: higher default correlation makes the equity tranche less risky and senior
  tranches more risky; with perfect correlation and zero recovery all tranches are equally risky. For a basket,
  higher correlation lowers the chance of one or more defaults and raises the chance of ten or more.

  Exam traps
  • Funded versus unfunded: a CLN investor pays cash upfront and bears the credit risk (protection seller); a CDS
  seller pays nothing upfront. Calling the CLN investor the protection buyer is a role flip.
  • TRS roles: the payer owns the bond and receives floating plus spread; the spread compensates the payer for the
  receiver’s default risk.
  • CDS is not insurance: the buyer need not own the reference obligation, and protection pays only on a credit
  event, not on a price fall.
  • Compound versus base (sibling swap): compound prices a single tranche; base prices the 0 to aq tranche.
  Compound shows a smile, base a skew.

  • Correlation polarity: higher correlation helps the equity tranche and hurts the senior tranche.
  • Risk-neutral, not real-world: CDS valuation uses risk-neutral default probabilities implied from bonds or CDS
  quotes.
