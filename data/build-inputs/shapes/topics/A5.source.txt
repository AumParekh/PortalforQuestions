  Revision notes
  • Equity as a call option (CR-9 l, CR-5 e): with one zero-coupon bond repaying D at T , ET = max(VT −
  D, 0). If VT < D it is rational to default and equity is worth zero. Doumpos: the shareholders are the buyers
  of the option and the creditors are the writers.
  • Two unknowns, two equations (CR-9 l, S2): V0 and σV are not observable. E0 (market capitalisa-
  tion) and σE (from historical equity data) are. Equation 9.4 (the BSM equity value) and equation 9.5
  (σE E0 = N (d1 )σV V0 , from Ito’s lemma with equity delta N (d1 )) are solved together: Hull uses Excel
  Solver minimising [F ]2 + [G]2 ; Doumpos uses “a simple iterative Newton-type algorithm”.
  • Inputs in Doumpos: face value of debt L is “usually taken as the short-term debt of the firm”; T is the time
  in years up to the repayment of the loan; r is the T-bill yield. Doumpos writes A, L, σA where Hull writes
  V0 , D, σV .
  • Leverage multiplier (S5): equation 9.5 rearranges to σE = N (d1 )(V0 /E0 )σV . The same delta gives
  ∆E/E0 ≈ N (d1 )(V0 /E0 )(∆V /V0 ): a higher leverage ratio V0 /E0 amplifies both the volatility and the
  return on equity relative to the assets.
  • Debt and expected loss (CR-9 l): market value of debt = V0 − E0 ; PV of the promised payment = De−rT ;
  expected loss = (PV promised − market value)/PV promised. Hull Example 9.4: 9.51 versus 9.40, about
  1.2%.
  • Risk-neutral PD (CR-9 m, S3): the company defaults when the option is not exercised; the probability is
  N (−d2 ). It is risk-neutral “because it is calculated from an option pricing model” with assets growing at r.
  • Real-world PD (CR-5 e, CR-9 k): replace r with the expected return on assets µ (Doumpos eq. 5.3); µ is
  estimated as the rate of change in A over past periods after solving 5.1 and 5.2. Since real-world asset growth
  is usually higher than r, risk-neutral PDs are higher than real-world PDs.
  • Which PD to use (CR-9 k): risk-neutral for valuation (pricing, credit spreads, CVA); real-world for scenario
  analysis (Hull 9.7.2). Either can come from Merton “provided that the model is calibrated appropriately”.
  • Distance to default (CR-9 m, S1): “the number of standard deviations the asset price must change for default
  to be triggered T years in the future”; one definition is d2 . As DD declines, default becomes more likely.
  Example 9.4: one-year DD = 1.14. The horizon is T years: nothing in either reading restricts Merton to one
  year (S7).
  • Short-form DD (S6): with a one-year horizon and a small drift, DD ≈ ln(V /F )/σV . Use it when the
  question says “approximation” or drift ≈ 0; use the full d2 when r or µ is given and asked for.

  • Moody’s KMV EDF (CR-5 e, f): the best-known implementation of Merton. Two differences: the default
  point is short-term debt plus half of long-term debt, and DD is mapped to PD through an empirical distribu-
  tion of historical default frequencies, not the normal distribution.
  • Quality of Merton PDs (CR-9 n): Merton and its extensions produce a good ranking of default probabilities,
  so a monotonic transformation converts the output to a real-world or risk-neutral PD. Moody’s KMV and
  Kamakura sell that transformation into a real-world PD; the assumption is that the rankings of risk-neutral,
  real-world and Merton PDs are all the same. These market-based estimates do not target stability and respond
  faster than agency ratings.
  • Limitations and extensions (CR-5 e): basic Merton assumes one zero-coupon bond, default only at T ,
  constant σV , unobservable V0 , σV . Extensions: default whenever assets fall below a barrier; debt payments at
  more than one time; implied volatilities (Hull et al. 2004). Evidence is mixed: Hillegeist et al. find structural
  models beat empirical models; Agarwal and Taffler, Campbell et al. find little added information; reduced
  form approaches were found superior in most cases.
  • Comparison of approaches (CR-5 f): structural models treat default as endogenous (assets versus debt);
  reduced form models treat it as a random event driven by an exogenous Poisson jump process calibrated to
  bond and credit-derivative prices. CreditMetrics is a mark-to-market model on a rating transition matrix,
  recovery rates and yield spreads (not a rating tool or pricing model). CreditRisk+ (Credit Suisse Financial
  Products, 1997) is default-mode, Poisson (mean equals variance), does not use the capital structure and needs
  minimal data.
  • Hazard conversion (CR-9 e, S4): a cumulative PD Q(T ) from Merton converts to an average hazard rate
  λ̄ = − ln(1 − Q)/T (Hull eq. 9.2), not Q/T .

  Exam traps
  • r versus µ (wrong-input twin): N (−d2 ) built on r is risk-neutral and higher. A “real-world” PD for scenario
  analysis needs µ; a “risk-neutral” PD for valuation or CVA keeps r.
  • d1 versus d2 , and the sign: PD is N (−d2 ), not N (−d1 ) (too low) and not N (d2 ) (the probability of no default).
  In equation 9.5 the multiplier is N (d1 ), not N (d2 ) and not 1.
  • σE versus σV : equity volatility only calibrates σV . It never goes into d2 . A stem that lists both is planting a
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A5  Merton Model, Distance to Default and Risk-Neutral PD
  red herring.
  • Hazard conversion: the average hazard rate is − ln(1 − Q)/T . Dividing the cumulative PD by T understates
  it; forgetting /T overstates it.
  • Default point: KMV uses short-term debt plus half of long-term debt, not total debt (DD too low) and not
  short-term debt alone (DD too high).
  • Scope (absolute claim): “Merton cannot be applied beyond one  √ year” is false: DD is defined for default “T
  years in the future” and the drift scales with T , volatility with T .
