  Revision notes
  • Non-parametric approaches estimate risk measures without strong assumptions about the P/L distribution:
  they “let the P/L data speak for themselves”. Core assumption: the near future will be sufficiently like the
  recent past.
  • HS P/L (eq. 2.1) is the P/L the current portfolio would have earned had it been held through the sample
  period, not the P/L actually earned. Basic HS VaR is the highest loss observation outside the tail (1000 obs
  at 95%: the 51st highest loss, 1.704 in the book’s example; 100 obs: the 6th); ES is the average of the losses
  in the tail (2.196 against a true 2.063). See also A13 for MR-1 HS VaR.
  • Bootstrapped HS (MR-2 a): resample from the existing data set with replacement; each resample gives a
  VaR, and each resample ES is “the average of the losses in each resample exceeding the resample VaR”. The
  best estimates are the means of the resample estimates (1000 resamples: about 1.669 and 2.114, closer to the
  true 1.645 and 2.063). Often more accurate than the raw sample estimate and useful for gauging precision.
  • Bootstrap confidence intervals (Appendix 2): the percentile interval reads the bounds off the quantiles of
  the bootstrapped estimates (book: 95% VaR [1.554, 1.797], ES [1.986, 2.271]); the bias-corrected and accel-
  erated (BCa ) method corrects the end-points and is transformation-respecting. The order-statistics approach
  gives very similar intervals (VaR [1.552, 1.797]). Bootstrap estimates have little bias but often substantial
  variance.
  • Bootstrap limitation: standard bootstrap procedures presuppose that observations are independent over
  time. Remedies: bootstrap GARCH residuals (exposes us to model and parameter risk), a block approach
  (can “whiten” the data), or selection probabilities that depend on recently chosen observations.
  • Non-parametric density estimation (MR-2 b): basic HS only gives VaR at discrete confidence levels set
  by sample size (100 obs: 95% yes, 95.1% no). Drawing lines through the tops of histogram bars, or using
  kernels, gives a surrogate pdf, so VaR can be estimated at any confidence level. Kernels are theoretically
  superior, but four kernels all gave 1.735 versus basic HS 1.704: “they do not necessarily produce much better
  estimates in practice”.
  • Weighted HS is semi-parametric: it adds parametric features (GARCH volatility forecasts) to the HS frame-
  work. Problems with equal weights: the 1/n weight is hard to justify, risk estimates are unresponsive to major
  events, the iid assumption is unrealistic, and weights drop suddenly to zero at age n, creating ghost effects.
  • Age-weighted HS (Boudoukh, Richardson and Whitelaw, BRW): weights decay exponentially with age;
  λ close to 1 means slow decay. Equal-weight HS is the special case λ → 1. Rear-view mirror analogy:
  equal-weight HS is safe only if the road is straight; age-weighting is safe if it bends gently.

  • Age-weighting attractions: (1) generalises traditional HS; (2) more responsive to large recent losses and
  better with loss clusters; (3) reduces distortions from unlikely-to-recur events and reduces ghost effects (an
  observation leaves with weight λn w(1) < 1/n); (4) lets the sample size grow over time, improving efficiency
  and effectively eliminating ghost effects.
  • Age-weighting drawbacks: reduces the effective sample size; a sequence of major profits or losses can
  distort the risk profile; VaR can still be insufficiently responsive (Pritsker); the approach is ad hoc: except
  for λ = 1 no asset-return process makes BRW theoretically correct.
  ∗
  • Volatility-weighted HS (Hull and White, HW): replace each historical return by rt,i  = (σT,i /σt,i ) rt,i .
  Returns are increased when the current forecast is greater than the forecast for day t, and decreased when it
  is less; then estimate VaR or ES in the traditional equal-weight way.
  • HW advantages: handles volatility changes in a natural, direct way (age-weighting treats them “in a rather
  arbitrary and restrictive way”); sensitive to current GARCH information; VaR and ES can exceed the maxi-
  mum historical loss; HW evidence shows superior VaR to BRW. Can be combined with age-weighting and
  with order-statistics or bootstrap confidence intervals.
  • Correlation-weighted HS (Duffie and Pan suggestion): adjust returns to the current correlation matrix us-
  ing Choleski matrices, R̃ = ÃA−1 R. A major generalisation of HW (correlations as well as volatilities).
  Example 2.1: historical ρ = 0.3 to current 0.9 gives a second row [0.7629, 0.4569].
  • Filtered HS (Barone-Adesi et al.): a semi-parametric bootstrap. Fit an asymmetric GARCH (leverage ef-
  fect), divide realised returns by their volatility forecasts to get iid standardised returns, bootstrap these and
  multiply by tomorrow’s volatility forecast. For a multi-asset portfolio, bootstrap whole standardised vectors,
  which keeps the correlation structure without a variance-covariance matrix.
  • FHS attractions and ES: combines HS with a sophisticated volatility treatment; fast; VaR and ES can exceed
  the maximum historical loss; can be combined with order-statistics or bootstrap intervals. FHS “can also be
  extended easily” to ES (Giannopoulos and Tunaru). Evidence is mixed: Pritsker finds FHS VaR may not pay
  enough attention to extreme observations or time-varying correlations; “a partial response to the first point
  would be to use ES instead of VaR”.
  • Advantages of non-parametric methods (MR-2 d): intuitive and simple; accommodate fat tails, skewness
  and other non-normal features; any position type including derivatives; easy on a spreadsheet; no covari-
  ance matrices or curse of dimensionality; readily available data; easy to report; confidence intervals easy to
  produce; can be refined with semi-parametric add-ons.
  • Disadvantages: results are very (often completely) dependent on the historical data set; a quiet sample gives
  VaR too low, a volatile sample too high; slow to reflect shifts and major events; unlikely-to-recur extremes
  can dominate; ghost (shadow) effects; no allowance for plausible events that did not occur; constrained by
  the largest historical loss, so poorly suited to extremes with small or medium samples.
  • Window length: most experts want at least 500 daily observations; a longer window worsens aged data,
  ghost effects, drowning-out of current news and data-collection problems. Never rely on non-parametric
  methods alone: complement them with stress testing.

  Exam traps
  • Volatility polarity (the documented trap): HW scales returns up when current volatility is above the volatility
  on the day they were observed. The reversed statement, and the inverted ratio σt /σT , are the distractors.
  • Dropped normaliser: using w(i) = λi−1 (1−λ) without dividing by 1−λn (or using raw λi−1 as probabilities).
  Weights then do not sum to 1.
  • Exponent off by one: the weight on an i-day-old observation uses λi−1 ; yesterday’s observation carries the full
  w(1).
  • Tail-counting convention: VaR is the first observation outside the tail (100 obs at 95%: the 6th largest loss,
  not the 5th). In age-weighted HS, keep accumulating until the cumulative weight first exceeds 1 − α.
  • Sibling swap on the maximum loss: basic HS and the bootstrap cannot produce a VaR or ES above the largest
  historical loss; HW and FHS can, because returns are rescaled to current volatility.
  • Bootstrap scope: the standard bootstrap assumes independence over time; “resampling fixes clustering” is false.
