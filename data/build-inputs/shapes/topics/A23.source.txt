  Revision notes
  The specific: cyber-resilience and its supervision (ORR-8 a, b)
  • Definition. BCBS uses the FSB Cyber Lexicon: cyber-resilience is “the ability of an organisation to con-
  tinue to carry out its mission by anticipating and adapting to cyber threats and other relevant changes in
  the environment and by withstanding, containing and rapidly recovering from cyber incidents”. Cyber-risk
  management (vulnerabilities and threats) is narrower than IT risk management.
  • Initiatives. G20 (March 2017) warning on malicious use of ICT; G7 Fundamental Elements of Cyber-
  security; CPMI-IOSCO guidance for FMIs (June 2016; resume critical operations within two hours); EU
  Fintech Action Plan; BCBS Operational Resilience Working Group. Banking is the only financial sector
  in which all FSB jurisdictions have issued regulation, guidance or supervisory practice. Most jurisdictions
  treat cyber through the lens of IT and general operational risk, drawing on NIST, ISO 27000 and COBIT.
  Examples (Box 8.1): APRA CPS 234 (board ultimately responsible for information security; notify APRA
  of material incidents), BaFin BAIT, US proposed rules, NYDFS (requires a CISO).
  • Strategy. Most regulators do not require a standalone cyber-security strategy, but all expect a board-approved
  information security strategy, policy and procedures; cyber risk is expected to sit in the organisation-wide
  risk or information security framework. Three enforcement approaches: regulator-set strategy (emerging
  markets), bank-set strategy reviewed by the regulator, IT strategy review (Europe).
  • 3LD and the CISO. Most regulators use the 3LD model to assess cyber risk, but most do not require it or
  prescribe how responsibilities are split; supervisory focus is greater on lines 1 and 2 than on line 3, which
  could hamper checks and balances; few specify an independent reporting line from the chief audit executive
  to the board audit committee. The CISO usually reports to the CRO or CIO with no independent line to
  the CEO or board; placing the CISO under the CRO is not ideal (the CRO may prioritise compliance over
  innovation), so some banks have the CISO report to the CEO or board. SAMA requires a cyber-security
  function independent from IT.
  • Culture and workforce. Cyber-risk awareness and a common risk culture are prerequisites; screening at
  hiring and periodic re-verification against insider threats. Only Hong Kong, Singapore and the UK have
  dedicated frameworks to certify cyber-workforce skills (HKMA PDP, MAS board training, BoE CBEST and
  CREST).
  Testing, response, metrics, sharing, third parties (ORR-8 c, d, e)
  • Supervision and testing. Off- and on-site reviews, risk specialists, triage tools for front-line supervi-
  sors, powers to appoint a third-party auditor. Mapping and classifying business services underpins testing.
  Regulator-led penetration tests are typically voluntary, bank-funded and aimed at larger institutions, mostly
  testing protection and detection. TIBER-EU (ECB, May 2018): first Europe-wide threat-intelligence-based
  red-teaming framework; tailor-made; no pass or fail.

  • Response and recovery. Most regulators require a prevention, detection, response and recovery framework,
  but few issue cyber-specific continuity rules. Exercise Resilient Shield (UK/US, 2015) rehearsed communi-
  cation and coordination; it was not a test of firms.
  • Metrics are not mature. No quantitative metrics comparable to financial risk; reliance on backward-looking
  indicators, while adaptive adversaries (DDoS) call for forward-looking ones. Patch ageing is a widespread,
  comparable metric; no single metric suffices and no standard benchmark exists.
  • Information sharing, five types. Among banks 75%; bank to regulator 75%; with security agencies 68%;
  regulator to banks 32%; among regulators 29% (least observed, often ad hoc via supervisory colleges).
  Threat intelligence is most shared among banks; incident information flows to regulators and security agen-
  cies. Incident reporting is fragmented (taxonomy, time frames from immediately to 72 hours, templates,
  thresholds) and one-directional. FS-ISAC: anonymised, member-driven. HKMA-MAS (2018): voluntary,
  timely, effective, confidential, Traffic Light Protocol.
  • Third parties. Broad: outsourcing including cloud; non-outsourcing services (power, telecoms, hardware,
  software); interconnected counterparties and FMIs. Board-approved outsourcing framework; contracts cov-
  ering strategic, compliance, security, continuity, vendor lock-in, counterparty, country, contractual, access
  and concentration risk; inventory of outsourced functions; rights to inspect and audit (some jurisdictions
  grant them to supervisors); pooled audits. If an institution outsources, the ultimate responsibility remains
  with the institution. A supervisory college model for large cloud providers could cover blind spots.
  Threats, frameworks and Equifax (ORR-9 a, b)
  • Information security is wider than cyber. Four quadrants: internal vs external causes, theft vs loss or
  involuntary disclosure, including paper and archives. McAfee 2017: 43% of data leaks initiated by insiders,
  half unintentionally. COVID changed the threat landscape (remote work, digitalisation, QR codes, social
  media).
  • Frameworks. NIST CSF: voluntary; identify, protect, detect, respond, recover. CIS: 18 critical security con-
  trols (version 8). ISO/IEC 27001: certification, ISMS, PDCA cycle. CIA: confidentiality and integrity (se-
  curity), availability (resilience). Behavioural controls (awareness, conduct, training) and technical controls
  (preventive: architecture, access, firewalls, encryption, patching; detective: DLPD, honeypot). Technical
  monitoring sits mostly in IT (first line); the information security department is most often second line.
  • Equifax (2017). Apache Struts flaw exploited via the online dispute portal on 10 March; the patch was re-
  leased 7 March and an alert sent on 8 March to 400 staff, but it was never applied; hackers undetected for
  about three months. Five weaknesses: no comprehensive IT asset inventory; failure to enforce patch manage-
  ment (8,500 unpatched vulnerabilities found by internal audit in 2015); inconsistent internal communication;
  an expired SSL certificate that blinded monitoring of encrypted traffic until 29 July; poor external communi-
  cation (announced 7 September, 143 million customers, later 147 million). About USD 5bn of USD 15.5bn
  market capitalisation lost; CEO, CSO, CIO resigned; up to USD 700 million in fines and compensation.
  Lesson: large events have multiple causes in weak control environments. (Ch.1 Box 1.1 quotes 145 million
  and USD 3.5bn; use the Ch.9 figures.)
  ORMF, characteristics and operational resilience (ORR-1 a, c, d)
  • ORMF and scope. Operational risk: “the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes,
  people, and systems, or from external events”; includes legal risk, excludes strategic and reputational risk,
  though RPSMOR 2021 says they should be considered “where appropriate”. Frameworks reduce to iden-
  tification, assessment, mitigation, monitoring, plus governance, culture and appetite. See also A22 for the
  seven event types (ORR-1 b).
  • Characteristics. Heterogeneous, idiosyncratic and diffuse (“everyone is a risk manager”; cannot be elim-
  inated), heavy-tailed, interconnected (boundary events with credit and market risk), dynamic (cyber and
  resilience followed digitalisation; TSB 2018).
  • Operational resilience. UK first (BoE, PRA, FCA discussion paper, July 2018): the ability to prevent,
  adapt, respond to, recover from and learn from disruptions; components: continuity of business services,
  important business services (a service-based rather than process-based view: services whose disruption
  causes intolerable harm to consumers or market integrity), impact tolerances, management of disruption,
  lessons learned. Mindset: severe disruptions are occasionally inevitable, so prevent, detect and correct. Fed
  (2020) Sound Practices. BCBS Principles (March 2021), seven: governance, operational risk management,
  business continuity planning and testing, mapping interconnections and interdependencies, third-party de-
  pendency management, incident management, ICT including cybersecurity; “operational resilience is an
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A23 Cyber Risk and Operational Resilience
  outcome that benefits from the effective management of operational risk”. ECB DORA draft (September
  2020); MAS and ABS remote working paper (March 2021). COVID: remote working and critical payments
  under conditions beyond tested scenarios; fraud attempts tripled and phishing rose six- to seven-fold (see
  also A29).
  Exam traps
  • Absolute claim on 3LD: most regulators use 3LD to assess cyber risk; they do not require it or prescribe the
  split of responsibilities.
  • Polarity flip on information sharing: sharing among regulators is the least observed type (29%); among banks
  and bank to regulator are the most common (75%).
  • Scope error on strategy and testing: a standalone cyber-security strategy is expected but not required; TIBER-
  EU gives no pass or fail; cyber metrics are not mature and have no standard benchmark.
  • Case cross-mapping on Equifax: not a zero-day attack; the patch existed for three days and the failures were
  asset inventory, patch enforcement, communication and an expired SSL certificate.
  • Sibling swap on resilience: impact tolerances attach to important business services, not to internal processes;
  resilience sits alongside existing operational risk requirements rather than replacing them.
  • Role misassignment on third parties: outsourcing never transfers ultimate responsibility to the provider or to
  the supervisor.
