  Revision notes
  • Risk budgeting (IM-6 a): “the process of allocating and managing risk using a top-down approach to dif-
  ferent aspects of the investment process”. It builds on VaR measures applied to asset classes, asset managers
  and even securities, and is “equivalent to a top-down allocation of economic risk capital”. It gives a dynamic
  comparison of current risk profiles with prespecified risk budgets.
  • Sell side versus buy side (IM-6 b): bank trading has a short horizon (1 day, intraday), rapid turnover and high
  leverage, so historical risk measures are “basically useless” and VaR, position limits, VaR limits and stop-loss
  rules dominate. Investors have long horizons (month, quarter, years), slow turnover and low leverage; risk is
  managed through asset allocation, tracking error, diversification, benchmarking and investment guidelines.
  • Investment process of large investors (IM-6 c): step one, a consultant’s strategic long-term asset-allocation
  study, usually mean-variance, sets the amounts per asset class using passive benchmarks; step two, money
  is delegated to active managers reviewed on tracking error and controlled by investment guidelines. Risk is
  usually measured ex post. VaR is needed because investing is global, instruments are complex (Wisconsin’s
  MexSpread swap: thought USD 10 million at risk, lost USD 35 million on one contract) and positions are
  dynamic across many managers.
  • Hedge funds (IM-6 d): heterogeneous, mostly leveraged, some with high turnover (LTCM), so closer to
  bank desks. Funds holding illiquid assets (e.g. convertibles) report a stale NAV, which artificially lowers
  correlations (fix: regressions with lagged market factors, summing coefficients, Dimson) and artificially
  lowers volatility (shows up as positive serial autocorrelation). Lack of transparency on positions is the third
  problem. See also D9 and A24.
  • Absolute versus relative risk (IM-6 e): absolute (asset) risk is the risk of a dollar loss over the horizon,
  return Rasset . Relative risk is the dollar loss of the fund relative to its benchmark; the relevant return is
  Jorion’s tracking error e = Rasset − RB , and with normality VaR = αW σe .
  • Policy-mix versus active-management risk (IM-6 e, S1): policy-mix risk is the risk of a dollar loss owing to
  the policy mix selected by the fund; since it can be implemented with passive funds, it is the risk of a passive
  strategy. Active-management risk is the risk of a dollar loss owing to the total deviations from the policy
  mix, summed across all managers (with diversification across styles), and includes temporary deviations of
  the current asset mix from the policy mix.

  • The decomposition (eq. 6.2): Rasset = i wip Rib + i (wi Ri − wip Rib ). Total VaR comes from the policy-
  mix VaR, the active-management VaR and a cross-product term. OTPPB (99%, annual): policy-mix 19.6%,
  active 1.6%, asset 19.3%. Most risk is policy mix (Brinson et al. 1986); active VaR is small (diversified
  styles, near-indexed assets); the two do not add up, and a slightly negative correlation means managers could
  take larger deviations without raising total VaR.

  • Funding risk (IM-6 e): the risk that assets will not be sufficient to cover the fund’s liabilities, measured on
  the surplus S = A − L. Nominal liabilities behave like a short position in a long-term bond, so falling rates
  can raise liabilities more than equities rise. Minimum-risk position: immunisation (asset duration equal to
  liability duration), with an opportunity cost. Defined-benefit plans leave the shortfall to the fund’s owner;
  defined-contribution plans put the risk on the employees. Also called surplus at risk (SAR).
  • PERF example: assets 1,000, liabilities 900, surplus 100; expected surplus growth 5% of assets = 50;
  surplus volatility 9.4% gives a 99% VaR of 22% or 220. Expected surplus 150 minus 220 = −70: a 1%
  chance of a √
  deficit of 70 or more. OTPPB: 99% over 1 year is “equivalent” to 90% over 4 years because
  (1.28/2.33) 4 ≈ 1.
  • Sponsor risk (IM-6 e): the risk to the plan sponsor, split into cash-flow risk (year-to-year fluctuations in
  contributions) and economic risk (variation in the sponsor’s total economic earnings). Pension management
  should be integrated with the sponsor’s financial goals, in line with enterprisewide risk management.
  • VaR to monitor risk (IM-6 f): VaR checks compliance (Common Fund: USD 138 million unauthorised
  loss, then an independent risk oversight officer and fewer managers) and gives a central position repository.
  Passive benchmarks do not keep risk constant (the late-1990s tech weight in the S&P 500). A VaR jump has
  three causes, each needing a different action: a manager taking more risk, different managers taking similar
  bets (visible only at portfolio level), or more volatile markets. Global custodians are the natural centralisation
  point; large plans may build their own systems.
  • VaR for guidelines and the investment process (IM-6 g): limits on notionals ignore risk and correlations;
  limits on sensitivities have blind spots (hedged portfolios); structured notes evade both. VaR limits are
  comparable across assets and capture diversification, leverage and derivatives, e.g. “tracking-error volatility
  of at most 3%”. Marginal VaR picks between equal-return stocks (utility with portfolio beta 0.5 versus
  Internet stock with 2.0); Chrysler’s currency position had individual VaR of 44 but incremental VaR of only
  3, so it was not hedged. See also A12.
  • Budgeting across asset classes (IM-6 h): PERF’s 10% total volatility on 1,000 gives 2.33 × 0.10 × 1,000 =
  233. Individual risk budgets are ασi wi W (U.S. stocks 2.33 × 0.151 × 525 = 184); they sum to 308,
  the undiversified VaR. Two equal managers in U.S. stocks (16% volatility, correlation 0.78) get 98 each,
  recombining to 184. Managers within budget trade without approval: no micromanaging.
  • Budgeting across active managers (IM-6 h): IR = µ/ω (Grinold and Kahn: 0.50 is “good”, top quartile).
  Maximise portfolio IR subject to a TEV target with independent active returns: xi ωi = (IRi /IRP ) ωP , so
  relative risk budgets are proportional to IRs and any slack sits in the index (TEV
  √  0). Jorion’s example: 55%,

  Exam traps
  • Policy weights versus current weights (wrong-input twin): policy-mix VaR uses the policy weights on bench-
  mark returns. The current mix’s deviation from policy belongs to active-management risk.
  • Non-additivity (absolute claim): policy-mix VaR plus active-management VaR is not the asset VaR; OTPPB’s
  19.6% and 1.6% gave 19.3% because of a slightly negative correlation.
  • Undiversified versus diversified: the sum of individual risk budgets (308 for PERF) is the undiversified VaR;
  the fund VaR (233) is lower. A distractor plants the sum.
  • Cash-flow versus economic risk (sibling swap): cash-flow risk is fluctuation in contributions; economic risk
  is variation in the sponsor’s total economic earnings.
  • Who bears funding risk (role): the fund’s owner in a defined-benefit plan; the employees in a defined-
  contribution plan.
  • Jorion’s “tracking error”: Jorion’s
  qPTE is the active return itself and TEV its volatility; IR = µ/ω. Portfolio
  IR with independent managers is  IR2i , not the sum or the average.
