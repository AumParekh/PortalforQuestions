  Revision notes
  • The specific: TSECF and TSAA (LTR-7 d, e). The book’s abbreviation is TSECF (term structure of ex-
  pected cash flows); “TSEF” in recall is the same object. The TSECF collects expected positive and negative
  cash flows by date; the TSECCF cumulates them; the TSAA records, for each date, the quantity of an asset
  in the bank’s possession, regardless of ownership. A purchase records the dirty-price outflow, coupons and
  redemption in the TSECF and raises the TSAA until expiry; the TSLGC is unaffected.
  • The specific: trend, seasonal and cyclical components (LTR-5 c, sources and uses of funds approach).
  Forecast deposits (sources) and loans (uses) as the sum of a trend element (constant-growth line from at
  least 10 years of data), a seasonal element (how the week or month typically compares with the most recent
  year-end level) and a cyclical element (last year’s actual minus last year’s trend-plus-seasonal estimate for
  that week, assumed to recur). Estimated liquidity deficit (−) or surplus (+) = change in deposits − change
  in loans. Book Table 5.2: deficits of 150, 200, 100, a surplus of 550, then a deficit of 200. See also A30.
  • Cash-flow taxonomy (LTR-7 a). Time and amount, each deterministic or stochastic. Deterministic amount
  and time: coupons and amortisation of risk-free fixed-rate bonds and mortgages. Deterministic amount,
  stochastic time: one-touch option payout, the bank’s withdrawals from credit lines it has received. Stochas-
  tic amount: credit related (missing flows and recoveries after default), indexed/contingent (Libor coupons,
  European and American option payouts), behavioural (deposit withdrawals, credit-line drawdowns, prepay-
  ments), new business (new loans and deposits, rollover, new debt issuance).
  • Liquidity options (LTR-7 b). The right of a holder to receive cash from, or give cash to, the bank at pre-
  defined times and terms; exercised because of the holder’s need for (or surplus of) liquidity, not for profit.
  Examples: committed credit lines sold to clients, sight and savings deposits, prepayment of fixed-rate mort-
  gages or loans. Two impacts: a liquidity impact (amount withdrawn or repaid) and a financial impact (con-
  tract vs market rate and spread). The financial effect can be partly hedged; the liquidity impact only by cash
  reserves, liquid assets or credit lines.
  • Liquidity risk definitions (LTR-7 c). Definition 7.3.1: receiving smaller than expected cash flows to meet
  payment obligations (covers funding and market liquidity risk). Definition 7.3.2, funding cost risk: paying
  a greater than expected spread over risk-free to raise funds. Definition 7.3.3: economic losses because cash
  flows plus existing cash differ from the desired level.
  • Causes vs sources (LTR-7 c). Causes: flows of existing and forecast contracts from ordinary business; they
  enter the TSECF. Sources: factors that generate positive cash flows to manage liquidity risk and can be
  disposed of promptly; they form the LGC and do not enter the TSECF. The maturity ladder is the TSECF up
  to one year (short-term liquidity); beyond is structural liquidity.
  • TSECCF (LTR-7 d). The perfect condition is a TSECCF positive at all times; it cannot be guaranteed
  because flows are only expected values and maturity mismatches create negative periods. A negative expected
  TSECCF means the bank may become insolvent.

  • Liquidity generation capacity (LTR-7 c). The ability to generate positive cash flows beyond contractual
  ones: balance sheet expansion (unsecured funding, credit-line withdrawals, new bonds) or shrinkage (asset
  sales); repo is “balance sheet neutral”. Three sources: asset sales (AS), secured funding and repo (RP),
  unsecured funding (USF). AS and RP give balance sheet liquidity (BSL). Only unencumbered (“available
  for sale”) assets count, and a bond pledged for a period must be excluded from the TSLGC for that period.
  • Transaction effects (LTR-7 e, Table 7.4). Repo: TSAA down, cash in the TSCLGC (haircut applied), TSECF
  unchanged because coupons stay with the owner bank; the repo interest enters the TSECF at the end. Reverse
  repo: TSAA up, cash out in the TSECF (haircut applied). Sell/buyback: TSAA down, cash in the TSCLGC.
  Buy/sellback: TSAA up, purchase and sellback in the TSECF, TSCLGC unchanged, coupons belong to the
  bank. Security lending: TSAA down, fee in the TSECF; the only contract that decreases LGC. Security
  borrowing: TSAA up, only the fee in the TSECF.
  • Term structure of expected liquidity (LTR-7 c). TSLe = TSECCF + TSCLGC, with TSECCF(t0 , t0 ) =
  Cash(t0 ); it must always be positive for the bank to stay solvent. Example 7.5.1: the TSECCF is −3.2 in
  year 7, so 4 notional of a bond is sold at 99.00 for 3.96, and the lost coupons and redemption are fed back
  into the TSECF.
  • Cash flow at risk (LTR-7 c). Positive cfaR = cash flow at confidence α minus the expected cash flow;
  negative cfaR at 1 − α; driven by market, credit and behavioural risk factors and usually simulated (the
  book uses CIR with 10,000 scenarios). Cumulating the unexpected term structures makes little sense; a term
  structure of liquidity at risk combines cash flows and LGC.
  • Types of liquidity (LTR-9 a). Operational: cash to fund the business daily and clear payments; unavailable
  under stress. Restricted: liquid assets usable only for defined purposes (e.g. collateral for wholesale borrow-
  ings); applied only to the outflows they support. Contingent: available for general obligations under stress,
  held as the liquid asset buffer. Strategic: held for future business needs (acquisitions, capital expenditure);
  its high-quality part can be redirected to contingent needs.
  • Contingent liquidity and the buffer (LTR-9 b). Stressed liquid asset buffer = liquid asset buffer − stressed
  outflows + stressed inflows (inflows may be capped, e.g. 75% of outflows under the LCR). Buffer assets need
  low credit and market risk, ease and certainty of valuation, an active sizable market, low concentration of
  buyers and sellers, and central treasury control.
  • Stress test design (LTR-9 c). Scope: the organisational level where liquidity is commingled and account-
  able; transfer restrictions, currency, jurisdiction. Horizon at least 12 months, daily early on then weekly or
  monthly. Techniques: historical CFaR, deterministic scenarios (preferred), Monte Carlo. Scenarios: sys-
  temic, idiosyncratic and combined, graduated severity, clearly defined; historical (WaMu, Northern Rock)
  or hypothetical; reverse stress tests. Assumptions: deposit runoff (most important), unsecured wholesale
  funding, haircuts, collateral, contingent liabilities, business dial back.
  • Outputs, governance, integration (LTR-9 c). Available liquidity relative to net outflows, survival hori-
  zon, prospective metrics; at least quarterly. ALCO owns the framework and approves scenarios; Treasury
  (first line) owns the modelling; independent risk management (second line) challenges and monitors limits;
  internal audit (third line) reviews; model risk management validates. Link to capital stress testing, ALM
  (consistent deposit behaviour) and FTP.
  • Liquidity reports (LTR-10 a, b). Deposit tracker (LTD ratio, board limit e.g. 85%); daily liquidity re-
  port (liquid assets, liabilities by tenor, cumulative liquidity, counterbalancing capacity, liquidity risk factor);
  funding maturity gap (mismatch) and cash flow survival horizon; funding concentration (large depositor: a
  deposit of 5% of total liabilities; the book’s top depositor at 16.5% breaches a 10% limit); undrawn commit-
  ments; liability profile; firm-specific funding yield curve; a 1-page monthly summary for senior management;
  qualitative reports from branches and subsidiaries.
  • Stress test reporting (LTR-10 c). The primary output is the cash flow survival report, before and after miti-
  gating actions (sale or repo of marketable securities, contingency funding), with behavioural stickiness (OBF)
  assumptions stated; a 1-slide summary for senior management; a quarterly line-by-line report of individual
  and combined shocks with their probability and impact. Conservative treatment of derivative collateral and
  undrawn commitments is best practice. LCR and NSFR: see A32.

  Exam traps
  • Wrong term structure (sibling swap): repo and sell/buyback cash goes to the TSCLGC; reverse repo and
  buy/sellback cash goes to the TSECF; the TSAA follows possession, not ownership; security lending is the only
  contract that decreases LGC.
  • Haircut and accrual: haircuts apply to repo and reverse repo cash, not to sales, buy/sellbacks or sell/buybacks;
  accrued interest is c × τ since the last coupon, not the full semiannual coupon.
  • Single flow vs cumulative, and initial cash: solvency is read from the cumulated TSECCF starting from
  Cash(t0 ), and the asset sale must cover the most negative point, not the final one or one year’s flow.
  • Intermediate result: the repo repayment at maturity is not the cash raised at the start.
  • Sources and uses direction (polarity flip): rising loans and falling deposits drain liquidity, so the gap is
  ∆deposits − ∆loans; the cyclical element is actual minus expected; dropping the cyclical element misstates the
  requirement.
  • Liquidity types (sibling swap): restricted is tied to a specific exposure such as pledged collateral; strategic is
  for M&A or capital projects; operational is needed daily; only contingent liquidity meets general obligations
  under stress.
