  contingent actions, market signals); S4 testing requirements and operational readiness (notes: testing).
  Revision notes
  • What a CFP is (LTR-11 a): a CFP “serves as a logical connection to its companion, the liquidity stress testing
  framework, by linking the stress test results and other related information as inputs to the CFP governance,
  menu of contingent liquidity actions, and decision framework.” Low-impact, high-probability events are
  handled in business-as-usual (BAU) funding and liquidity management; the CFP addresses the high-impact,
  low-probability end.
  • Relationship with stress testing (LTR-11 a): CFP scenarios should be aligned to the liquidity stress testing
  scenarios and to recovery and resolution plans. The stress tests cover systemic and idiosyncratic risks, market
  (asset) and funding liquidity, over short-term and prolonged periods; the CFP “in turn should provide a
  tactical mechanism for escalating a developing crisis to management’s attention and ensuring actionable
  responses are available.” The CFP may add its own scenarios outside the limit structure: the Fedwire intraday
  debit cap is exceeded, specific counterparties fail, or Federal Home Loan Bank funding becomes unavailable.
  See also A26 (LTR-9 stress test design).
  • Supervisory backdrop (LTR-11 b): the 2008 EU report on 84 institutions; the Fed’s Enhanced Prudential
  Standards require a CFP for US banking organisations with USD 50 billion or more of assets, with policies
  on governance, roles, liquidity measures, triggers, contingent actions and communication, tailored to the
  institution and regularly tested. CLAR and daily liquidity reporting link to its measures; the CFP bridges
  BAU liquidity management and recovery and resolution planning. Smaller institutions often embed their
  CFP in business continuity plans; larger ones formalise it, often with several CFPs by subsidiary or legal
  entity.
  • Five design considerations (LTR-11 b): (I) aligned to business and risk profiles, with the risk appetite state-
  ment tied in through quantifiable EWIs, limits and escalation levels, and refreshed as the business changes
  (leaders build it into strategic planning); (II) integrated with broader risk management (ERM, capital man-
  agement, business continuity and crisis management) and explicitly linked to the liquidity stress test through
  the limit structure and escalation levels; (III) operational and actionable but a flexible playbook, balancing
  recommended actions with management discretion; (IV) inclusive of stakeholder groups (ALCO, risk and
  capital committee, investment committee, business units, finance, treasury, risk, operations and technology);
  (V) supported by a communication plan.
  • Five key components (LTR-11 c): (1) governance and oversight; (2) scenarios and liquidity gap analysis;
  (3) contingent actions; (4) monitoring and escalation; (5) data and reporting.
  • Roles (LTR-11 c, S1): corporate treasury monitors the funding and liquidity profile in BAU; “the treasurer, in
  consultation with the CFO and others, may invoke the CFP and convene the liquidity crisis team (LCT)”. The
  LCT (C-level executives, heads of segments, geographies and legal entities) is the central point of contact,
  monitors continuously, recommends CFP actions, and “is responsible for designing the CFP and submitting
  it to the senior management group for review and approval”. The management committee oversees the LCT
  during a crisis and consults the board. The board is actively engaged as advisor and counsel, especially on
  strategic actions such as large asset or subsidiary sales.
  • Communication (LTR-11 c): messaging is centrally managed (“everyone should be working off the same
  page”), but each relationship stays with its existing owner: business units with clients and counterparties;
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D26  Contingency Funding Planning
  corporate treasury with regulators, rating agencies and clearing banks; investor relations with investors, ana-
  lysts and media; legal and compliance with regulators and supervisors. Clear, timely communication sustains
  confidence; loss of confidence “can quickly spiral downward when rumors start to take over”.
  • Policies and procedures (LTR-11 c): document the CFP and align it with risk management, business con-
  tinuity and recovery planning. Illustrative outline: purpose, related policies, governance, roles, review and
  approval, periodic review, stress testing and scenarios, methodology (scenario design, inputs, key assump-
  tions, data control, model validation), monitoring and escalation (liquidity gap analysis, contingent actions),
  reporting frequency and briefing decks.
  • Testing (LTR-11 c, S4): periodically test targeted elements for relevance and execution effectiveness. Busi-
  ness divestitures, large asset sales and Federal Reserve borrowing “may be impractical or unavailable for
  testing”; debt issuance, brokered deposit issuance and limited securities sales from the investment portfolio
  may be appropriate to test. Also test governance, escalation, communication, coordination and reporting;
  frequent simulation exercises improve response times and expose gaps a purely theoretical design would
  miss.
  • Contingent actions menu (LTR-11 c, S3): chosen in light of the size, timing and pattern of the shortfall, the
  expected relief and the ability to execute. Examples: maintain committed lines of credit; tighten underwrit-
  ing and dial back lending; raise deposit pricing; roll off (not reinvest) maturing securities; shift short-term
  to longer-term funding; brokered CDs or direct-to-consumer deposits; securitise retail assets; pledge assets
  at the discount window; sell liquid assets; draw securitisation conduits; issue subordinated debt; slow asset
  growth; at-call loans; sell consumer loans or businesses; raise equity; cut capital distributions and discre-
  tionary spending.
  • Reliability under stress (LTR-11 c, S3): availability depends on whether the stress is systemic or idiosyn-
  cratic and how severe. Impediments: securitisation markets shut; repo access restricted by solvency concerns,
  downgrades or reputation; downgrade-driven collateral and margin calls; predatory margin practices; higher
  custodian cash deposits; higher funding costs; deposit runoff; collapse of interbank and wholesale funding;
  counterparties unwilling to roll. The CFP should document mitigants for each.
  • Signalling risk (LTR-11 c, S3): early defensive steps (curtailing business, tightening credit, limiting risky
  counterparties) may “send inadvertent signals” that reduce the availability of later actions. Hedge funds
  may move prime brokerage elsewhere; debt holders may force buybacks (agreeing drains liquidity, refusing
  questions viability). Accessing a sovereign or emergency facility may make lenders “immediately withdraw
  existing credit”; selling businesses or suspending reverse repo can read as distress, so such actions belong to
  later stages.
  • Early warning indicators (LTR-11 c, S2): market and business measures (external and internal) plus liquid-
  ity health measures (internal), chosen together so the institution can tell idiosyncratic from systemic stress.
  Macro: repo spreads, haircut trends, CDS spreads. Industry: sector profitability, rating actions, capital ade-
  quacy, peer analysis. Institution-specific: stock price drop, rapid asset growth funded by volatile liabilities,
  negative publicity, falling asset quality or earnings, rating downgrades, wider debt and CDS spreads, higher
  collateral demands, counterparties refusing unsecured or longer-term deals, early withdrawal requests, debt
  buyback calls, FX volatility. EWIs are most useful before or at the onset of a crisis. See also D7 (LTR-3).
  • Liquidity health measures (LTR-11 c, S2): projected net funding requirements to unused funding capacity;
  non-core funding to long-term assets; overnight borrowings to total assets; short-term liabilities to total
  assets; funding source concentration; funding maturity profile; used to total borrowing capacity; liquid assets
  to volatile liabilities; unpledged eligible collateral to total assets; loans to commitments; for advanced firms,
  daily liquidity reporting, LCR and NSFR. Their importance “is related to their associated limits”.
  • Escalation levels (LTR-11 c, S2): no set number; three to five is common practice. Level 1: elevated mon-
  itoring, triggered for example by stress test results showing liquidity falling below risk appetite targets or
  a shift in market perception; LCT convened, communication plan executed. Level 2: noticeable market or
  idiosyncratic events hurting the business; closer monitoring of peers and counterparties, curtailing activity
  and new credit, strategic sales of less liquid assets, evaluating larger actions. Level 3: dramatic steps, focus
  “primarily on survival”, resembling recovery and resolution. Movement up or down “should be explicitly
  considered and approved by the LCT”; Level 1 actions may need only ALCO or the Treasurer, Levels 2 and
  3 involve senior management and/or the board. Expert judgement is not to be understated.
  • Data and reporting (LTR-11 c): many institutions would benefit from more frequent liquidity reporting
  beyond treasury and funding desks (senior management, ALCO, risk committees, board), with context and
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D26  Contingency Funding Planning
  qualitative guidance; reports should capture intraday positions, contingent liabilities and capacity usage in
  funding sources.
  • Institution type and structure (LTR-11 b, c): depositories focus on deposit runoff; wholesale-funded firms
  on credit lines and funding markets; insurers fund from policy liabilities and emphasise cash flow matching.
  Revolver lenders must model drawdowns; prime brokers and dealers face client withdrawals and collateral
  calls; wholesale borrowers face higher margins and haircuts. One document rarely covers everything, so
  firms run a portfolio of CFPs by business, product, geography and FX, and legal entity (ring-fenced entities
  under another regulator have their own; some build a currency CFP).
  • Liquidity and capital (LTR-11 b): historically siloed; an integrated approach brings common EWIs and a
  joint view of liquidity and capital stress responses, aligned with recovery and resolution and ERM. Effective
  CFP attributes: strong management involvement, alignment with capital and risk frameworks, a wide range
  of scenarios, a documented action plan, and a communication plan. The CFP “is a playbook” that needs
  regular updating.
  Likely distractors
  • Corrupted: The CFP is designed mainly for frequent, low-impact funding shortfalls, while severe, low-
  probability events are left to business-as-usual liquidity management.
  True: BAU manages the low-impact, high-probability events; the CFP addresses the high-impact, low-
  probability ones. (Polarity flip.)
  • Corrupted: Once an early warning indicator breaches its limit, the institution moves automatically to the next
  escalation level without further approval.
  True: movement between levels, up or down, “should be explicitly considered and approved by the LCT”, and
  management’s expert judgement matters. (Scope/condition error.)
  • Corrupted: A well-designed institution should cover all businesses, geographies and legal entities in a single
  CFP document.
  True: a single document is unlikely to cover them clearly; institutions run a portfolio of CFPs (business, product,
  geography and FX, legal entity, ring-fenced entities). (Absolute claim.)
  • Corrupted: Investor relations should manage the institution’s communications with rating agencies and clearing
  banks during a liquidity crisis.
  True: corporate treasury owns regulators and supervisors, rating agencies and clearing banks; investor relations
  owns investors, analysts and media. (Role misassignment.)
  • Corrupted: Drawing early on an emergency central bank facility is a low-cost first response, because it signals
  that official support is in place.
  True: accessing a sovereign or emergency facility may lead lenders to withdraw existing credit at once; such
  actions may be necessary later but early signals of weakness “may actually precipitate a liquidity crunch”.
  (Polarity flip on the signalling effect.)
  • Corrupted: Periodic CFP testing should include business divestitures and Federal Reserve borrowing, since
  these are the actions most likely to be needed in a severe crisis.
  True: those actions “may be impractical or unavailable for testing”; debt issuance, brokered deposits and limited
  portfolio sales may be tested, along with governance, escalation and communication. (Scope/condition error.)
