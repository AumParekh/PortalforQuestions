  Revision notes
  The specifics first: SMB, HML, style, value vs momentum, low risk (IM-2 d, e; IM-3 a, f, h, i)
  • SMB, not SML (S1). Fama-French (1993) adds two factors to the CAPM market factor: SMB, the returns
  of small stocks minus big stocks (market capitalization), and HML, high book-to-market (value) minus low
  book-to-market (growth) stocks. The security market line is a different object: the CAPM beta pricing
  relationship E(ri ) − rf = βi (E(rm ) − rf ). The SML is a pricing line; SMB is a tradeable long-short factor.
  • Factor-mimicking, long-short portfolios. SMB = USD 1 in small caps minus USD 1 in large caps; HML
  = USD 1 in value minus USD 1 in growth. Loadings s and h: s = 0 for a medium-size stock, s > 0 moves
  with small stocks, s < 0 with large; h > 0 value orientation, h < 0 growth. The market is size neutral and
  value neutral (zero s and h): the average stock has only market exposure, just as the average investor holds
  the market. Fama and French did not discover size and value; they gave a parsimonious model to capture
  them.
  • Berkshire Hathaway as a factor benchmark. CAPM: alpha 0.72% per month, beta 0.51, adjusted R2 14%.
  Fama-French: alpha 0.65%, market 0.67, s = −0.50, h = 0.38 (large, value), adjusted R2 27%. Benchmark:
  USD 0.33 T-bills + 0.67 market − 0.50 small + 0.50 large + 0.38 value − 0.38 growth. Adding UMD:
  loading −0.04, insignificant, R2 unchanged, alpha 0.68%.
  • Size has disappeared. Banz (1981) and Reinganum (1981): small stocks beat large after adjusting for beta.
  Since the mid-1980s there has been no significant size effect, internationally too. Two responses: data
  mining (Fischer Black, 1993; the “file drawer problem”) or rational investors bid up small caps until the
  effect vanished (Grossman-Stiglitz near-efficiency), so size should be removed from the model. Small stocks
  still have higher raw returns, are less liquid, and value and momentum are stronger in them; the unconstrained,
  investment-only case for a small-cap tilt is not compelling.
  • Value is robust (IM-2 a). Value stocks have low prices relative to book value; value has produced gains
  for fifty years but lost in the early-1990s recession, the late-1990s internet bull market and 2007 to 2008.
  Rational: value stocks co-move and the common risk cannot be diversified away; firm investment risk (Berk,
  Green and Naik: options exercised when market returns are low); Zhang/Cochrane: value firms are stuck
  with unproductive capital (high, asymmetric adjustment costs). Behavioral: overextrapolation of past growth
  (Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny, 1994); loss aversion plus mental accounting (Barberis and Huang, 2001).
  The book-to-market effect is a three- to six-month effect, unlike Buffett’s five- to ten-year “deep value”.
  • Value in other asset classes. Buying high yields (low prices) and selling low yields: riding the yield
  curve in fixed income (a duration premium), the roll return in commodities, carry in FX (long high-rate
  currencies, short low-rate).
  • Momentum (S3). WML (winners minus losers) or UMD: buy stocks with the highest returns over the past
  six months or so, short the lowest. Cross-sectional, like size and value; returns an order of magnitude
  larger than size and value; observed in every asset class (“trend” investing). Momentum is not the opposite
  of value: correlation of HML with WML only −16%. Value is a negative feedback, stabilizing strategy;

  momentum is positive feedback, destabilizing and prone to crashes (skewness −1.43). Of the eleven largest
  crashes, seven were in the Great Depression, one in 2001, three in 2008 (bailouts lifted the loser financials
  the strategy was short). Explanations mainly behavioral: overreaction (conservatism bias; overconfidence
  and biased self-attribution) or underreaction (Hong and Stein’s “news watchers”).
  • Investing style (S2). “Style factors”, “investment factors” and “dynamic factors” are used interchangeably
  (practitioners: “smart beta”, “alternative beta”). Static factors (the market) are simply held long; dynamic
  factors require constant trading. Many hedge funds and private equity funds are bundles of dynamic factors.
  For the asset owner: are these bad times bad for me? The volatility premium has a rational story, momentum
  a behavioral one, value both.
  • Style analysis (IM-3 f). Sharpe (1992): a factor benchmark whose exposures evolve through time, built
  from tradeable low-cost funds (SPY, SPYV, SPYG), weights summing to one, estimated over the previous
  60 months, with or without a no-shorting constraint. It fixes two shortcomings of Fama-French regressions:
  the factor portfolios are not tradeable, and loadings vary over time. Buffett: strongly value in 2006, growth
  in the crisis, value again after. Allowing shorting reduces alphas; time-varying styles have larger standard
  errors.
  • The low-risk anomaly (S4; IM-3 a, h). Three effects: volatility is negatively related to future returns;
  realized beta is negatively related to future returns; minimum variance portfolios beat the market. Volatility
  anomaly (Ang et al., 2006): the highest idiosyncratic-volatility quintile returned 0.1%; raw Sharpe ratios
  fall from 0.8 to 0.0. Beta anomaly: average returns are roughly flat across beta quintiles (about 15%, 12.7%
  for the top quintile), but Sharpe ratios fall from 0.9 to 0.4; contemporaneous beta and returns are positively
  related; the real mystery is that past betas predict future betas poorly.
  • BAB and VOL factors. BAB (Frazzini and Pedersen) goes long low-beta and short high-beta, each scaled
  to unit beta (so the long side is levered); VOL scales to a 15% target volatility and can be traded without the
  risk-free asset. Correlation BAB/VOL −9%: the anomalies are distinct. Alphas: BAB 0.33% per month (t
  1.89, borderline), VOL 0.42% (t 4.37). Both tilt to value and momentum; BAB loads +0.29 on SMB, VOL
  −0.63: the beta anomaly shows more in small stocks, the volatility anomaly in large, liquid stocks. Do both.
  • Explanations of the risk anomaly (IM-3 i). Data mining (but the effect appears across countries, bonds,
  credit, commodities, FX, options); leverage constraints (explain overpricing of high-beta stocks, not the
  underpricing of low-beta stocks; and institutions actually underweight high-risk stocks); agency problems
  (market-weighted benchmarks with tracking error limits stop managers buying low-risk stock A or shorting
  high-risk stock B); preferences (“hopes and dreams”, lottery preferences explain close to half); disagreement
  with short-sale limits (Hong and Sraer). Buffett’s alpha: 12.5%, 11.1% after BAB, 7.0% after a quality factor.
  The surrounding material: factor theory and CAPM (IM-1)
  • Factor theory (IM-1 a). Factors are to assets what nutrients are to food: factors matter, not assets; assets
  are bundles of factors; different investors need different factors. Unlike nutrients, factor risks are bad: each
  factor defines a set of bad times, and investors are paid premiums for bearing losses in them. 2008 did not
  kill diversification: asset-class labels misled investors about common factor exposures.
  • CAPM lessons (IM-1 b, c). Hold the factor (the market diversifies away unrewarded idiosyncratic risk); each
  investor has his own optimal factor exposure; the average investor holds the market; the market premium has
  an economic story, E(rm ) − rf = γ̄σm  2
  ; risk is factor exposure (beta = ρi,m σi /σm : high beta means low
  diversification benefit); assets paying off in bad times have low, even negative, risk premiums. Equilibrium:
  demand equals supply, so the MVE portfolio becomes the market. The CAPM is “a spectacular failure”
  empirically but remains the workhorse.
  • CAPM assumptions and failures. Only financial wealth (human capital, Jagannathan and Wang); mean-
  variance utility (downside risk earns a premium); single period (minor; Merton’s dynamic CAPM); homoge-
  neous expectations (matters with short-sale limits: Miller, 1977); no taxes or transaction costs; price takers;
  costless information. Failures should be most visible in illiquid, inefficient markets.
  • Multifactor models and the SDF (IM-1 d, e). APT (Ross, 1976): factors cannot be arbitraged or diversified
  away. The pricing kernel (SDF) m is an index of bad times; the CAPM is the special case m = a + b rm with
  b < 0. Pi = E[m × payoffi ], 1 = E[m(1 + ri )], 1/(1 + rf ) = E[m]. Higher covariance with m (paying
  off in bad times) means a lower risk premium. Bad times for the representative agent: high marginal utility,
  low consumption or growth.
  • Efficient markets (IM-1 f). Grossman-Stiglitz (1980): markets cannot be perfectly efficient if information
  is costly; active managers earn returns in pockets of inefficiency (illiquid, poor information). Deviations are
  rational (premiums persist, scalable) or behavioral (persist only with barriers to entry of capital).

  Macro factors, volatility, benchmarks (IM-2 b, c; IM-3 c, e, g)
  • Macro factors. Shocks matter more than levels. Growth: large stocks 5.6% in recessions vs 12.4% in
  expansions; government bonds 12.3% vs 5.9%; high yield shows no discernible difference. Inflation: high
  inflation hurts both stocks (14.7% low vs 8.0% high) and bonds. Volatility: VIX changes correlate −0.39
  with stocks (leverage effect) and only 0.12 with bonds; large stocks −4.6% in volatile vs 24.9% in stable
  times. Also productivity (correlation 48%), demographic and political risk.
  • Managing volatility risk. Volatility has a negative price of risk: buy protection (puts, volatility swaps)
  or hold more bonds; collect the premium by selling out-of-the-money puts (VIX implied about 2% to 3%
  above realized). Selling volatility had skewness −8.26 (only −0.37 before 2008) and losses near 70% in
  September to November 2008. Rebalancing is a short-volatility strategy. Challenge: estimates of γ in the
  return-volatility trade-off are positive, negative and zero.
  • Alpha is about the benchmark. Martingale: alpha 1.50% (IR 0.24) against the naive Russell 1000, 3.44%
  (IR 0.78) against the beta-adjusted 0.27 T-bills/0.73 Russell benchmark. Sound benchmarks are well defined,
  tradeable, replicable and risk adjusted. CalPERS: alpha −1.11% (t −1.16) against 32% bonds/68% stocks:
  fail to reject. Nonlinear (short-option) strategies produce illusory alpha; include tradeable nonlinear factors
  or test nonlinear terms. See also A18 for alpha, tracking error, IR, Sharpe and the fundamental law (IM-3 b,
  d).

  Exam traps
  • Sibling swap, SMB vs SML: SMB is the Fama-French size factor (small minus big); the SML is the CAPM’s
  beta pricing line. A “loading on SML” does not exist.
  • Scope error on the market’s loadings: the market (and the average stock) has zero SMB and HML loadings
  and a beta of one; only tilted stocks carry nonzero s and h.
  • Sibling swap, size vs value: size vanished after the mid-1980s; value has been robust for fifty years; momentum
  earns the most but crashes. Swapping these robustness profiles is the standard distractor.
  • Polarity flip on feedback: value is negative feedback (stabilizing); momentum is positive feedback (destabiliz-
  ing). Momentum is not the opposite of value (correlation −16%).
  • Sibling swap, beta vs volatility anomaly: the beta anomaly lives in Sharpe ratios (average returns are flat), so
  BAB must lever the low-beta leg; the volatility anomaly shows in average returns, so VOL trades directly. The
  source sentence on SMB signs runs opposite to its own table: rely on the conclusion, beta anomaly in small
  stocks, volatility anomaly in large stocks.
  • Absolute claim on explanations: leverage constraints explain only the overpricing of high-beta stocks; no
  single explanation covers the whole anomaly (lottery preferences, the best group, explain close to half).
