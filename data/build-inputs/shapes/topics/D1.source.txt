  Revision notes
  • Why volatility should depend on the rate (MR-15 d): earlier models assume basis-point volatility inde-
  pendent of the rate level, which is “almost certainly not true at extreme levels”. Periods of high inflation
  and high short rates are inherently unstable, so basis-point volatility tends to be high; at very low rates it is
  limited because rates cannot decline much below zero.
  √
  • CIR process (MR-15 d, S1): risk-neutral
  √  dynamics dr = k(θ −r)dt+σ r dw. The drift term is not random
  and √the standard deviation of dw is dt, so the annualised standard deviation of dr, the basis-point volatility,
  is σ r. The parameter σ is constant; basis-point volatility is not, and increases with the level of the short
  rate (S2).
  • Proportional volatility (MR-15 d, S4): Courtadon dr = k(θ − r)dt + σr dw and the simplest lognormal
  model, Model 4, dr = ar dt + σr dw. Here σ is called yield volatility: constant as a percentage of the rate,
  while basis-point volatility σr rises in proportion to the rate.
  • Units and calibration (MR-15 e): basis-point volatility is in rate units (100 bp); yield volatility is a
  percentage
  √  of the rate (12.5%). Setting all three specifications to 100 bp at an 8% rate: σbp = 0.01;
  σCIR 8% = 1% ⇒ σCIR = 0.0354; σY × 8% = 1% ⇒ σY = 12.5%. CIR and proportional volatil-
  ity both rise with the rate but at different speeds, and both equal zero at a zero rate (Figure 15.2).
  • Why CIR rates cannot go negative (MR-15 d, S3): basis-point volatility equals zero when the rate is zero,
  and the drift is positive when the rate is zero. This is “in some respects” an improvement over constant-
  volatility models that allow negative rates, but model choice depends on purpose: a trader who believes
  constant volatility fits the current environment, that negative rates barely affect the securities, and who values
  computational simplicity may well accept some probability of negative rates.
  • Distribution shape matters (MR-15 d): with the same mean (5%) and standard deviation (2.32%) after 10
  years, the CIR, normal and lognormal terminal distributions differ enough away from the mean to give signif-
  icantly different out-of-the-money option prices. The shape of the distribution is “an important determinant
  of that model’s performance”.
  • Salomon Brothers model: lognormal with deterministic drift (MR-15 f, S4): dr = a(t)r dt + σr dw; by
  Ito’s lemma d[ln r] = [a(t) − 12 σ 2 ]dt + σ dw, so ln r is normal and r is lognormal. It is “the Ho-Lee model
  √
  based on the natural logarithm of the short rate”. Tree nodes: r0 ea1 dt±σ dt .
  • Multiplicative perturbations (MR-15 f): because ex > 0, every node is positive whenever r0 > 0. Since
  ex ≈ 1 + x, the up node is about r0 (1 + σ) for a1 = 0, dt = 1: with σ = 12.5% the up-state rate is
  12.5% above the initial rate, which is why lognormal volatility is quoted as a percentage of the rate. The drift
  constants a1 , a2 are fitted to bond prices, as in Ho-Lee.

  (CIR, lognormal) or set negative tree rates to zero. See also A7 and A25.

  Exam traps
  √
  • σ is not the bp vol in CIR: the constant parameter (for example 0.05) must be multiplied by r (95 bp at 3.6%),
  not read as 500 bp.
  √  √
  • Unit/time conversion: drift scales with dt, the random term with dt. Forgetting dt on the drift or dt on the
  shock are separate named mistakes.
  √
  • Sibling swap r versus r: CIR basis-point volatility rises with the square root of the rate; lognormal (Courta-
  don, Model 4, Salomon Brothers, Black-Karasinski) rises in proportion to the rate.
  • Mean reversion does not prevent negative rates: Vasicek mean-reverts but has constant basis-point volatility
  and a normal distribution, so rates can go negative. Non-negativity comes from volatility falling to zero at zero
  (CIR) or from multiplicative shocks (lognormal).
  • Salomon Brothers versus Black-Karasinski: deterministic drift only versus mean reversion in the log rate.
  Ho-Lee has no mean reversion and no long-run level.
  • Absolute claim: “CIR is always the better model” is false; the book’s trader may rationally choose a constant-
  volatility model.
