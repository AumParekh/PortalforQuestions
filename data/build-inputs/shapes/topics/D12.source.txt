  Revision notes
  • Expectations alone (MR-13 a): with certainty that the one-year rate will be 8%, 7% and 6%, P (1) = 1/1.08,
  P (2) = 1/(1.08 × 1.07), P (3) = 1/(1.08 × 1.07 × 1.06), so forward rates equal the expected rates. Without
  volatility, expectations completely determine the term structure, which can take any shape.
  • Granularity of expectations (MR-13 a): views can be specific over short horizons (central bank meeting
  dates, tax dates, issuance calendars, quarter-end balance sheet management) but not over long ones: the
  expected one-year rate in 29 years is unlikely to differ from that in 30 years. Macroeconomics may anchor a
  long-run expectation (book: 4% = 1% real rate + 3% inflation).
  • Volatility in a risk-neutral tree (MR-13 b): book tree with annual steps and 50% probabilities: 9% today;
  13% or 5%; 17%, 9% or 1%. Expected rate is 9% at every horizon and each transition has a volatility of
  400 bp. Prices of one-, two- and three-year zeros: 0.917431, 0.842815, 0.776366; forward rates 9.0000%,
  8.8532%, 8.5590%. The forward curve slopes down although expectations are flat: interest rate volatility
  interacting with the convexity of bond prices.

  Exam traps
  • Polarity of Jensen: the expected price exceeds the price at the expected rate; hence forward rates lie below
  expected rates once volatility is added.
  • Polarity of the convexity value: it increases with volatility and with maturity. A statement that it falls with
  maturity, or is independent of volatility, is a flip.
  • Scope: the convexity effect is not a parallel shift of forwards: equal per-step volatility still gives a larger effect
  at longer terms because C grows with term.
  • Absolute claim: being long convexity does not earn a riskless excess return; the lower forward rate (lower yield)
  is the price paid for convexity.
  • Sign in the decomposition: expectations and risk premium enter with a plus sign, convexity with a minus sign.
