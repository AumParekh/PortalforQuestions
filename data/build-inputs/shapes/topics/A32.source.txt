  Revision notes
  • Why a leverage ratio (ORR-22 f): minimum capital had been a percentage of RWA; during and after the
  crisis many felt RWA understated risk and left banks over-leveraged, and market participants focused on
  simple ratios of equity to unweighted assets.
  • Leverage ratio requirement (ORR-22 f): a “simple” supplement to the risk-based requirements, not a re-
  placement: Core Tier 1 capital / Leverage Exposure ≥ 3%. Leverage Exposure includes on-balance-sheet
  assets and fractions of off-balance-sheet assets (e.g. derivatives, potential future exposures), specified in
  detail for cross-country comparability because IFRS and GAAP differ.
  • 2017 leverage ratio framework (ORR-23 b): the leverage ratio complements risk-weighted requirements,
  guarding against unsustainable leverage and mitigating gaming and model risk across internal models and
  standardised approaches. G-SIB leverage buffer: met with Tier 1 capital, 50% of the G-SIB’s risk-weighted
  higher-loss absorbency requirement (2% HLA gives a 1% buffer), divided into five ranges with distribution
  constraints. Refinements to the exposure measure (derivatives; off-balance-sheet items consistent with the
  SA credit conversion factors). National discretion to exempt central bank reserves temporarily in exceptional
  circumstances, with a commensurate recalibration of the minimum and disclosure. Earlier US rule: 2 and 3
  percentage point leverage buffers (5% and 6% minimums).
  • Why liquidity requirements (ORR-22 f): solvent institutions can fail because depositors and counterparties
  withdraw faster than assets can be sold. Northern Rock, dependent on securitisation and wholesale funding,
  lost retail deposits after news (Thursday 13 September) of Bank of England liquidity support; by the following
  Monday all UK deposits were guaranteed. Lesson: central bank funding can make funding stress worse and
  destabilise a system, so banks must survive funding stress with their own resources: a smaller proportion of
  illiquid assets and a larger proportion of stable liabilities.
  • LCR (ORR-22 f): gives banks and authorities a month to manage a crisis by selling liquid assets while
  restoring confidence. LCR = HQLA / net cash outflows over 30 days ≥ 1. “Liquid” means a high likelihood
  of a quick sale with little price reduction.
  • HQLA (ORR-22 f): central bank deposits and 0%-risk-weight sovereign securities with no haircut; corpo-
  rate debt and equity 50% haircut; individual mortgage loans excluded. Level 2 assets (e.g. BBB corporate
  securities) may not exceed 40% of HQLA after haircut.
  • Net cash outflows (ORR-22 f): stress run-off assumptions, e.g. 3% of insured retail deposits, 100% of most
  non-operational wholesale deposits, 30% of undrawn lines of credit to nonfinancial wholesale customers.
  “The definition of the LCR is simple but the implementation is complicated.”
  • NSFR (ORR-22 f): a one-year horizon, focused not on what can be sold but on what funding would remain
  after a stressful year. NSFR = available stable funding / required stable funding ≥ 1.
  • ASF factors weight liabilities and equity and are “similar to haircuts”: equity and 10-year bonds with none
  maturing within the year 100%, stable retail deposits 95%, 3-month wholesale CDs 0%. RSF factors weight
  assets and are higher the more illiquid the asset, since it cannot be sold easily when funding runs off: vault
  cash 0%, own-sovereign debt 5%, BBB corporate securities in the trading account 50%, business loans over
  one year with risk weights of 50% or more 85%. The NSFR categories differ from those of the LCR.

  • The book’s example (ORR-22 f): ASF = 475 + 0 + 200 + 100 = 775; RSF = 0 + 5 + 50 + 595 = 650;
  NSFR = 1.19. LCR: HQLA = 100 + 100 + 50 = 250 (corporate debt USD 50 is within the 40% Level 2
  cap); outflows = 5% × 500 + 100% × 200/3 = 25 + 67 = 92; LCR = 2.72.
  • A major change in regulation (ORR-22 f): before the crisis the presumption was that regulators would
  instantly know if a bank was solvent and could fund it until confidence returned. The new ratios constrain
  balance-sheet composition instead. The initial Basel III phase introduced the LCR and NSFR to mitigate
  “excessive liquidity risk and maturity transformation” (ORR-23 a).
  • Classic swaps: LCR = 30 days, stock of liquid assets versus stressed outflows (short-term resilience); NSFR
  = one year, structural funding (ASF versus RSF). The leverage ratio is a capital measure on unweighted
  exposure, not a liquidity ratio, though it sits in the same LO.
  • See also C5 (Basel III capital, buffers and the December 2017 reforms) and A26 (liquidity stress testing,
  TSECF and TSAA).

  Exam traps
  • Inversion: NSFR is ASF/RSF. RSF/ASF gives a number below 1 for a compliant bank.
  • LCR factors in an NSFR question (sibling swap): HQLA factors are not RSF factors (cash: HQLA 100%,
  RSF 0%); LCR run-off rates are not ASF haircuts; the LCR’s “one-third of CDs maturing this month” has no
  role in the NSFR.
  • Dropped term: omitting common equity (ASF 100%) or the 10-year bonds from ASF; using total assets or
  total funding as the denominator.
  • Two-leg remedies: new funding adds ASF and its deployment adds RSF; dividing the gap by the ASF factor
  alone understates the amount.
  • Polarity: RSF factors rise with illiquidity; ASF factors fall as funding becomes less stable (short wholesale
  CDs: 0%). Treating short CDs as stable inflates the NSFR.
