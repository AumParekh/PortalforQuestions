  Revision notes
  Why extremes are hard (MR-3 a)
  • The core problem: extreme events are rare by definition, so there are few extreme observations; estimates
  are very uncertain, especially for events beyond the range of the historical data (an unprecedented market
  fall).
  • Why not fit one distribution to everything: a distribution fitted to the whole data set accommodates the
  many central observations, not the sparse extremes: good for the centre, ill-suited to the tails. Extremes are
  governed by extreme-value theorems, not central limit theorems; do not use distributions justified by central
  limit theory (especially the normal) for extremes. The standard analogy is hydrology: how high to build
  dikes and sea walls.
  GEV: the distribution of block maxima (MR-3 b)
  • Fisher-Tippett theorem: for iid losses, as sample size n grows, the distribution of the sample maximum
  Mn converges to the generalised extreme value (GEV) distribution. Three parameters: location µ (central
  tendency of Mn ), scale σ (dispersion of Mn ), and the tail index ξ (shape, heaviness of the tail). µ and σ are
  related to, but are not, the mean and standard deviation.
  • Three cases of ξ: ξ > 0 Frechet (power-law, heavy tails: Levy, Student t, Pareto parents; “particularly
  useful for financial returns”, with estimates typically positive but below 0.35). ξ = 0 Gumbel (exponential,
  relatively light tails: normal and lognormal parents). ξ < 0 Weibull (lighter than normal tails; not useful for
  financial returns). The parent’s domain of attraction decides the case.
  • Frechet versus Gumbel: both right-skewed; Frechet is more skewed with a longer right tail. Frechet quan-
  tiles rise with ξ and tend to the Gumbel values as ξ → 0. Example 3.5 (µ = 2%, σ = 0.7%, ξ = 0.3,
  n = 100): 99.5% VaR 2.537% Frechet versus 2.483% Gumbel; 99.9%: 4.322% versus 3.612%. The GEV
  probabilities refer to the extreme-loss distribution: its 5th percentile is a small extreme loss but sits in the
  right tail of the parent.
  • Choosing Frechet or Gumbel: pick the EV distribution whose domain of attraction contains the parent; or
  test the significance of ξ (danger: wrongly accepting ξ = 0 underestimates risk); safest given model risk:
  always choose the Frechet.
  • Parameter estimation: maximum likelihood (well grounded, consistent and asymptotically normal if ξ >
  −1/2, but needs numerical methods, may not be robust, small-sample issues); Gumbel’s regression method;

  k is a bias/variance trade-off: higher k means more data, lower variance, higher bias. Hill plots can turn
  into “Hill horror plots” that never settle; Danielsson and de Vries minimise MSE but need at least 1500
  observations.

  • Horizon scaling: the mean scales with h, the standard deviation with h, the tail index not at all; empirical
  VaR scaling exponent is about 0.45, so the square-root rule is usually inappropriate for EV distributions.
  POT and the generalised Pareto (MR-3 c, e)
  • Peaks over threshold: models the distribution of excess losses X − u over a high threshold u. GPBdH the-
  orem (Gnedenko-Pickands-Balkema-deHaan): as u gets large, the excess-loss distribution converges to the
  generalised Pareto (GP) distribution, “pretty much regardless” of the parent distribution. Two parameters
  only: positive scale β and shape ξ (the same tail index as GEV).
  • POT risk measures: F (u) is estimated by the proportion of observations below the threshold, (n − Nu )/n;
  VaR follows from inverting eq. 3.21, and ES = VaR plus the mean excess loss over VaR, valid for ξ < 1.
  Example 3.6 (β = 0.8, ξ = 0.15, u = 2%, Nu /n = 4%): 99.5% VaR 3.952%, ES 5.238%; at 99.9%: 5.942
  and 17.578.
  • Threshold choice, the weak spot of POT: u must be high enough for GPBdH to apply reasonably closely,
  but if too high there are too few exceedances for reliable estimates. It is “inevitably arbitrary and therefore
  judgmental”, “very much akin to the problem of choosing k”: a lower u gives more data and lower variance
  but more bias. Aid: choose u where the mean-excess function becomes horizontal. ML estimators are
  asymptotically normal for ξ > −0.5.
  GEV versus POT (MR-3 d)
  • Same theory, different targets: GEV models the distribution of extremes (maxima); POT models ex-
  ceedances over a high threshold. GEV disadvantages: typically one additional parameter, and the block
  maxima approach can lose useful data because some blocks contain more than one extreme. POT disad-
  vantage: the threshold must be chosen, a problem that does not arise with GEV. “Either approach is usually
  reasonable”; choose what suits the problem (e.g. limited data).
  • Operational-risk framing (ORR-4 f): the LDA is complemented by EVT or scenario data for the tail. Block
  maxima (Fisher-Tippett) examines “the maxima equally spaced in time” (the maximum loss per period and
  per unit of measure); POT focuses on observations above a high threshold u, whose excess converges to the
  GPD. Word of caution: EVT is valid only “if a single mechanism is responsible for all observed losses”;
  with losses from different institutions, business lines and risk types this is unlikely. Alternatives: scenarios,
  causal modelling, fixed multipliers, macro determinants.
  Refinements and multivariate EVT (MR-3 f)
  • Conditional EV: unconditional EVT suits long horizons; for short horizons with dynamic structure, fit a
  GARCH-type model and apply EVT to its iid residuals (McNeil and Frey two-step).
  • Dependent (clustered) data: violates iid, can produce very poor estimator performance and changes how
  often a VaR is breached. Remedies: GEV on block maxima (maxima are less clustered; costs efficiency and
  creates a block-length bandwidth problem), or the tail of the conditional (GARCH-standardised) distribution;
  advanced: the extremal index.
  • Multivariate EVT: beyond elliptical distributions, correlation no longer suffices; the limiting multivariate
  extreme distribution is a member of the family of EV copulas, and tail dependence is the central focus.
  Curse of dimensionality: with independent variables and 1-in-100 univariate extremes, a joint extreme is 1
  in 10,000 for two variables and 1 in 1,000,000 for three. Extremes are not independent in practice (earthquake,
  tsunami, market crash).
  • Limitations of EVT: few extreme observations and wide confidence intervals (not a fault of EVT), consid-
  erable model risk and subjective judgment (thresholds), and sensitivity to small-sample effects, biases and
  non-linearities. Estimate confidence intervals and stress test. “We shouldn’t ask more of the theory than it
  can deliver” (Diebold et al.).

  Exam traps
  • Polarity flip on ξ: ξ > 0 is Frechet, heavy tails, the relevant case for financial and loss data; ξ < 0 is Weibull,
  lighter than normal tails. Statements that attach “lighter” or “Weibull” to a positive tail index are wrong.
  • Sibling swap GEV/POT: “three parameters” and “wastes data in blocks” belong to GEV (block maxima); “two
  parameters” and “needs a threshold” belong to POT (GP). Swapped attributes are the standard distractor.
  • Threshold trade-off polarity: a lower u (or a higher Hill k) means more data, lower variance and higher bias;
  a higher u means fewer exceedances and higher variance.
  • Scope on GPBdH: the GP limit holds as the threshold gets large; it is not a licence to lower u to collect data,
  and “regardless of the parent” is a limiting result, not an exact one at any threshold.
  • Absolute claims: EVT is not “free of judgment” (threshold choice), and in operational risk it is not valid for
  pooled losses unless a single mechanism generated them.
  • Wrong distribution twin: GEV percentiles refer to the extreme-loss distribution, not the parent; and normal
  distributions justified by central limit theory are exactly what EVT says not to use for extremes.
