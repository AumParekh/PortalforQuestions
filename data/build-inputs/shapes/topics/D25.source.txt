  Revision notes
  • Traditional convertibles (ORR-22 g): issued by non-financial firms that wished to avoid the dilution of
  issuing equity before performance improved; they convert at the option of the holder when the share price
  exceeds thresholds in the indenture.
  • CoCos as the mirror image (ORR-22 g): they “cause a bank’s equity to increase when distress occurs, as
  reflected by triggers written into the indenture, and not at the option of the holder”. Good times do not trigger
  them; distress does.
  • Conversion versus write-down (S2): “With CoCos, equity increases either because the bond converts to
  equity or because its value is written down.” In a write-down no new shares are issued, but the liability
  shrinks, so equity still rises.
  • Triggers (S1): “a common trigger is when the ratio of Core Tier 1 Capital to RWA falls below a threshold”
  (a mechanical, accounting trigger), “or when a bank’s primary regulator declares it to be nonviable” (a
  discretionary trigger owned by the regulator, not the bank or the holders).
  • Capital treatment (S1): CoCos “may be included in Additional Tier 1 Capital if the threshold is 5.125
  percent or higher, and Tier 2 capital otherwise”. The AT1 bar sits above the 4.5% Core Tier 1 minimum, so
  the bond absorbs losses while the bank is still a going concern.
  • The cost puzzle (ORR-22 g): holders “receive little or none of the high returns received by equity holders
  when a bank does well, but holders bear losses not so different from those of equity holders when a bank
  fails”, so CoCos “should be expensive for a bank to issue”. Their advantage is accounting: they do not appear
  in the equity account until converted, so the bank can report a higher return on equity.
  • Basel III tiers (ORR-22 d): Tier 1 Equity (Core Tier 1) is common equity, retained earnings and limited
  minority interest, less goodwill, intangibles, deferred tax assets and any IRB reserve shortfall. Additional Tier
  1 includes unsecured, unguaranteed, non-cumulative perpetual preferred equity subordinated to depositors
  and subordinated debt, callable only after five years or more, plus “debt with appropriate triggers that cause
  conversion to equity or write-downs”. Tier 2 (subordinated debt with five years or more original maturity;
  general loan loss reserves capped at 1.25% of standardized RWA or 0.6% of IRB RWA) absorbs losses after
  failure. Minimums: Core Tier 1 4.5%, Tier 1 6%, total capital 8% of RWA. Tier 3 was eliminated.
  • Buffers (ORR-22 e): capital conservation buffer 2.5%; G-SIB add-on of 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 or 3.5%; countercycli-
  cal buffer 0 to 2.5% at national discretion. Breaching a buffer is less severe than breaching a minimum; the
  formal restrictions are on dividends and bonuses, plus a plan to restore ratios.
  • CoCos versus bail-in (ORR-22 h, 22.4): to recapitalize a failing G-SIB (FSB 2014: 12 key attributes;
  sufficient TLAC), convertible bonds can become equity, or certain wholesale debt can be bailed in (written
  down or converted). Conversion terms are “written into the indentures” and “often require conversion when
  a bank appears to be solvent”; bail-ins “are governed by national law and details are generally chosen by
  authorities after they have seized control of a bank”.
  • Living wills and super-equivalence (ORR-22 h): G-SIBs prepare resolution plans on funding, recapital-
  ization and continuing operations. Switzerland, with two huge G-SIBs it could not recapitalize in the crisis,

  • AT1 ranking and the inversion controversy (S3): in the usual creditor hierarchy AT1 ranks above common
  equity. In March 2023 the Swiss regulator ordered a write-down to zero of about CHF 16 billion of Credit
  Suisse AT1 bonds under their viability terms, while shareholders received UBS shares in the rescue takeover
  (outside-source). The in-syllabus anchor is Carey’s line that holders bear losses “not so different from those
  of equity holders”.
  • Neighbouring ratios: leverage ratio (Core Tier 1 to Leverage Exposure ≥ 3%), LCR and NSFR. See also
  A32; the CAR built from RWA is in D3.

  Exam traps
  • Role misassignment: conversion is triggered by the indenture (capital ratio) or by the primary regulator (non-
  viability), never at the holder’s option. The holder’s option belongs to the traditional non-financial convertible.
  • Polarity: traditional convertibles convert when the share price rises; CoCos convert or write down in distress.
  • Sibling swap, write-down versus conversion: both mechanisms increase equity; a write-down is not “equity-
  neutral”.
  • Scope error on the 5.125% line: a Core Tier 1 trigger alone does not make a CoCo AT1; the threshold must
  be 5.125% or higher, otherwise Tier 2.
  • Absolute claim on ranking: CoCo holders are not shielded until shareholders are wiped out; they bear equity-
  like losses, and the 2023 Credit Suisse write-down hit AT1 while shareholders kept some value (outside-source).
  • Cost direction: economically CoCos should be expensive; the issuer’s advantage is the higher reported ROE,
  not a cheaper cost of capital.
