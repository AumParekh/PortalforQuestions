  Revision notes
  • Mechanics (LTR-13 a): a repo is “a contract in which a security is traded at some initial price with the under-
  standing that the trade will be reversed at some future date at some fixed price.” Book example: counterparty
  A sells EUR 100 million face of the DBR 4s of 2037 to B at an invoice price of EUR 111.772 million and
  agrees to repurchase it 92 days later at the invoice price plus interest at a repo rate of 0.23%, actual/360:
  111,772,000(1 + 0.23% × 92/360) = EUR 111,837,697.10 (eq. 14.1).
  • Haircut and margin calls (LTR-13 a, c): because the lender of cash faces a borrower default at the same
  time as a fall in the collateral, it lends less than the collateral value (EUR 106 million against EUR 111.772
  million). Repos are also subject to margin calls: if the bund falls to EUR 110 million the borrower posts the
  EUR 1.772 million difference, so the investor still holds EUR 111.772 million of collateral against a EUR
  106 million loan. A dealer financing inventory must fund the haircut from its own capital.
  • Motivation 1, cash management (LTR-13 b): money market funds, municipalities (tax receipts arrive before
  spending), mutual funds, insurers, pension funds and some corporates lend cash in repo for a short-term rate
  “without sacrificing much liquidity or incurring significant default risk.” They prefer overnight or open
  repo (a one-day repo that renews daily until cancelled) and take general collateral: government, GSE and
  government-guaranteed MBS, any issue within the class. Many crisis-era lenders found they lacked the
  expertise or operations to take possession of and liquidate collateral.
  • Motivation 2, long financing (LTR-13 b): financial institutions are the typical cash borrowers. A trading
  desk that buys bonds from a client “repos out” (sells the repo) the bonds to finance them until a buyer is
  found. If no buyer appears it must roll the repo or unwind it and repo the bonds with a new counterparty C;
  because the new cash depends on the bond price at the roll, the roll leaves a cash surplus or deficit (offset
  by any hedge P&L). Customer financing (for example a hedge fund) is done back-to-back, with each leg’s
  haircut set by that counterparty’s creditworthiness and negotiating power.
  • Motivation 3, shorting through a reverse repo (LTR-13 b, e): a hedge fund that sells a bond short borrows
  it by doing a reverse repo (“reverses in” the security, “buys the repo”): it lends cash and takes the specific
  bond. It profits on the outright short if the bond’s return is below the repo rate. The same trade is a repo for
  the lender of the security, so always say whether cash or securities are being lent.
  • Counterparty risk (LTR-13 c): for the lender of cash, the risk “that the borrower defaults and the value of
  the collateral turns out to be insufficient to cover the loan amount.” With high-grade government collateral
  this is a right-way risk: a dealer default would likely trigger a flight to quality that raises the collateral’s value.
  • Liquidity risk and the funding spectrum (LTR-13 c): equity is the most stable funding, then long-term
  debt; short-term unsecured funding (commercial paper) is the least stable. Repo is cheap and sits on the
  less stable side, though more stable than unsecured short-term debt because collateral should stop lenders
  “bolting too quickly”. If repo investors lose confidence, the financing “can disappear as fast as the repos
  mature, which is mostly overnight”, forcing fire sales. “The loss of financing is often the killing blow”, and
  the same applies to all leveraged investors, “like part of the hedge fund world”. Borrowers want longer terms,
  lenders shorter; the system cannot do both.
  • Pre-crisis collateral drift (LTR-13 c, d): borrowers financed lower-quality corporate bonds and MBS at low
  repo rates and haircuts; in the crisis the worst-hit could not meet margin calls, post collateral against higher

  haircuts, or replace lost financing, and suffered collateral liquidations, capital depletion and failure.
  • Bear Stearns (LTR-13 d): in late 2006 to 2007 it cut short-term unsecured funding from USD 25.8 billion to
  USD 11.6 billion (commercial paper from USD 20.7 billion to USD 3.9 billion) and replaced it with secured
  repo, lengthening terms to six months or more for whole loans and non-agency MBS. In the week of 10
  March 2008 a loss of confidence brought three consequences: prime brokerage clients withdrew cash and
  unencumbered securities; repo lenders declined to roll even on agency collateral; FX counterparties refused
  to pay until Bear paid first.
  • Lehman and JPMorgan (LTR-13 d): JPM was Lehman’s tri-party clearing agent. Investors’ tri-party loans
  are overnight, but during the day the agent itself lends to the dealer; JPM’s intraday lending to Lehman
  typically exceeded USD 100 billion. JPM historically took no haircut on intraday advances, then phased
  haircuts in to match overnight investors by mid-August 2008, demanded USD 5 billion of cash collateral in
  September and seized USD 8.6 billion of cash in the last four business days. Lehman’s estate: JPM used its
  “life and death power” to become over-collateralised. JPM’s CRO Zubrow: JPM bore the dealer’s entire tri-
  party book each day with no assurance investors would return each evening, and much collateral was illiquid
  and overvalued.
  • General versus special collateral (LTR-13 e): in GC trades the cash lender accepts any security in a class;
  in specials the cash lender initiates the trade to obtain a particular security, so every special rate is typically
  below the GC rate. Special spread = GC rate − special rate. Funding trades are predominantly GC, but a
  holder of a bond trading special lends it in the specials market and borrows cash below GC. US GC trades
  close to but below fed funds (on average about 16 bp below between 23 October and 1 July 2010 in the book’s
  sample); the gap widens when Treasuries are scarce and in flights to quality.
  • Special spreads and the auction cycle (LTR-13 f): the on-the-run (OTR) issue trades most special, then the
  old, then the double-old; the OTR 10-year trades more special than the OTR 30-year. Spreads are volatile and
  often hundreds of basis points; they are small just after an auction (shorts can stay in the old OTR; reopenings
  add supply and get less special), then rise as shorts migrate and spike into the next auction as dealers short
  the OTR to hedge purchases of the new issue. Before May 2009 the special rate could not fall below zero
  (failing to deliver cost the same as borrowing at 0%), so the spread was capped by the GC rate; the TMPG
  fails penalty of max(3% − fed funds target, 0) made the penalty rate the new cap.
  • Financing advantage of a special bond (LTR-13 g): value of lending 100 of cash at the term special spread
  until the bond is expected to trade as GC. Book: 0.22% for 125 days gives 100 × 125 × 0.22%/360 = 0.076
  per 100 market value, about 7.7 cents per 100 face at a price of 101.90, against a total premium of over 2
  points; divided by DV01 0.085 it is only about 0.9 bp. Most of the OTR’s richness is liquidity, not financing.
  • See also D27 for Duffie’s dealer-bank runs (repo creditors, tri-party clearing banks) and A26 for how a repo
  moves the TSECF and TSCLGC.

  Exam traps
  • Dropped accrued (dropped term): the collateral is valued at the invoice (full) price; using the clean price
  understates both the cash lent and the repurchase price.
  • Haircut direction (role): the haircut protects the lender of cash; the borrower of cash funds it from its own
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D6  Repurchase Agreements
  capital. Forgetting it gives zero own capital and a repurchase price on the full invoice value.
  • GC versus special rate (wrong-input twin): a funding trade on a bond that is not trading special pays the GC
  rate; the special rate belongs to the bond in demand and is always below GC.
  • Who is exposed to the roll (role misassignment): non-renewal hurts the borrower of cash (refinance or fire-
  sell); the lender gets its cash back and holds over-collateralisation in the meantime.
  • “Collateral makes repo funding stable” (absolute claim): repo is more stable than unsecured short-term
  funding, but it “can disappear as fast as the repos mature”.
  • Special spread cap (scope): before the 2009 fails penalty the cap was the GC rate (special rate floored at 0%);
  after it, the cap is the penalty rate.
