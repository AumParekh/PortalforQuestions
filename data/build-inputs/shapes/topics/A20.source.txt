  Revision notes
  • The specific: the test (CR-20 k; S2). Wrong-way risk (WWR) is an unfavourable dependence between
  exposure (EPE) and counterparty credit quality: the exposure is high when the counterparty is more likely
  to default. Right-way risk (RWR) is the favourable dependence and reduces CVA. Standard CVA assumes
  EPE, PD and LGD are independent. To classify a trade, ask two questions: what makes my exposure to this
  counterparty rise, and does the same event weaken the counterparty?
  • Character of WWR: hard to identify, model and hedge because of subtle macro-economic and structural
  effects; often reasonable to ignore, but potentially dramatic. Losses: Asian crisis 1997/98 (sovereign and
  corporate defaults with collapsing local currencies); from 2007, banks buying protection from monoline
  insurers. Mortgage lending is a natural example: recession brings falling property prices and higher default
  rates together.
  • General vs specific WWR (Table 20.7): general WWR is macro-economic, may be detectable in historical
  data, can be incorporated into pricing models, and should be priced and managed. Specific WWR comes
  from structural or causal links, is hard to detect without knowledge of the market, the counterparty and the
  rationale of the trade, is dangerous to model with naive correlation (address it qualitatively, e.g. stress testing),
  and should in general be avoided. Example: the 2010 European sovereign crisis, which historical data did
  not bear out.
  • Put options (CR-20 l; S1): buying a put on a stock (or index) whose fortunes are highly correlated with
  the counterparty’s is WWR (a put on one bank’s stock bought from another bank). Equity calls should be
  right-way.
  • FX forwards and cross-currency swaps: paying a sovereign its local currency (or hedging that trade with
  a bank in the same region) is WWR: currency weakening and default go together, either way round. A cross-
  currency swap is like a loan collateralised by the opposite currency. Levy and Levin: residual currency
  values on sovereign default average 17% (AAA) to 62% (CCC).
  • Interest rate swaps: a bank receiving fixed from a corporate that pays fixed while the economy is strong
  has WWR (rates are cut in a recession, so the swap moves in the bank’s favour as the corporate weakens);
  paying fixed during a recovery may be right-way. Duffee (1998): corporate defaults cluster when rates are
  falling. Uncollateralised receiver swaps with weakening sovereigns or corporates show the “cross-gamma”
  of spreads and rates even without defaults.
  • Commodity swaps: a producer (e.g. a mining company) hedging its output owes money only when the
  commodity price is high and it is profitable: right-way, arising from hedging as opposed to speculation.

  • Credit default swaps (CR-20 l, 20.6.5): buying protection where the reference entity and the counterparty
  are strongly linked is extreme WWR (a bank selling protection on its own sovereign); selling protection in
  that case is right-way. For credit derivatives WWR is unavoidable and may be specific, and margin may help
  little. Book example (reference spread 250 bps, counterparty 500 bps, LGDs 60%): at 60% correlation the
  fair premium for buying protection is about 200 bps (CVA 50 bps, one-fifth of the premium); at 100% it is
  just above 100 bps = 250 × 40%, the recovery value alone.
  • Credit quality and WWR: WWR increases as the counterparty’s credit quality increases: the default of
  a strong name is a less probable, bigger shock. Levy and Levin implied FX jumps: 83% AAA vs 27%
  BBB sovereigns; euro jumps for Greece, Italy, Spain, Germany of 9%, 17%, 20%, 25%; Chung and Gregory
  (Japan): sovereign 38.4%, financials 13.5%, non-financials 8%.
  • Collateral and WWR (CR-20 m): timing decides. If exposure rises gradually before default, margin can be
  received; a jump in exposure at default makes margin useless. Jump approaches show collateral as near use-
  less against WWR; continuous approaches (intensity, structural) show it as effective; the truth is in between
  and depends on the trade and the counterparty. Pykhtin and Sokol: allow for jumps and higher volatility
  during the MPoR; WWR reduces the benefit of collateralisation and is greater for systemic parties (banks),
  which are the ones that post margin, while corporates often do not post at all.
  • Wrong-way collateral (CR-20 n; S3): (i) a payer swap collateralised by a high-quality government bond:
  rates up raise the swap value and lower the bond value (general WWR, minor because rate volatility is low;
  for a receiver swap it is right-way margin); (ii) a cross-currency swap collateralised by cash in the currency
  being paid: one FX move raises the exposure and cuts the margin value (more significant); (iii) an entity
  posting its own bonds: specific WWR, a very weak mitigant for exposure and CVA, though it may mitigate
  FVA; (iv) a bank posting bonds of its own sovereign. CR-17 adds euros received from European sovereigns
  or banks, and LTCM’s Russian bonds; the bilateral margin rules forbid “wrong-way” margin (own bonds or
  equity).
  • CCPs are particularly prone to WWR (CR-20 o; S4): they rely on variation and initial margin as their
  primary protection, especially when clearing CDSs. The danger is that the defaulter-pays resources are
  insufficient, losses are imposed on surviving members, and the CCP itself may become insolvent.
  • CCPs disassociate credit quality and exposure: membership needs a minimum credit quality (set by the
  CCP, not by external ratings), but initial margin and default fund contributions are driven mainly by the market
  risk of each portfolio, so CCPs risk implicitly ignoring WWR. Because WWR rises with credit quality, the
  logic perversely implies more initial margin and default fund from better-quality, more systemically important
  members (Pykhtin and Sokol: a large dealer is more WWR than a smaller or weaker one).
  • The CCP waterfall as a CDO: defaulter’s initial margin, default fund and CCP equity form the first loss; sur-
  viving members’ default fund contributions and other loss allocation are a rather senior second-loss tranche,
  heavily concentrated in systemic risk. CCPs also face WWR and adverse selection on margin received:
  members post the riskiest eligible assets relative to the haircut (a European bank posting European sovereign
  debt).
  • Quantifying WWR (CR-20 p; S5): replace EPE in eq. 20.3 with EPE conditional on default at that date. The
  regulatory alpha factor plus stressed data partially capitalise general WWR. With a single (abstract) correla-
  tion parameter, +50% roughly doubles EPE and −50% at least halves it. Pitfalls: uninformative historical
  data; misspecification (causality rather than correlation: zero correlation does not prove independence, e.g.
  Y = X 2 ); unclear direction (high-rate credit stress is possible).
  • Hazard rate (intensity) approach: a stochastic credit spread correlated with the exposure drivers; tractable
  (spread paths first, exposures only on default paths); correlations observable from historical spread data. It
  generates only weak dependence even near ±100% correlation, so it may underestimate WWR. For a payer
  swap, positive correlation of spreads with rates raises the positive exposure (WWR).
  • Structural approach: maps the exposure distribution and the default time separately onto a bivariate distri-
  bution (e.g. a Gaussian copula); no re-simulation, WWR added on top of pre-computed exposures; a much
  stronger effect than the intensity model. Drawbacks: the correlation is opaque and hard to calibrate, and the
  unconditional exposures may not hold all the relevant information.
  • Parametric approach (Hull and White 2011): links the default probability to the exposure through a sim-
  ple functional relationship, calibrated by a what-if scenario or by history (past portfolio values against the
  counterparty’s spread); it needs today’s portfolio to resemble the historical one.

  • Jump approach (Levy and Levin 1999): the FX rate jumps at the counterparty’s default; more realistic
  for specific WWR. Supported by quanto CDS data: euro CDS on Italy about 30% cheaper than dollar CDS
  at all maturities; Ehlers and Schönbucher show intensity models cannot match these data even at ±100%
  correlation.
  • See also A6 for the CVA formulas WWR breaks, A21 for margin and the MPoR, and D24 for the CCP loss
  waterfall.
  Exam traps
  • Polarity flip (credit quality): “WWR is greatest for weak, low-rated counterparties.” It increases with credit
  quality.
  • Role misassignment (CDS): buying protection on a reference entity linked to the counterparty is WWR; selling
  it is right-way. State the buyer and the seller before classifying.
  • Scope error (commodity hedge): the producer’s right-way risk arises from hedging; a speculative position
  does not inherit it.
  • Absolute claim (collateral): “margin eliminates WWR”. Jump approaches show it near useless; WWR reduces
  the benefit of collateral.
  • Sibling swap (models): intensity = weak dependence, may underestimate; structural = strong effect, opaque
  correlation; parametric = functional link (Hull and White); jump = specific WWR, quanto CDS evidence.
  • Polarity flip (wrong-way collateral): a government bond securing a payer swap is wrong-way (the bond falls
  as the swap gains); securing a receiver swap it is right-way.
