  Revision notes
  • What counts as retail (CR-7 a, Box 7.1): Basel defines retail exposures as homogeneous portfolios of a
  large number of small, low-value loans, with a consumer or business focus, where the incremental risk of
  any single exposure is small; small business loans qualify if total exposure is under EUR 1 million. Products:
  home mortgages (LTV is the key risk variable), home equity loans (HELOC), installment loans, unsecured
  credit card revolving loans, small business loans.
  • Retail versus corporate (CR-7 b, S3): retail exposures “arrive in bite-sized pieces, so that default by a single
  customer is never expensive enough to threaten a bank”. Portfolios behave like well-diversified portfolios in
  normal markets, so EL is predictable, treated as a cost of doing business and built into the price. Corporate
  portfolios contain large single-name exposures and concentrations, and their risk is dominated by credit losses
  rising to some unexpected level.
  • Diversification as a “fickle friend” (CR-7 b): in 2007 to 2009 house prices fell at about the same time across
  the US; a systematic change in lending behaviour (advancing money without checking incomes) can plant a
  hidden systematic risk in whole credit industries.
  • The dark side (CR-7 c, S2): “the danger that losses will suddenly rise to unexpected levels because of
  some unforeseen but systematic risk factor that influences the behavior of many of the credits”. Four prime
  causes: new products without enough loss history; well-understood products behaving unexpectedly when
  risk factors worsen together (the “perfect storm”, e.g. recession plus higher rates plus falling house prices);
  changing social and legal attitudes to default (personal bankruptcy in the 1990s); operational issues in the
  semiautomated credit process that hit the whole portfolio.
  • Measures against the dark side (CR-7 c): keep only a limited number of retail portfolios exposed to new
  kinds of risk (“a little exposure … a lot makes the bank a hostage to fortune”); stress test large conventional
  portfolios. Post-crisis, the CFPB (from Dodd-Frank) requires an “ability to repay” check with eight under-
  writing standards; a “qualified mortgage” has no excess upfront points and fees, no toxic features, a DTI cap
  (e.g. below 43%) and no balloon payments.
  • Retail early warnings and the supertanker (CR-7 c): rising defaults are often signalled by customer be-
  haviour (missed minimum card payments), letting the bank tighten lending to existing customers, change
  marketing and acceptance rules, or price in the risk. A commercial portfolio is a “supertanker”: by the time
  trouble is obvious it is often too late. Banks still ignore the signs to chase volume (Box 7.4: 2/28 and 3/27
  teaser-rate ARMs designed to be refinanced).
  • Regulatory capital for retail (CR-7 b): regulators accept that retail risk is relatively predictable and mort-
  gages safer, so retail banks set aside a relatively small amount of capital under Basel II and III, but must
  provide PD, LGD and EAD by segment, segmented by credit score and by vintage. Basel uses three formu-
  las for three subsectors: residential mortgages, revolving credit, other retail.
  • Other retail risks (CR-7 a, Box 7.5): interest rate risk (transferred to treasury for ALM); asset valuation risk
  (mortgage prepayment, auto lease residual values); operational risk (customer fraud); business risk (volumes,
  strategy, M&A); reputation risk with customers and regulators.

  • Scoring model types (CR-7 d): credit bureau (FICO) scores, low cost and quick, typically 300 to 850 with
  subprime below 660; pooled models built by vendors from several lenders’ data, tailored to an industry but
  not company-specific; custom models built in-house for a specific product and population. Higher score
  means lower risk; characteristics (questions) take attributes (answers); about 50 items per application, up to
  150; logistic regression is most common; population odds of 15:1 imply a 1 in 16 (6.25%) chance of a bad.
  Bureau files hold identifying information (not used in scoring), public records, collection information, trade
  lines and inquiries.
  • Mortgage variables, cutoffs and loss rates (CR-7 e): risk score = f (doc type, transaction type, FICO,
  LTV, DTI, occupancy type, property type, payment type, economic cycle); pre-crisis originators leaned too
  heavily on bureau scores. Moving the cutoff score up cuts off more bads but forgoes more goods. By score
  band, implied PD = historical loss rate / LGD (2% with 50% LGD gives 4%).
  • CAP and accuracy ratio (CR-7 f): the CAP plots cumulative actual defaults against the population sorted
  from highest to lowest risk score; the perfect model captures all defaults in the first (default-rate) slice, the
  random model is the 45-degree line. AR = AR /AP , closer to 1 is better. Monitor quarterly; performance
  deteriorates as population characteristics or behaviour change, and a product switch (auto loans to credit
  cards) justifies a new custom scorecard.
  • Customer relationship cycle (CR-7 g): marketing initiatives, screening applications, managing accounts,
  cross-selling. Scorecards now include application, behavior, revenue, response, attrition, insurance and tax
  authority scores. Trade-off: marginally less creditworthy customers may be more profitable (they borrow
  often and pay higher rates); the question is whether that survives a downturn.
  • Risk-based pricing (CR-7 h, S1): “explicitly incorporating risk-driven account economics into the annual-
  ized interest rate” at account level, using operating expenses, probability of take-up, PD, LGD, EAD, capital
  allocated and the cost of equity. “A ‘one price fits all’ policy in a competitive market leads to adverse selec-
  tion”: the bank attracts mainly high-risk customers and discourages low-risk ones, and the degree of adverse
  selection “may only become apparent when the economic climate deteriorates”. Tiered pricing: the higher
  the score, the lower the price. RBP is still in its infancy; better pricing can improve key metrics by 10 to 20%
  or more, trading market share against the bad rate (strategy curve).
  • Securitization and reforms (CR-7): about 50% of US mortgages were securitized before 2007; some struc-
  tures aimed at regulatory capital arbitrage rather than transferring economic risk. Reforms: better disclosure,
  originator retention (e.g. 5%), public rating methodologies, capital that better reflects securitization risk.
  • Scoring versus rating, PIT versus TTC (CR-6 a, b): see D7.

  Exam traps
  • Sibling swap, dark side versus concentration: the dark side is a systematic factor hitting many small credits
  at once; a large single-name loss is the corporate risk profile.
  • Polarity of adverse selection: one price for all attracts the high-risk customers and repels the low-risk ones,
  and the damage shows up in a downturn.
  • Polarity of tiered pricing: higher score band, lower price.
  • Absolute claim on capital: predictable EL does not mean zero capital; retail carries “a relatively small amount”
  of regulatory capital, and the dark side is precisely the unexpected loss.
  • CAP axis: the population is sorted from highest to lowest risk; the random model is the 45-degree line; AR
  closer to 1 is better.
