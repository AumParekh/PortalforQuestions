  Revision notes
  • Why daylight overdrafts exist (LTR-6 a): in the high-rate Volcker era banks ran balances down to reserve
  and correspondent minimums and went negative intraday. Central banks worried about (1) inadvertently
  lending to a member that fails during the day and (2) overdrafts acting as a “shadow” money supply (peak
  overdrafts near 10% of M1 then, 0.7% in 2013). The Fed’s 1985 policy introduced the net debit cap (self-
  assessed, board-approved); fees followed: 24 bp above a deductible of 10% of capital (1994), 36 bp (1995),
  and in 2008 50 bp for unsecured overdrafts, zero for fully collateralised ones, no deductible, and a 150 bp
  penalty for institutions without discount window access. Even with about USD 1.5 trillion at the Fed at
  day end, overdrafts arise from market conventions (fed funds delivered at once but returned first thing next
  morning; FMU settlement windows at day end) and from intraday credit to clients (payroll before an expected
  incoming wire; custody clients buying before a maturity settles).
  • Uses of intraday liquidity (LTR-6 a, S2): outgoing wire transfers (“typically the largest use”; payments
  are throttled near the debit cap); settlements at PCS systems (mostly once a day, late afternoon, a use or a
  source); funding of nostro accounts (managed to a target average monthly balance as compensation to the
  correspondent, a use or a source); collateral pledging (OTC trading, public deposits, margin calls); asset
  purchases and funding (securities, client loans, fixed assets).
  • Sources of intraday liquidity (LTR-6 a, S2): cash balances at the central bank and correspondents; incoming
  funds flow (“the largest source of intraday funding in periods of normal market function”, real-time LVPS or
  batch); intraday credit (the Fed’s net debit cap is an unsecured committed line that charges interest; the Bank
  of England requires collateral of the highest-quality government securities but charges no interest; interbank
  daylight lines are generally uncommitted and free; FMUs let positions build until settlement); liquid assets
  (low volatility, predictable); overnight borrowings (fed funds, Libor, Eurodollar, weighing excess liquidity
  cost against breaching a daylight limit); other term funding (FHLB, term repo; an incremental, not consistent,
  source). The treasurer’s three complications: variable cash flows, market forces (unexpected margin calls,
  central bank desks) and no single real-time data source.
  • Governance (LTR-6 b): leaders manage intraday risk actively rather than as a cost of doing business, putting
  settlement and systemic risk in the risk taxonomy and risk appetite. Three lines of defence: Treasury (first
  line) manages intraday and end-of-day funding positions; Corporate Risk Management (second line, where
  expertise is emphasised) oversees policy, monitors all funding desks, aggregates reporting and gives an in-
  dependent view; Internal Audit (third line) assesses adherence. Committees include treasury, operations, IT
  and lines of business; risk self-assessments cover settlement risk of new products. Monitoring has two per-
  spectives: intraday credit the bank extends to clients and intraday credit the bank uses; the second is harder
  (FMU and correspondent data gaps, aggregation).
  • Measures for understanding flows (LTR-6 c, S1): total payments (sent and received by client type and
  for the bank, net settlement position at any time, trends); other cash transactions and settlement positions
  at every FMU (the Fed tracks collateral at one-minute intervals); time-sensitive obligations (amounts and

  deadlines); total intraday credit lines extended to clients (mostly uncommitted and undisclosed); total bank
  intraday credit lines available and their usage (as expected in resolution planning).
  • Measures for monitoring risk levels (LTR-6 c, S1): daily maximum intraday liquidity usage (largest net
  negative balance relative to the committed or uncommitted line, peak and average tracked monthly, per ac-
  count and on a consolidated basis where liquidity moves freely); intraday credit relative to Tier 1 capital
  (total and unsecured, available and used; available unsecured credit to Tier 1 is “a rough measure of the
  inadvertent systemic risk”); client intraday credit usage (client peak overdraft relative to its line; flags clients
  to re-price or change); payment throughput (share of outgoing payments by time of day, FMU deadlines,
  efficiency of FMUs’ use of daylight credit).
  • Intraday stress testing (LTR-6 c): BCBS (April 2013) suggests at least four scenario categories: own finan-
  cial stress, counterparty stress, customer stress (a correspondent’s customer bank) and market-wide credit or
  liquidity stress; also reverse stress tests and events such as natural disasters. Benefits include the management
  discussion, playbooks and a data aggregation capability.
  • FMU lines of defence (LTR-6 c, S2): (1) membership criteria; tiering (indirect members routed through
  direct ones) concentrates risk: CHAPS had 18 direct members in 2010 against 866 at Target2 and over 7,000
  at Fedwire; (2) monitoring participants (financial statements, intraday margin cycles, cutting or removing
  the net debit cap, pre-funding, extra collateral, letters of credit); (3) risk mutualisation (prefunded guarantee
  funds; central bank underwriting, as the Fed does for Fedwire to give immediate finality). Tools: net debit
  caps, collateral, liquidity savings mechanisms (bilateral and multilateral netting, throughput requirements,
  prioritisation, intra-system lending), staggered settlement windows, contingent backup lines.
  • Dealer bank lines of business (LTR-8 a): securities dealing, underwriting and trading (plus proprietary
  trading); repo intermediation through clearing accounts at JPMorgan Chase and BNY Mellon (tri-party repo
  USD 2.5 trillion in 2007); OTC derivatives run as a matched book under ISDA master agreements (about
  USD 600 trillion notional; dealer exposures of USD 25,372 billion fall to USD 3,744 billion after netting; over
  80% of 2008 collateral was cash, with collateral demanded doubling to about USD 4 trillion; USD 23 trillion
  of USD 28 trillion CDS notional was dealer-to-dealer); prime brokerage to hedge funds (mostly Morgan
  Stanley, Goldman Sachs, Bear Stearns); asset management including internal hedge funds; off-balance-sheet
  SPEs and SIVs. Risks include market, credit and counterparty risk, reputational support of internal funds
  and SIVs (Bear USD 3.2 billion, HSBC USD 35 billion, Citigroup USD 49 billion), and diseconomies of
  scope in governance.
  • Run 1: short-term secured creditors (LTR-8 b, S3): dealers financed inventories with mostly overnight
  repo at average haircuts under 2%; Bear and Lehman had leverage above 30. Creditors “may have little or
  no incentive to renew” when solvency is doubted: they can reinvest with another dealer and avoid having
  to sell collateral (money market funds under Rule 2a-7) or litigation. A refusal en masse forces fire sales,
  which lower the value of unsold collateral and repo capacity: a “death spiral” that can spread to other banks.
  Investment-grade corporate bond haircuts rose from under 5% to about 20% after Lehman. Mitigants: credit
  lines, a buffer of cash and liquid securities, laddered liability maturities; lender-of-last-resort financing; in-
  sured deposits are less runnable, but Rule 23A bars using them to fund broker-dealer affiliates.
  • Run 2: prime brokerage clients (LTR-8 b, S3): in the UK client cash and securities are commingled with
  the broker’s assets (“equivalent to uninsured deposits”); US Rule 15c3-3 pools free credit balances. Example:
  cash of Hedge Fund A USD 150 million, margin loan to B USD 100 million, USD 50 million in reserve; if
  A leaves, the broker must find USD 100 million. Brokers re-hypothecate up to USD 140 of client assets per
  USD 100 of margin loan (equity advance rate 50%). Even without a run, clients can keep demanding margin
  loans the weakened broker cannot refinance. Re-pledgeable client collateral fell 69% at Morgan Stanley
  (USD 832 billion to USD 294 billion), 51% at Merrill Lynch, 30% at Goldman Sachs; Lehman’s London
  clients could not reclaim re-hypothecated assets.
  • Run 3: derivatives counterparties (LTR-8 b, S3): counterparties cut exposure by borrowing from the dealer,
  entering trades that make the dealer pay cash, harvesting in-the-money positions, or novating to another
  dealer (dealers began refusing Bear Stearns novations, spreading alarm). Collateral-on-downgrade triggers
  drain cash (Morgan Stanley: USD 498.3 million for one notch, a further USD 1,456.2 million for two; the
  proximate cause of AIG’s bailout). Counterparty exposures to Citibank fell from USD 126 billion to USD
  81 billion while those to JPMorgan grew. Most OTC derivatives are “qualifying financial contracts” exempt
  from the automatic stay, so close-out at replacement cost (for example 0.2% bid-offer on USD 30 trillion
  notional, about USD 60 billion) drains the estate with priority, encouraging others to run first.
  • Final step: loss of cash settlement privileges (LTR-8 b, S3): clearing banks extend daylight overdrafts
  against securities in the clearing account (intraday peak around 10 a.m., “several hundred billion dollars”).

  When liquidity is in doubt the clearing bank uses its right of offset to stop payments that would take the
  account negative; JPMorgan’s refusal to process Lehman’s wires left Lehman unable to settle, and it filed.
  Duffie’s composite “Alpha Bank” runs the whole sequence: failed equity raise, client bailouts, prime bro-
  kerage flight, counterparties’ exposure cuts and refused novations, repo non-renewal, then the clearing bank.
  • Policy measures (LTR-8 c): higher capital (higher for uncleared derivatives), supervisory councils, special
  resolution powers, securitisation retention, trade repositories. Lender-of-last-resort facilities and TARP-style
  capital injections limited fire sales but are costly and raise moral hazard. For repo: a tri-party repo utility with
  less discretion in rolling dealers’ repos (Bernanke), central bank insurance of tri-party repo, or an “emer-
  gency bank” funded by repo participants with discount window access. For derivatives: central clearing
  (multilateral netting; only standard contracts, so not AIG’s customised CDS; CCP strength and implicit gov-
  ernment backing are crucial). For recapitalisation: distress-contingent convertible debt and mandatory rights
  offerings triggered by leverage or liquidity thresholds.
  • Northern Rock (LTR-1 b, S4): a top-five UK mortgage lender funding much of its book with short-term debt
  instruments and securitisation (Granite). After August 2007 it could not replace maturing funding, although
  the FSA judged it solvent. It sought Bank of England support on 12 September; the BBC broke the news
  on 13 September and the first British bank run in 150 years followed (about GBP 2 billion withdrawn by 17
  September), ending only when the government guaranteed all deposits. Emergency borrowing at a penalty
  rate reached GBP 25 billion by February 2008; it was nationalised. Lessons: liquidity is not solvency;
  wholesale funding disappears in stress; news of central bank borrowing can itself trigger a run. See also
  A32.
  • Ashanti Goldfields and Metallgesellschaft (LTR-1 b, S4): both hedged illiquid long-term positions with
  margined contracts. Ashanti sold gold forward; when 15 European central banks limited gold sales (Septem-
  ber 1999) gold jumped over 25%, Ashanti could not meet margin calls and had to restructure. MG sold
  5- to 10-year fixed-price oil supply contracts and hedged with rolled short-dated futures; oil fell, margin
  calls mounted, management closed the hedges for a USD 1.33 billion loss. Lesson: not to avoid futures and
  forwards, but to ensure access to funding for the cash flow mismatch.
  • Case↔lesson matching (S4): Northern Rock: short-term wholesale funding and a retail run triggered by
  news of central bank support. Bear Stearns: repo lenders refused to roll even agency collateral, prime bro-
  kerage clients withdrew cash and unencumbered securities, dealers refused novations. Lehman: tri-party
  clearing bank demanded collateral and invoked its right of offset; London clients lost re-hypothecated assets.
  AIG: collateral-on-downgrade triggers. Ashanti and Metallgesellschaft: margin calls on hedges of illiquid
  assets. See also D6 (repo cases in Tuckman and Serrat).

  Likely distractors
  • Corrupted: Outgoing wire transfers are typically a bank’s largest source of intraday liquidity, while incoming
  payment flows are its largest use.
  True: outgoing wires are typically the largest use; incoming funds flow is the largest source in normal markets.

  (Polarity flip.)
  • Corrupted: The Federal Reserve requires intraday overdrafts to be collateralised and charges no interest, while
  the Bank of England offers an unsecured committed line and charges for its use.
  True: the Fed’s net debit cap is an unsecured committed line that charges interest; the Bank of England requires
  top-quality government collateral and charges no interest. (Sibling swap.)
  • Corrupted: Internal Audit, as the second line of defence, oversees funding policy and aggregates intraday
  liquidity reporting across the bank’s funding desks.
  True: that is Corporate Risk Management (second line); Treasury is the first line, Internal Audit the third,
  assessing adherence to intraday policies. (Role misassignment.)
  • Corrupted: Because they hold collateral after a haircut, repo creditors have a strong incentive to keep rolling
  overnight repo to a dealer whose solvency is in doubt.
  True: they “may have little or no incentive to renew”: they can reinvest with other dealers and avoid forced
  collateral sales or litigation. (Polarity flip.)
  • Corrupted: Because most OTC derivatives are subject to the automatic stay at bankruptcy, derivatives coun-
  terparties have little reason to cut their exposure to a weakening dealer.
  True: most are qualifying financial contracts exempt from the stay; close-out drains the estate with priority,
  which raises others’ incentive to run first. (Polarity flip.)
  • Corrupted: Northern Rock failed when its clearing bank exercised its right of offset and stopped processing its
  payment instructions.
  True: that is the Lehman (and Duffie’s Alpha Bank) endgame; Northern Rock’s lesson is reliance on short-term
  wholesale funding and a retail run triggered by news of Bank of England support. (Case cross-mapping.)
