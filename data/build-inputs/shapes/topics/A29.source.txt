  Revision notes
  The specific: governance and the chief AML/CFT officer (ORR-10 a)
  • Board and appointment. The board approves and oversees the policies for risk, risk management and
  compliance, including ML/FT, and must understand ML/FT risks. The board and senior management appoint
  an appropriately qualified chief AML/CFT officer with overall responsibility for the AML/CFT function and
  the stature and authority to be heard by the board, senior management and business lines.
  • Three lines in AML/CFT. Line 1: business units (front office, customer-facing activity) identify, assess
  and control the risks of their business; written policies and procedures, internal procedures for detecting and
  reporting suspicious transactions, staff screening and tailored ongoing training. Line 2: the chief AML/CFT
  officer, the compliance function, and also human resources or technology. Line 3: internal audit, reporting
  to the board audit committee. External auditors may also evaluate controls and compliance.
  • What the chief AML/CFT officer does. Ongoing monitoring of the fulfilment of all AML/CFT duties
  (sample testing, exception reports, alerting senior management or the board); contact point for all AM-
  L/CFT issues for internal and external authorities, including supervisors and FIUs; responsible for reporting
  suspicious transactions; assesses alerts from the IT monitoring system; ensures prompt disclosure where
  suspected proceeds remain in an account.
  • What the role may and may not combine. To avoid conflicts it should not have business line responsibilities
  and should not be entrusted with data protection or internal audit. It may also perform the function of the
  CRO or the chief compliance officer, with a direct reporting line to senior management or the board; where
  duties are separated, the relationship between these officers must be clearly defined. So the trap is about
  roles, not titles: the STR and FIU duties attach to the chief AML/CFT officer role, whoever holds it.
  • Risk assessment and monitoring. Identify inherent and residual risk at country, sectoral, bank and business
  relationship level; understand risks in customers, products (including those under development), delivery
  channels, services and jurisdictions; measures proportional and risk-based and demonstrable to the super-
  visor. Transaction monitoring adequate to size and complexity, usually automated for internationally active
  banks (otherwise document an effective alternative), covering all accounts, with parameters reflecting the
  bank’s own risk rather than vendor defaults. Internal audit evaluates the IT system and audits policies, staff
  effectiveness, compliance oversight (including alert parameters) and training.
  Customer acceptance and due diligence (ORR-10 b)
  • Customer acceptance policy. Identify higher-risk customer types by background, occupation (public or
  high-profile position), source of income and wealth, country of origin and residence, products, purpose of
  accounts, linked accounts, business activities. Basic due diligence for all; simplified measures for proven
  lower risk if law allows; enhanced due diligence for higher risk (large balances with regular cross-border
  wires, PEPs; required for foreign PEPs), with senior management approval to enter or continue the relation-
  ship. The policy must not deny access to the financially or socially disadvantaged (FATF Financial Inclusion

  Guidance).
  • Identification and verification. CDD covers customers, persons acting on their behalf and beneficial own-
  ers (natural persons who ultimately own or control a customer). Verify with reliable, independent source
  documents (best: hardest to obtain illicitly or counterfeit); a written declaration of beneficial ownership may
  be requested but not relied on solely; non-face-to-face is no excuse to skip verification. Generally no re-
  lationship or transaction until identity is verified; completion after onboarding only where essential not to
  interrupt business, with restrictions; if problems cannot be resolved, close or block and consider an STR.
  • Specific cases. No anonymous or obviously fictitious-name accounts; numbered accounts get exactly the
  same CDD, with identity known to sufficient staff. Funds transferred from an account in the customer’s name
  at another bank subject to the same CDD standard give some comfort, but the bank must still conduct its own
  due diligence; refusal by another bank over illicit activity points to higher risk, EDD, an STR or rejection.
  • Ongoing monitoring and records. All relationships, intensity based on risk, enhanced for higher risk, plus
  cross-sectional product monitoring; flag transactions without economic sense, large cash deposits, deviations
  from the customer risk profile. Screen the database whenever sanction lists change, and periodically for
  foreign PEPs. Keep records at least five years after the relationship or occasional transaction ends, and until
  case closure if under investigation or litigation.
  • STRs, FT and freezing. Report suspicious transactions promptly to the FIU; the customer must not be
  informed, even indirectly (no tipping off). FT differs from ML: funds may come from legal sources and
  transactions may be very small. Screen against terrorist lists before onboarding and on an ongoing basis;
  terrorist screening is not risk-sensitive and applies whatever the customer’s risk profile; freeze without delay
  and without prior notice.
  Group-wide, cross-border and correspondent banking (ORR-10 c)
  • Group-wide policies. Consistently applied and supervised across the group; local policies consistent with
  group policy; if host requirements are stricter, the branch or subsidiary adopts them; where home and host
  minimums differ, apply the higher standard of the two. If the host country prevents proper implementation
  of FATF standards, the chief AML/CFT officer informs the home supervisors; closing host operations may
  be considered.
  • Information sharing and the group officer. Branches and subsidiaries must be authorised to share customer
  information with head office and proactively send information on higher-risk customers; head office can
  require group-wide searches against lists; significant relationships monitored on a consolidated basis (on- or
  off-balance sheet, AUM, fiduciary). A bank operating nationally and abroad appoints a group AML/CFT
  officer who coordinates a single group strategy, can give orders to all branches and subsidiaries, and checks
  compliance including by regular on-site visits.
  • Introducers and correspondent banking. Never rely on introducers subject to less strict standards; higher
  reliance on a same-group introducer subject to the same standards is possible, but customer information must
  still be obtained. The guidelines increased focus on third-party introducers (Annex 1) and correspondent
  banking (Annex 2); the June 2017 revision applies the risk-based approach to correspondent relationships,
  recognising that not all correspondent banking relationships bear the same level of risk, with a list of risk
  indicators. Mixed financial groups share customer information across banking, securities and insurance and
  apply sector-appropriate requirements when cross-selling.
  Financial crime controls and USAA (ORR-11 a, b)
  • Scope. FCA definition of financial crime: fraud or dishonesty; market misconduct or misuse of informa-
  tion; handling the proceeds of crime; financing of terrorism. ML and TF map to the Basel CPBP category.
  Internal fraud has two Level 2 types: unauthorised activities (now often called misconduct) and theft and
  fraud; external fraud: theft and fraud, systems security. “Zero tolerance” for internal fraud means every
  caught perpetrator is sanctioned, not that the risk cannot materialise. Fraud triangle: opportunity, incentive,
  rationalisation.
  • Control framework. Four components for both internal fraud and AML: selection (screening, due diligence,
  tone and culture; for AML: CDD/KYC, identity and origin of funds), prevention (segregation of duties,
  access rights, delegation of authority; for AML: risk-based approach, training, allocation of responsibilities),
  detection (supervision, monitoring, reconciliations; for AML: transaction monitoring, alerts, reconciliation
  with OFAC and PEP lists), deterrents (sanctions, legal pursuits; for AML: escalation to the FIU, closure
  of accounts). A dedicated MLRO is mandatory in most jurisdictions. RegTech automates profiling and
  screening.

  • Three stages of laundering. Placement: techniques that disguise the origins of funds (cash transfer to
  a business, false invoicing, trusts and offshore companies, smurfing: many small transactions below the
  reporting threshold instead of one large one, foreign accounts). Layering: combining placement and ex-
  traction strategies with varying amounts and patterns to make tracking as hard as possible. Integration or
  extraction: getting the money out to use (fake payments to employees, fake loans, dividends to accomplices).
  • USAA (2022). USD 140 million from FinCEN and OCC consent orders for a “willful” failure to maintain
  a BSA/AML programme (January 2016 to April 2021): weak internal controls, suspicious activity identifi-
  cation and reporting, understaffing filled by untrained contractors, a new monitoring system creating about
  90,000 unreviewed alerts and 6,900 unreviewed cases. Fined for a weak control environment, not for proven
  laundering. Lesson: “ignore regulatory findings at one’s own peril”. See also A22 for the general three lines
  model.
  Exam traps
  • Role misassignment (the map’s trap): STRs and the FIU and supervisor contact point belong to the chief
  AML/CFT officer, not to the CRO as such, not to internal audit and not to the front office. The CRO may hold
  the chief AML/CFT officer role; then the duties come with it.
  • Forbidden combinations: the chief AML/CFT officer must not have business line, data protection or internal
  audit responsibilities; combining with the CRO or CCO is allowed.
  • Scope error on reliance: funds from another bank’s account, or a same-group introducer, never remove the
  bank’s own due diligence; never rely on introducers with less strict standards.
  • Absolute claims: not all correspondent banking relationships bear the same risk; terrorist screening is the ex-
  ception that is not risk-sensitive; verification after onboarding is allowed only where essential, with restrictions.
  • Polarity flip, cross-border: apply the higher of home and host standards; stricter host rules are adopted locally.
  • Sibling swap on stages: smurfing is placement; layering mixes amounts and patterns; integration extracts the
  funds for use.
