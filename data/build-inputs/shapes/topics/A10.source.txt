  Revision notes
  • Agency ratings aim for stability (CR-9 a): agencies want to avoid ratings reversals, so ratings change only
  when there is reason to believe in a long-term change in creditworthiness. They “rate through the cycle”: a
  downturn that raises the six-month PD but barely moves the three-to-five-year PD would not usually change
  the rating. Bond traders, often bound by rating rules, are major users. See also D7 for point-in-time versus
  through-the-cycle.
  • Market-based PDs (CR-9 a): Moody’s KMV and Kamakura estimates are built from equity prices and other
  variables, do not have stability as an objective, and respond more quickly to market information than ratings
  (see A5).
  • Internal ratings (CR-9 a): needed because agency ratings usually exist only for companies with publicly
  traded debt; many small and medium-sized firms are unrated. Inputs are profitability ratios (return on assets)
  and balance sheet ratios (current ratio, debt to equity). Banks convert the financials to a cash flow statement
  because it is cash rather than profit that repays a loan.
  • Altman (1968) (CR-9 b): pioneered the use of accounting ratios to predict default, using “a statistical tech-
  nique known as discriminant analysis” on five ratios. Doumpos dates the roots of data-driven empirical
  models to these late-1960s statistical bankruptcy models (CR-5 d).
  • Linear discriminant function (S4): Z is a fixed-weight linear combination of the five ratios, and cut-off
  values of Z assign a firm to a group (fail or not fail). The weights were estimated so that the score separates
  the failed from the non-failed firms in the estimation sample (the statistical mechanics of discriminant analysis
  are outside-source; Hull gives only the technique’s name and the fitted equation).
  • Estimation sample (CR-9 b): 66 publicly traded manufacturing companies, 33 that failed within one year
  and 33 that did not. The model “proved to be fairly accurate when tested out of sample”.
  • Type I and Type II errors (CR-9 b): Type I = predicted not to go bankrupt but did; Type II = predicted to
  go bankrupt but did not. Both were small. Type I errors are liable to be the more costly for a commercial
  bank’s lending department.
  • Scope (CR-9 b): the 1.2 / 1.4 / 3.3 / 0.6 / 0.999 equation is for publicly traded manufacturing companies.
  Variations exist for manufacturers that are not publicly traded and for non-manufacturers, and the methodol-
  ogy has been revised and extended since 1968.

  • Direction and inputs: a higher Z means a lower likelihood of default. Every coefficient is positive, so
  negative working capital or negative retained earnings pulls Z down. X4 is the only ratio not over total
  assets: market value of equity over book value of total liabilities. X3 uses EBIT, not net income.
  • Cut-off zones (CR-9 b, S3): above 3.0 unlikely to default; 2.7 to 3.0 “on alert”; 1.8 to 2.7 a good chance of
  default; below 1.8 the probability of “a financial embarrassment” very high. Example 9.1: Z = 5.46, not in
  danger of defaulting in the near future.
  • Rating and PD (CR-9 c): Table 9.1 shows cumulative PDs rising sharply as ratings fall. For investment
  grade, the PD in a year tends to increase with time in the first few years (A: 0.05%, 0.08%, 0.09%, 0.11%);
  for the lowest grade it can decrease (CCC/C: 28.3%, 10.03%, 5.09%, 2.94%) because the next year or two
  are critical. Migration matrices and hazard rates: see A15.
  • Empirical models, strengths and limits (CR-5 d): they add transparency and consistency, can be validated
  against actual data, support scenario analysis and stress testing, and can be customised (large firms, SMEs,
  sectors). Their main weakness is reliance on historical data that are backward looking and static (financial
  statements reflect only the current status) and not updated in real time.
  • The other LDA (S5): in operational risk, LDA is the loss distribution approach: a frequency distribution
  and a severity distribution are combined (convolution) into an annual loss distribution for capital. It shares
  only the acronym with discriminant analysis. See D19.
  Formulas
  Altman’s original Z-score (Hull eq. 9.1), publicly traded manufacturing companies:
  Z = 1.2X1 + 1.4X2 + 3.3X3 + 0.6X4 + 0.999X5
  • X1 = Working capital / Total assets (working capital = current assets − current liabilities, can be negative)
  • X2 = Retained earnings / Total assets
  • X3 = Earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) / Total assets
  • X4 = Market value of equity / Book value of total liabilities
  • X5 = Sales / Total assets
  Zones (as stated in the book):
  Z-score  Interpretation
  Z > 3.0  Unlikely to default
  2.7 to 3.0  “On alert”
  1.8 to 2.7  Good chance of default
  Z < 1.8  Probability of a financial embarrassment very high
  Reverse-solve for one input. Required ratio = (target Z − contribution of the other four)/its coefficient; then mul-
  tiply by that ratio’s denominator.
  Book check (Example 9.1). WC 170,000; TA 670,000; EBIT 60,000; Sales 2,200,000; MVE 380,000; TL 240,000;
  RE 300,000 give X1 = 0.254, X2 = 0.448, X3 = 0.0896, X4 = 1.583, X5 = 3.284 and Z = 5.46.
  Exam traps
  • Wrong input twin in X4 : book value of equity instead of market value, or total assets instead of total liabilities
  as the denominator.
  • Wrong input twin in X3 : net income instead of EBIT.
  • Coefficient swap: 1.4 belongs to retained earnings, 3.3 to EBIT; 0.6 to the equity ratio, 0.999 to sales. A swap
  moves Z by enough to change the zone.
  • Working capital twin: current assets instead of current assets minus current liabilities; dropping the minus
  sign when working capital is negative.
  • Zone boundaries (polarity): high Z is safe. The boundaries are 3.0, 2.7 and 1.8; 1.8 to 2.7 is “good chance of
  default”, 2.7 to 3.0 is “on alert”.
