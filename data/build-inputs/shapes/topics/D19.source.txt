  Revision notes
  The specific: frequency × severity, then the 99.9th percentile (S1, S2)
  • Key principle. The LDA is an old actuarial technique that decomposes loss data into frequency (how often
  events occur) and severity (how much they cost). Decomposition doubles the observational data points,
  valuable because early operational risk data were scarce. Frachot, Georges and Roncalli (Crédit Lyonnais,
  2001) applied it to operational risk. Statistical modelling grew more in continental Europe; scenario-based
  modelling and stress testing more in the United States.
  • Convolution (S1). Frequency and severity distributions are estimated independently, then convoluted into
  an aggregated loss distribution. The most common method is Monte Carlo (a million or more random
  draws of frequency and severity); Fast Fourier transform and Panjer recursion are equation-based alternatives
  needing more coding and mathematics but less computer time.
  • Capital (S2). Economic capital is determined by extrapolating the 99.9th percentile of the estimated loss
  distribution at a one-year horizon; the 99.9th percentile of the aggregated distribution is the stand-alone
  operational risk capital when only the LDA is used. Under the AMA, banks held capital for all operational
  losses up to 99.9% at one year (“VaR at 99.9% = Regulatory Capital”).
  • Frequency: Poisson. Discrete count of events per period (typically a year). One parameter λ, equal to both
  mean and variance (the intensity rate). Observations i.i.d. Additive: Pois(λX ) + Pois(λY ) = Pois(λX +λY ).
  In a 2009 BIS study, nine of ten AMA firms used Poisson; the rest used the negative binomial.
  • Severity: lognormal and heavier. Continuous, asymmetric and heavy-tailed: many small losses and a few
  very large ones. Lognormal: ln X is normal with location µ and scale σ; positive values only; mode eµ−σ .
  Heavier tails increasingly used: Weibull and the generalized Pareto distribution (GPD).
  Limitations and units of measure (ORR-4 f)
  • Two strong assumptions. (1) Frequency and severity are independent, a strong condition given the contrast
  between the frequency of small and large events. (2) Within each risk class (unit of measure, UoM) losses
  are i.i.d., with no implicit correlation and one data-generating mechanism; tested by clustering homogeneous
  losses and checking for no autocorrelation.
  • UoM trade-off. Good UoMs are homogeneous sets driven by the same factors (external fraud by business
  line; damage to physical assets for all lines; internal fraud by business entity; processing errors by business
  line). Finer segmentation: more homogeneous data, but less data, more uncertainty and harder aggrega-
  tion. UoMs are aggregated with explicit correlations or copulas (which can capture tail dependence); the
  dependency structure strongly drives capital and attracts validators and regulators.
  • The tail. LDA alone does not capture the extreme skewness of operational losses: the tail is completed with
  extreme value theory (block maxima or peaks over threshold), scenario data, or both. EVT is valid only if
  one mechanism generates all losses, which is unlikely for operational data (see A17).

  Data inputs and the regulatory context (ORR-4 a, f)
  • Internal and external data. Internal loss data are the backbone of LDA and best for the body (frequent,
  moderate losses); rules are needed for near misses, accidental gains and rapidly recovered losses (conserva-
  tive firms include them in frequency and severity). External data supplement the tail: public databases (full
  context, scarcer) and consortia such as ORX (anonymous, abundant). Mixing requires scaling (size, infla-
  tion), a cut-off mix (severity level where external data enter) and filtering rules that avoid cherry-picking.
  • AMA and its four inputs. Internal loss data (ILD), external loss data (ELD), scenario data (SD), business
  environment and internal control factors (BEICF): the first two are past observations, the last two prospective.
  AMA approval took about three years and around 30 criteria. A Tier-1 European bank calibrated body and
  tail with two distributions and traced every loss and scenario to the capital number.
  • After AMA. With the new Standardized Approach (effective 1 January 2023) there is no formal requirement
  to model operational risk capital; modelling effort moves to Pillar 2 (ICAAP), stress testing and operational
  resilience. Smaller firms often hold capital at least equal to the largest scenario and to clusters of two to four
  scenarios that could occur together; Tier-1 firms aggregate scenarios with variance-covariance matrices or
  copulas. Economic capital and ICAAP largely share a purpose.
  • Why the tail dominates (ORR-6 c). ORX 2014 to 2019: losses above EUR 10 million were 0.4% of
  occurrences but 66.6% of severity; losses of EUR 20,000 to 100,000 were 57.9% of occurrences but 4.4%
  of severity. “No averages in operational risk.”
  • The other LDA. Linear discriminant analysis (Altman’s Z-score) classifies borrowers into groups from
  weighted ratios; it is a credit scoring tool, unrelated to capital. See also A10.

  Exam traps
  • Acronym swap: “LDA” for scoring is linear discriminant analysis (Z-score, A10); “LDA” for capital is the loss
  distribution approach.
  • Dropped term: convolving only zero- and one-event years cuts the tail off at the largest single loss; years with
  two or more events must be counted when the frequency is high enough to reach the 99.9% tail.
  • Sibling swap with credit capital: the reading equates operational risk capital with the 99.9th percentile of the
  aggregated loss distribution; do not subtract expected loss as in the IRB unexpected-loss formula, and do not
  use 99%.
  • Aggregation error: adding stand-alone UoM percentiles assumes perfect dependence; independent Poisson
  UoMs with the same severity merge exactly into one Poisson UoM, and dependence in general is modelled with
  copulas.
  • Scope/condition: Poisson has one parameter (mean = variance); frequency and severity independence and i.i.d.
  within a UoM are assumptions, not facts; the 99.9% one-year standard was the AMA rule, and under the 2023
  SA modelling is no longer required for Pillar 1.
