  Revision notes
  • Backtesting (MR-4 a): “a formal statistical framework that consists of verifying that actual losses are in line
  with projected losses”. An exception (exceedance) is a day when the loss exceeds the previous day’s VaR.
  Too many exceptions: the model underestimates risk (too little capital, regulatory penalties); too few: excess,
  inefficient capital allocation.
  • Which return? (MR-4 b): VaR assumes a frozen portfolio. The actual return is contaminated by intraday
  trades, fees, commissions, spreads and net interest income; the hypothetical return applies fixed positions
  to close-to-close returns; the cleaned return is actual minus non-mark-to-market items. Use both actual
  and hypothetical: passing with hypothetical but failing with actual points to intraday trading; failing with
  hypothetical means the methodology must be reexamined.
  p(MR-4 c): under the null, exceptions x are binomial with mean pT and variance p(1 − p)T ;
  • Failure rate test
  z = (x − pT )/ p(1 − p)T , reject if |z| > 1.96 (JPM 1998: 20 exceptions at 95% over 252 days, z = 2.14,
  reject). The test is fully nonparametric. Kupiec’s LRuc is χ2 (1): reject if LRuc > 3.841 (JPM: 3.91).
  Nonrejection region at 99%, T = 252: N < 7; at 95%, T = 252: 6 < N < 20.
  • The test confidence level is not the VaR confidence level (MR-4 c): Basel backtests a 99% VaR with a
  decision rule at, for example, 95% test confidence.
  • Type 1 and Type 2 errors (MR-4 d; S4): Type 1 is rejecting a correct model (bad luck penalised); Type 2 is
  not rejecting an incorrect model. At p = 0.01, T = 250: more than four exceptions occur 10.8% of the time
  under a correct model (Type 1); a model with true 97% coverage passes 12.8% of the time (Type 2). Power
  = 1− Type 2 rate.
  • Raising power (MR-4 d): exception tests have low power at high VaR confidence and short samples. Low-
  ering the VaR confidence level to 95% (cutoff 17 exceptions, Type 1 12.5%) cuts Type 2 to 7.4%; raising T
  to 1,000 (cutoff 14) gives Type 1 13.4% and Type 2 0.03%. Detection of systematic bias is hardest for small
  p (rare events).
  • Conditional coverage (MR-4 e; S3): exceptions should be evenly spread; bunching should invalidate the
  model. Christoffersen’s LRind compares π0 (exception after a non-exception day) with π1 (exception after
  an exception day); reject independence if LRind > 3.841. LRcc = LRuc + LRind ∼ χ2 (2): reject if
  LRcc > 5.991. JPM: π0 = 14/232 = 6.0%, π1 = 6/20 = 30.0%, LRind = 9.53: exceptions cluster.
  • Basel rules (MR-4 f; S5): daily exceptions of the 99% one-day VaR over the last 250 days; 2.5 expected.
  Green 0 to 4 (k = 3); yellow 5 to 9 with plus factors 0.40, 0.50, 0.65, 0.75, 0.85; red 10 or more (plus 1.00,
  automatic). In the yellow zone the penalty depends on the cause: basic integrity of the model and model
  accuracy could be improved: penalty “should” apply; intraday trading: “should be considered”; bad luck:

  expected some of the time, and exceptions from sudden abnormal changes in rates, major political events or
  natural disasters may be excluded.
  • The capital formula (ORR-21 c): MR = max(VaRt−1 , m×VaRavg ), with 10-day 99% VaR and the 60-day
  average; m ≥ 3. Carey’s summary (“five or less exceptions enabled the multiplier m to be 3”, 10 or more
  required 4) is looser than Jorion’s Table 4.3; for MR-4 f questions use Jorion’s table.
  • Two properties of an accurate VaR model (MR-7 a; S2): unconditional coverage (Kupiec) and indepen-
  dence (Christoffersen). In a PIT backtest these become uniformity of the PITs on U (0, 1) and independence:
  a perfectly accurate model gives i.i.d. U (0, 1) PITs (Rosenblatt).
  • Deriving PITs (MR-7 b): pt+1 = Ft (P Lt+1 ), the model’s (previous day’s forecast) probability of observing
  a loss below the actual P&L. Exceedances test one percentile; PITs carry information “at any percentile of
  the forecast distribution”. Hull (MR-18 c) calls the same quantity the p-value, uniformly distributed if the
  model works perfectly.
  • Shape of the PIT histogram (MR-7 c): conservative misspecification (model distribution too wide) puts
  P&L in the middle, a hump-shaped PIT histogram; aggressive misspecification (too narrow) puts realised
  P&L in the tails, spikes at 0 and 1. The sample showed models conservative in the body but with tails thinner
  than realised P&L at subportfolio level.
  • Goodness-of-fit tests (MR-7 d): Kolmogorov-Smirnov measures the maximum distance between empirical
  and uniform CDFs but lacks sensitivity in the tails; Anderson-Darling puts more weight on the tails (more
  power for a 99% VaR); Cramer-von Mises uses the mean squared deviation. Independence of PITs: transform
  to normals and regress on lags.
  • Results and limits (MR-7 a, d): 67% of subportfolios passed Kupiec, 92% Christoffersen independence, 71%
  conditional coverage, but only 21% passed KS and 11% A-D. Christoffersen ignores dependence beyond one
  lag; Ljung-Box tests several lags; the Christoffersen and Pelletier duration test (exponential versus Weibull)
  adds power. Banks do better on exceedance tests than on distribution tests.
  • FRTB backtesting (MR-18 c): stressed ES is not backtested; each desk backtests a one-day VaR at 99%
  and 97.5% over 12 months; more than 12 exceptions at 99% or more than 30 at 97.5% sends the desk to the
  standardized approach. See also B2 for ES.

  Exam traps
  • Type 1 versus Type 2 (polarity flip): rejecting a correct model is Type 1 (the 10.8% for five or more exceptions);
  accepting a flawed one is Type 2 (the 12.8%).
  • Kupiec versus Christoffersen (sibling swap): Kupiec cannot see clustering if the count is right; Christof-
  fersen’s independence test sees only one lag, not breaches a week apart.
  • Excludable exceptions (scope error): only exceptions from sudden abnormal market moves, major political
  events or natural disasters may be excluded; ordinary bad luck (“markets were particularly volatile or correlations
  changed”) still counts.
  • Plus-factor row off-by-one: 6 exceptions is +0.50, 7 is +0.65, 8 is +0.75; 5 is already yellow (+0.40) in
  Jorion’s table.

  • PIT histogram polarity: PIT is the probability integral transform, not a point-in-time measure. A hump in the
  middle means a conservative (too wide) model; spikes at the ends mean an aggressive (too narrow) one.
  • KS versus A-D (sibling swap): Anderson-Darling, not Kolmogorov-Smirnov, weights the tails.
