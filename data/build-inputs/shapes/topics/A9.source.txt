  Revision notes
  P
  • The specific: the interim waterfall (CR-22 e). Loan interest from survivors Lt = (r +s) (N − τ ≤t dτ )×
  par; loans that default during the year pay no interest that year. Excess spread is Lt − B (after any fees, which
  take priority over coupons). The amount diverted to the overcollateralisation account is max[min(Lt −
  B, K), 0]; the equity receives max(Lt − B − OCt , 0). In Malz’s base case (2% default rate) the full USD
  1,750,000 is diverted every year.
  P
  • Overcollateralization account and the custodian’s tests (CR-22 f, g). Value at the end of year t: τ ≤t (1+
  r)t−τ (OCτ + Rτ ), i.e. last year’s balance grown at the money market rate, plus this year’s diversion, plus
  this year’s recoveries (40% of defaulted par in the example). Two-step test each year: is Lt − B > 0? If yes,
  is Lt − B ≥ K? (cap K goes to the account, any surplus to equity; otherwise all of it goes to the account
  and the equity gets nothing). If negative, can the account plus current recoveries cover B − Lt ? If yes, the
  shortfall is paid out of it; if not, the account is depleted. Funds are released to the equity only at maturity.
  Malz calls the name “a bit of a misnomer”: it is soft, not hard, credit enhancement.
  • Why recoveries are escrowed: if recoveries flowed through the waterfall, the equity would perversely benefit
  from defaults; in real deals recoveries go to the senior bonds, then the mezzanine.
  • The final year (CR-22 e). Four sources: survivors’ interest, redemption at par of survivors, final-year re-
  coveries, and the account balance grown one more year. No more diversion or OC test. Sequential short-
  fall tests: senior first (F vs senior par plus coupon), then mezzanine on F − senior due; the equity gets
  max(F − total bond due, 0). Malz’s equity IRR is 23% at a 2% default rate and −92.1% and −95.5% at
  7.5% and 10%.
  • Timing of defaults: collateral cash flows are larger early in a deal’s life; accelerating defaults hurt the bond
  tranches; other things equal the equity benefits if defaults come later.
  • Product types (CR-22 a). Covered bonds (cover pool stays on the bank’s balance sheet, so not a full se-
  curitisation); mortgage pass-throughs (true securitisations, prepayment risk); CMOs (sequential-pay time
  tranching); structured credit (sequential distribution of credit losses). CDOs repackage ABS, MBS or CLOs;
  CDO-squareds go one level further. Pools are static (auto, residential mortgages), revolving (credit cards) or
  managed (CLOs). Synthetic deals hold CDS against a reference portfolio.
  • Tranching (CR-22 b). Equity: no fixed coupon, residual claim, thin, first loss. Mezzanine: high coupon,
  thin, next in line. Senior: low coupon, bulk of liabilities; if the pool cannot be financed mainly by low-yield
  senior debt the deal is not viable. Attachment point of the senior tranche = detachment point of the junior
  one; subordination (credit enhancement) is the share of the pool that must be lost before a tranche loses. A
  senior bond with 20% enhancement facing 21% pool losses loses 1/80 = 1.25%. Default in securitisations
  is “material impairment”.

  • Waterfall (CR-22 c; CR-23 b). Cash is paid top down: fees, then Class A interest, A coverage tests, Class
  B interest, B coverage tests, and so on, with the equity taking the residual (Choudhry Figure 23.4). An
  independent third-party agent runs the tests; if a test fails, the vehicle pays down the notes starting with the
  senior notes.
  • OC, first-loss and equity piece (CR-23 b). Over-collateralisation: the principal value of notes issued is
  lower than the principal value of assets (hard enhancement, present at issue). The first-loss piece is the most
  junior note, lowest rated or non-rated, called the equity piece “even though it is a bond”, and usually held by
  the originator. Excess spread is soft enhancement that accrues over time.
  • Trust vs SPV and the three structures (CR-23 a, c). An SPV is bankruptcy remote from the sponsor; a
  Trust (represented by a Trustee) is more common in the US, companies in the Euromarkets. Amortising
  (pass-through): principal and interest paid each coupon, priced on expected maturity and WAL; RMBS,
  CMBS, consumer ABS. Revolving: principal collections buy new receivables during the revolving period
  (credit cards, auto loans); then controlled amortisation or soft bullet. Master trust: frequent issuers, multiple
  series from one pool; MBS and credit card ABS (preferred for cards since 1991).
  • Participants and conflicts (CR-22 d; CR-23 a). Originator (sponsor/seller); underwriter or arranger (is-
  suer/depositor, bears warehousing risk); rating agencies (paid by issuers and involved in setting enhance-
  ment, so incentive to require less); servicer (CMBS foreclose vs extend conflict between senior and junior);
  managers (first-loss retention, Dodd-Frank); trustee and custodian; monoline wraps.
  • Motivations (CR-22 m; CR-23 d). Issuers: term funding and maturity matching, cheaper funding (the notes’
  rating is de-linked from the originator’s), diversified funding, regulatory and economic capital relief (Basel
  I example: 12% target, 1.5% first loss retained, frees 10.5%), higher ROE, credit risk transfer; arbitrage vs
  balance sheet CDOs. Investors: access to loan pools, tailored risk-return, reaching for yield. Northern Rock:
  over-reliance on securitised funding.
  • Credit enhancements (CR-23 e): over-collateralisation, pool insurance, senior/junior note classes, margin
  step-up, excess spread held in a reserve account. The lower the asset quality, the greater the enhancement
  needed.
  • Simulation, PD and correlation (CR-22 h, i). Simulate correlated normals, map to default times t =
  − log[1−Φ(z)]/λ, count defaults per year, push each thread through the waterfall. Higher default probability
  hurts every tranche. Higher correlation raises equity value (U-shaped, “lottery ticket”) and raises senior
  losses; the mezzanine behaves like a senior at low default rates and like equity at high ones.
  • Default01, risk factors, implied correlation (CR-22 j, k, l). Default01 = −[V (π + 10bp) − V (π −
  10bp)]/20, peaking near each tranche’s attachment point. Risk factors: systematic risk, tranche thinness,
  granularity (concentration risk), prepayment, extension and refinancing risk. Implied (base) correlation is
  backed out of standard tranche prices (CDX: 0-3, 3-7, 7-10, 10-15, 15-30%); it varies by tranche (correlation
  skew) and is a market, not credit, risk concept (see D15).
  • Performance tools by asset class (CR-23 f, g). Credit cards: delinquency ratio (overdue more than 90 days
  / total receivables, an early indication of quality) and default ratio (written off in the period / receivables at
  period end), both tied to early amortisation triggers. MPR (principal and interest collections / pool balance)
  for all non-amortising classes, with a minimum set as an early amortisation trigger; it is not a prepayment
  measure. CMBS: DSCR (NOI / debt payments; below 1.0 the property cannot cover debt service; crucial be-
  cause CMBS is non-recourse). Mortgages: WAC, WAM, WAL. Autos: loss curves and absolute prepayment
  speed.
  • WAC, WAM, WAL (CR-23 g). WAC is balance-weighted and, compared with the net investor coupon,
  shows the pool’s ability to pay. WAM is balance-weighted remaining term in months: on stated matu-
  rityPit recognises liquidity risk; on the reset date it shows the shortening effect of prepayments. WAL
  = (a/365) P F (s) lets investors compare an MBS with investments of similar maturity (Table 23.1 gives
  2.58 years). A WAL is tied to a prepayment assumption.
  • Prepayment forecasting (CR-23 h). For a given price, prepayments reduce the yield on the MBS. SMM: an
  SMM of 0.65% means about 0.65% of the balance at the start of the month, less scheduled principal, prepays.
  CPR = 1 − (1 − SMM)12 , based on pool characteristics and the expected economic environment. 100%
  PSA: CPR starts at 0, rises 0.2% a month to 6% at month 30, then constant 6%; other speeds are multiples,
  reflecting slower prepayment in the first 30 months of seasoning. Applies to mortgages, home equity and
  student loans. Auto loans: no real prepayment culture, prepayment speed extremely stable.

  Exam traps
  • Two kinds of OC (sibling swap): hard over-collateralisation is collateral par in excess of note par, fixed at
  issue; the Malz overcollateralisation account is soft enhancement built from diverted excess spread and escrowed
  recoveries, compounding at the money market rate and released to equity only at maturity. Your card’s “funds
  paid less funds received” is not the books’ wording (outside-source): use the book definitions.
  • Dropped or mis-sized terms in the interim year: survivors are N minus cumulative defaults, not this year’s
  only; fees come before coupons; recoveries go into the account, not to the equity; last year’s balance must be
  grown at r.
  • Final-year order (sibling swap with pass-throughs): the senior is paid par plus its final coupon in full before
  the mezzanine, and the equity gets only the residual. Splitting terminal funds pro rata is pass-through logic, not
  a credit waterfall.
  • PSA ramp threshold: up to month 30 the 100% PSA CPR is 0.2% × m; after that it is a flat 6%. SMM is
  monthly and on the pool balance net of scheduled principal; in an overcollateralised deal the note balance is the
  wrong denominator.
  • Polarity flips: faster prepayments reduce yield for a given price and shorten WAL; correlation helps the equity
  and hurts the senior; an MPR below the minimum trips the early amortisation trigger.
  • Asset-class sibling swaps: delinquency (overdue > 90 days) vs default (written off); MPR is not a prepayment
  measure; DSCR is a CMBS credit measure; ABS speed and loss curves are for autos.
