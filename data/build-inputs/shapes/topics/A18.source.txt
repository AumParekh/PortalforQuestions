  Revision notes
  • Time-weighted versus dollar-weighted return (IM-7 a): the geometric average is a time-weighted average
  (each period’s return has equal weight). The dollar-weighted return is the IRR of all cash flows, so periods
  with more money invested weigh more. Book example: buy one share at 50, a second at 53, sell both at
  54: dollar-weighted 7.117% versus time-weighted 7.81%, lower because the weaker second year had more
  money in it. Time-weighting judges the manager, who does not control the client’s cash flows.
  • Comparison universes (IM-7 b): percentile rankings against funds of similar style are a useful first step,
  but subgroups differ in risk (high-beta growth, different durations) and the median fund is not an investable
  benchmark known in advance.
  • Sharpe ratio (IM-7 b; IM-3 b): (r̄P − r̄f )/σP , reward to total volatility, the slope of the capital allocation
  line. Relevant “when choosing among portfolios competing for the overall risky portfolio”; the benchmark
  is the market index’s Sharpe ratio. Ang: when the benchmark is the risk-free rate, the information ratio
  coincides with the Sharpe ratio (Martingale: 8.59/12.22 = 0.70 versus 0.45 for the Russell 1000).
  • M 2 (IM-7 c): mix P with T-bills (or borrow) so that the adjusted portfolio P∗ has the index’s standard
  deviation; M 2 = rP ∗ − rM = (SP − SM )σM , the vertical distance between M and P∗ . Same ranking as
  Sharpe, read as a return. Example 8.1: weights 0.714 and 0.286, rP ∗ = 26.7%, M 2 = −1.3%.
  • Treynor and T 2 (IM-7 b, c): excess return per unit of beta, the slope of the T-line; the SML slope is the
  market’s Treynor. Relevant “when ranking many portfolios that will be mixed to form the overall risky
  portfolio” (CalPERS’ funds of funds), because each manager’s nonsystematic risk diversifies away. T 2 mixes
  with T-bills to a beta of 1: Q (w = 0.8) gives T 2 = 2.8%, U (β = 0.8, w = 1.25, borrowing) gives 3.75%,
  so U wins despite a lower alpha (3.0% versus 3.5%).
  • Jensen’s alpha (IM-7 b; IM-3 b): αP = r̄P − [r̄f + βP (r̄M − r̄f )]; multifactor versions subtract SMB and
  HML loadings. Positive alpha is necessary to beat the index under every measure (so it is the most widely
  used), but not sufficient: departing from diversification can raise volatility until the Sharpe ratio falls. No
  situation uses alpha alone to choose between funds.
  • Information ratio (IM-7 b; IM-3 b): BKM: αP /σ(eP ), alpha per unit of nonsystematic risk (“tracking
  error”); some call this the appraisal ratio and define the IR with excess return, but BKM uses alpha consis-
  tently. Relevant “when evaluating a portfolio to be mixed with the benchmark portfolio” (a university adding
  a hedge fund to an indexed core): the improvement in the Sharpe ratio is driven by the IR (Eq. 8.2). Ang:
  alpha is the average excess (active) return over a benchmark, tracking error its standard deviation; IRs above
  one are not common.

  • Benchmark choice (IM-3 c): alpha is “first and foremost a statement about a benchmark”. Martingale
  against a naive Russell 1000 benchmark (beta assumed 1): alpha 1.50%, IR 0.24; against the risk-adjusted
  benchmark 0.27 T-bills plus 0.73 Russell 1000: alpha 3.44%, IR 0.78. A sound benchmark is well defined,
  tradeable, replicable and adjusted for risk; most industry benchmarks are not risk adjusted.
  √
  • Grinold’s fundamental law (IM-3 d): the maximum attainable IR = IC × BR, ignoring transaction
  costs and trading restrictions. IC is the correlation of forecasts with actual returns; breadth is the number of
  independent bets. A market timer making 4 bets a year needs IC 0.25 for IR 0.50; a stock selector making
  400 bets needs IC 0.025. “Play often and play well.”
  • Fundamental law: assumptions and limitations (IM-3 d): derived under mean-variance utility, so it ig-
  nores downside and higher-moment risk and assumes information is used optimally; forecasts must be in-
  dependent. Limitation 1: IC is assumed constant across BR, yet ICs fall as assets under management rise
  (decreasing returns to scale in mutual funds, hedge funds and private equity). Limitation 2: bets are corre-
  lated: 1,000 value overweights against 1,000 growth underweights is one bet on a value-growth factor; one
  hundred managers reaching for yield is one bet on illiquidity.
  √
  • Statistical significance (IM-7 d): t(α) = α N /σ(e). Joe Dart: monthly alpha 0.2%, σ(e) = 2%; t = 1.96
  needs N = 384 months (32 years), while typical manager tenure is under 5 years.
  • Selection and survivorship bias (IM-7 f): with thousands of funds a few “winners” appear by luck; restrict-
  ing a sample to funds alive for the whole period drops closed funds and creates survivorship bias, which can
  be substantial even when few funds fail. See also A24.
  • Style analysis (IM-7 e): Sharpe regresses fund returns on asset-class indexes with coefficients constrained
  to be non-negative and to sum to 100%; R2 is the share of return variability attributable to style choice, the
  intercept the average return from security selection. Magellan (Lynch era): 61% large cap, 35% medium
  cap, 5% growth, R2 = 97.5%, intercept 32 bp a month (19.19% over 5 years). A 100% R2 can coexist with
  a positive intercept.
  • Changing portfolio composition (IM-7 f): conventional measures assume constant risk. Example 8.3:
  Sharpe 0.5 in each year (1%/2%, then 9%/18%) but 0.37 on the eight quarters pooled (mean 5%, SD 13.42%),
  below the market’s 0.4: shifting means look like volatility and bias the Sharpe ratio downward.
  • Performance manipulation (IM-7 g): the Sharpe ratio is invariant to a constant T-bill mix, but a manager can
  raise leverage after poor early returns and deleverage after good ones, reweighting the subperiods; investors
  lose on average. Only the Morningstar Risk-Adjusted Rating (MRAR, γ = 2 for funds) is manipulation-
  proof; stars by percentile: 0 to 10, 10 to 32.5, 32.5 to 67.5, 67.5 to 90, 90 to 100.
  • Market timing (IM-7 h): Treynor-Mazuy adds c(rM − rf )2 ; Henriksson-Merton adds c(rM − rf )D with
  beta b in bear and b + c in bull markets; c > 0 indicates timing. In Table 8.2, Q’s alpha falls
  √ from 5.26% to
  −2.29% once timing (c = 0.10) is allowed. Perfect timing is a free call: C = 2N ( 21 σM T ) − 1 = 7.98%
  at σM = 20.04%; imperfect timing is worth (P1 + P2 − 1) × C; a coin flip scores zero.
  • Performance attribution (IM-7 i): theP  bogey is the passive benchmark (neutral weights,
  P index within each
  class). Asset allocation contribution = (wP −wB )×rindex ; selection contribution =  wP ×(rP −rindex );
  together they equal the excess return. BKM example: 31 bp allocation plus 106 bp selection = 137 bp; within
  equity, 147 bp excess splits into 129 bp sector allocation and 18 bp security selection, weighted by the 70%
  equity weight (102.9 bp) before adding to the portfolio total.

  Exam traps
  • Wrong lens (sibling swap): Sharpe for one of many managers (should be Treynor), Treynor for an active add-on
  to an index (should be the information ratio). Name the decision context before computing anything.
  • Raw instead of excess return (dropped term): dividing r̄P by σP or βP , or forgetting the T-bill leg of M 2 .
  This can flip a ranking.
  • Naive benchmark (wrong-input twin): alpha as r̄P −r̄M assumes beta of 1 (Martingale: 1.50% versus 3.44%);
  correlation used as beta; σP /σM used as beta without ρ.
  • Highest alpha wins (absolute claim): alpha is necessary, not sufficient; Table 8.1’s U beats the higher-alpha
  Q.
  • Attribution weights: allocation uses index returns, selection uses portfolio weights; a selection effect inside
  equity must be multiplied by the equity weight before it enters the portfolio total.
  • Fundamental law scope: it gives a maximum IR before costs, assumes independent bets and an IC that does
  not decay with size.
