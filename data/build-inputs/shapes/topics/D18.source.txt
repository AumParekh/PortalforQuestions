  Revision notes
  • Three determinants (MR-13): the shape of the term structure is set by expectations of future short rates, the
  risk premium investors demand for bearing interest rate risk, and convexity, whose effect comes from rate
  volatility: f (T ) = r0 + D E[dr]/dt + λD − 12 Cσ 2 .
  • Expectations term and inflation (MR-13 a, S1): “the higher the instantaneous rate, the higher the forward
  rate; the more rates are expected to increase, the higher the forward rate; and the greater the corresponding
  duration, the greater the effect of expected rate changes on the forward rate.” Long-run expectations can be
  anchored by macroeconomics: book example 4% = 1% long-run real rate + 3% long-run inflation. Higher
  expected inflation therefore works through this term, most strongly at long durations.
  • Granularity (MR-13 a): short-horizon expectations can be very specific (policy meetings, tax dates, issuance
  calendar, quarter-end balance sheets); long-horizon expectations are smooth, which is why inflation anchors
  matter most at the long end.
  • Risk premium term (MR-13 d, S2): risk-averse investors demand E[dP /P ] = r0 dt + λD dt: a premium
  proportional to duration. λ “may depend on time and on the level of rates, but not on the characteristics of
  any individual bond”. The forward rate rises by λD: “the greater the corresponding interest rate risk and
  the greater the risk premium, the greater the forward rate”. Book: r = 1%, D = 5, λ = 10 bp gives 1.5%
  expected return.
  • Sharpe ratio view: SR = λ/σ; 10 bp per year of duration over 100 bp rate volatility is a Sharpe ratio of
  10%.
  • Convexity term under stress (MR-13 b, c, S2): higher rate volatility lowers forward rates by 12 Cσ 2 , and
  C grows with term, so the effect is concentrated at the long end (book tree: forwards 9.0000%, 8.8532%,
  8.5590% with flat 9% expectations). Volatility pushes long forwards down; the risk premium pushes them
  up. See also D12.
  • Return decomposition and maturity (MR-13 d): dP /P = f (T )dt − D dr + 12 Cσ 2 dt. A rise in rates costs
  return in proportion to duration, so for the same rate move long bonds lose more than short bonds; volatility
  adds return in proportion to convexity.
  • Expectations cannot be separated from risk premium (MR-13): a drift of a number of basis points has
  the same pricing effect as a risk premium of that number per year of duration. “The term structure of interest
  rates cannot, on its own, be used to separate expectations of rate changes from risk premium”; only the
  risk-neutral process matters for pricing.
  • Two hypotheses (MR-13): pure expectations (λ = 0; forwards are realised in the no-change scenario) versus
  pure risk premium (E[dr/dt] = 0; the term structure stays unchanged in the no-change scenario). Reality
  can lie between.

  • Inflation and volatility (MR-15 d): “Periods of high inflation and high short-term interest rates are inherently
  unstable and, as a result, the basis-point volatility of the short rate tends to be high”, which motivates CIR
  and lognormal models. See also D1.
  • Inflation and mean reversion (MR-14 footnote): hyperinflation is not consistent with mean reversion and
  destroys a currency and its rates: “interest rates mean revert until they don’t”.
  • Inflation as a macro factor (IM-2 b): high inflation is bad for both stocks and bonds. Government, invest-
  ment grade and high yield bonds average 8.6%, 8.8% and 9.2% in low-inflation periods versus 5.4%, 5.3%
  and 6.0% in high-inflation periods: fixed payments lose real value. Part of the long-run bond risk premium
  compensates for doing badly when inflation is high. Government bonds do relatively well in low-growth
  periods (10.0% versus 3.9%) but are more volatile in recessions (15.5% versus 9.3%). See also B4.
  • Climate risk (outside-source): no 2026 MR or IM reading in the provided material treats climate risk and
  inflation directly. In an exam stem, treat a climate shock through the mechanics above: its effect on expected
  inflation (expectations term), on uncertainty (volatility and convexity) and on required compensation (risk
  premium).

  Exam traps
  • Polarity of the convexity term: higher volatility lowers long forward rates (and supports long bond prices); it
  does not raise them.
  • Scope of the risk premium: λ is common to all bonds, but the premium in a forward rate or expected return is
  λD: larger at long maturities, not equal across the curve.
  • Absolute claim: forward rates alone cannot split a move into expected inflation and risk premium.
  • Sign in the return equation: rate rises enter with −D, volatility with + 12 Cσ 2 .
  • Sibling swap with IM-2: government bonds do relatively well in low-growth periods but badly when inflation
  is high; swapping the two macro factors reverses the answer.
