  Revision notes
  The specifics first: AI vs GenAI, GenAI’s downsides, AI in market risk
  • Two model groups (S1, CI-1 a). The IMF groups models as (1) AI/ML models: established predictive
  analytics tools such as natural language processing (NLP), neural networks, decision trees and clustering;
  (2) sophisticated AI models: the most advanced innovations, including large language models (LLMs), deep
  learning and reinforcement learning.
  • What GenAI is and does (S1). GenAI builds on existing strategies and models: investment idea generation,
  code development for analysts, customer-facing work; LLMs are being integrated into analytical frameworks
  for more accurate forecasting. The Transformer architecture (which uses an attention mechanism and pro-
  cesses data in parallel) laid the foundation for LLMs and GenAI. GenAI moved chatbots from rules-based
  systems to LLM-based ones. Future benefits: lower barriers to entry for less liquid asset classes and faster
  market reactions to news.
  • Human in the loop (S1). Sophisticated AI has so far mainly generated signals as inputs to existing systems;
  human traders still make the final decisions, not fully autonomous AI trading agents. More autonomy is
  expected (particularly in equities), but a human-in-the-loop approach is likely to remain.
  • GenAI failure mode: hallucination (S1, S2). A type of model inaccuracy associated with GenAI in which
  a model produces a highly confident but incorrect response. Because LLM outputs are typically unstructured
  text, outcome analysis and error-rate measurement are difficult.
  • GenAI disadvantages (S2, CI-2 c). Fraud: video and voice generation enables deepfakes, fake profiles and
  IDs, bypassed security checks; generating fraudulent AI content may currently be easier than detecting it.
  Disinformation becomes more convincing and scalable, dangerous in stress. Cyber: GenAI enables more
  effective attacks at higher frequency and lowers barriers to entry for less sophisticated threat actors; bad actors
  may benefit more than legitimate users in the short term (legitimate users are expected to gain more over the
  long term). Cautious legitimate actors create an asymmetry with risk-taking malicious actors. Remedy for
  fraud: AI literacy and financial literacy among customers.
  • Attack vectors. Prompt injection extracts confidential information; model/data poisoning intentionally ma-
  nipulates model weights and/or training data. Best practice: a new AI risk management framework or AI-
  specific additions to the existing one, plus AI questions in vendor due diligence.
  • AI in market risk (S3). CI-1: in risk management (reporting and monitoring) AI streamlines compliance
  monitoring, generates tailored risk insights, develops advanced risk scenarios and enables dynamic risk sens-
  ing. CI-2: operations use cases include volatility and liquidity risk management, building models that better
  predict how heightened volatility may affect positions; firms increasingly use AI for liquidity, market and
  credit risk, but representative data on that use are lacking.

  IMF (CI-1): uses, market dynamics, stability, regulators
  • Uses and benefits (CI-1 a). IMF outreach: 27 firms, buy side and sell side. Four broad risk areas: opera-
  tional risk; volatility and speed under stress; cyber and manipulation; monitoring and transparency (opacity).
  Functions: client profiling, asset allocation (signals from alternative and unstructured data), trading (liquid-
  ity, execution, less market impact, price discovery), risk management. Value is greatest in liquid public
  markets (equities, listed derivatives, then government bonds and FX); strong link between market liquidity
  and AI adoption. Survey benefits: productivity, unstructured data processing, narrower bid-ask spreads,
  cheaper trading algorithm design. High fixed costs favour large firms; North America leads foundational
  models.
  • Market dynamics (CI-1 b). AI may favour NBFIs (over half of global financial assets) over legacy-bound
  banks. US algorithmic share: about 70% of equities, over 50% of futures, over 40% of options, under 10%
  of fixed income. Normal times: better efficiency and liquidity, fewer non-informational price swings; stress:
  volatility, occasional large swings, auto-shutdowns create feedback loops. Flighty liquidity under stress:
  HFTs reduce order cancellations as they step back; institutions increase hidden orders. New dynamics:
  faster, more accurate reactions to news (FOMC minutes), higher and procyclical volumes (AI ETFs have
  higher turnover), possible AI manipulation or unintended collusion.
  • Stability concerns (CI-1 c). Principal concern: concentration (strategies built on open-source models
  trained on data from few vendors; few dominant providers). Also market fragility (flash crashes, data poi-
  soning, cyberattacks), hallucinations and lack of explainability, manipulation via deepfakes, high fixed costs.
  Lesser: data access, customer fraud, shock transmission to emerging and developing economies. Respon-
  dents prefer best practices and flexible guidelines to strict rules and want extreme-scenario stress testing.
  Challenges: volatility under stress (herding, flight to safety), opacity as activity shifts to lightly overseen
  NBFIs, cyber and manipulation, third-party dependence.
  • Regulators and supervisors (CI-1 d). Institutions remain fully responsible for AI they deploy, built or
  bought. Focus areas: governance, transparency and explainability, outsourcing, data quality and bias, re-
  porting, human oversight (FSB, BCBS, BIS, NIST, IOSCO). Supervisory AI: automated data quality checks,
  data integration, anomaly detection, real-time monitoring, misinformation detection; GenAI for legacy code,
  retrieval, code generation and debugging. Priorities: (1) speed and volatility: circuit breakers, algorithm
  testing, initial margin in normal times, margin call volatility, variation margin, CCP margin models; (2)
  third-party concentration: map links between critical IT and AI providers, cyber resilience across the AI life-
  cycle; (3) opacity: map model, data and infrastructure interdependencies, NBFI disclosure, liquidity buffers;
  (4) OTC markets: transaction data, central clearing incentives, margin for non-centrally cleared derivatives,
  backstops.
  FSB (CI-2): drivers, use cases, vulnerabilities
  • Drivers since 2017 (CI-2 a). Evidence says the supply side has been the more influential driver. Supply:
  technology (embeddings for unstructured data, the Transformer, GPUs and cheaper compute; human capital
  shortage), data (digital engagement, unstructured data, possible need for synthetic data), business models
  (cloud and pre-trained models; trend toward open-source). Demand: profitability (revenue, cost reduction,
  risk management), competition, regulation (compliance).
  • Use cases (CI-2 b). Industry, four areas: customers (credit scoring, marketing, adviser matching, insurance
  pricing, LLM chatbots); operations (model risk management, capital optimisation, code generation, market
  impact, volatility and liquidity models; GenAI content, retrieval, legacy code, voice transcription); trad-
  ing and portfolio management (reinforcement learning execution, sentiment in text); regulatory compliance
  (RegTech: fraud, AML, CFT). SupTech: information collection, statistics, payments oversight, real-time
  analysis, LLM-drafted inspection reports; future GenAI simulation of stress tests and bank runs.
  • Third-party concentration (S4). Few cloud and chip suppliers, vertical integration, data aggregator consol-
  idation. Concentration pushed up by complexity and cost, vertical integration, GPU bottlenecks, incumbent
  investment, multimodality; pushed down by hardware competition, open-source models, architectural in-
  novations, domain- and task-specific models. Mitigant: third-party risk standards (periodic assessments,
  vendor inventories, ongoing monitoring and due diligence).
  • Market correlations (S4). Similar models and data (limited model choice, herding, network externalities,
  opaque design) raise correlations: flash crashes, amplified volatility, liquidity crunches. Pre-trained core
  models share architectures and data, so outputs correlate; GenAI output re-used as training data adds to it.
  Risk is higher when LLMs inform risk management, investment strategy or new AI applications, far lower

  for back-office uses. AI can also reduce correlations (customised strategies, new entrants). Mitigants: circuit
  breakers, transparency, monitoring and surveillance.
  • Model risk, data, governance (S4). In crises models are harder to monitor, validate and correct; complex-
  ity and lower explainability raise model risk; opaque, unstructured training data complicate data quality.
  Regulated institutions handle this through their existing model risk frameworks: data quality assessment,
  validation, performance and outcome analysis, ongoing monitoring, plans for limited data.
  • Other and longer term. Misalignment: profit-maximising AI that learns collusion or spreads disinformation
  triggering runs. Longer term: compute and data demand, uneven regulation, effects on rates, inflation and
  labour (greater risk for high-skilled workers), energy (about 1% of global use, rising). FSB priorities: close
  data gaps, test whether frameworks cover these vulnerabilities, build supervisory capability.
  Exam traps
  • Sibling swap on the model groups: NLP, neural networks, decision trees and clustering are the established
  AI/ML group; LLMs, deep learning and reinforcement learning are the sophisticated group. Also: prompt
  injection extracts data, poisoning manipulates weights or training data; cloud and pre-trained models are a
  supply-side (business model) driver, not demand side.
  • Polarity flips: bad actors gain more in the short term; LLM text output makes outcome analysis harder; under
  stress HFT order cancellations fall and hidden orders rise; the survey cited narrower spreads and high fixed
  costs.
  • Scope error on correlation: correlation risk is high when LLMs inform risk management or investment strat-
  egy, far lower for back-office uses such as retrieval, training or drafting.
  • Role misassignment: the institution stays fully responsible for bought AI; model risk sits in the institution’s
  existing model risk framework; humans make the final trading decisions.
  • Sibling swap on concentration: architectural innovations, open-source and domain-specific models push con-
  centration down; multimodality, vertical integration and GPU bottlenecks push it up.
  • Absolute claims: circuit breakers and transparency mitigate, they do not eliminate; respondents want flexible
  guidelines, not strict rules; a human in the loop is expected to remain.
