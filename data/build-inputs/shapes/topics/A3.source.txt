  Revision notes
  • The specific: the equity skew (MR-17 a, e). Since the October 1987 crash, the smile for equity options
  (individual stocks and indices) slopes downward and is called a volatility skew: implied volatility decreases
  as the strike price increases. Low-strike options (deep out-of-the-money puts, deep in-the-money calls)
  carry significantly higher implied volatilities than high-strike options (deep in-the-money puts, deep out-of-
  the-money calls). Before October 1987 there was no marked smile.
  • The specific: maturity (MR-17 g). Implied volatility tends to be an increasing function of maturity when
  short-dated volatilities are historically low (volatilities are expected to rise) and a decreasing function when
  they are historically high. Separately, the smile becomes less pronounced as option maturity increases (ob-
  served for currencies and for options on most other assets).
  • Smile and surface defined (MR-17 a). A volatility smile is the implied volatility of options with a certain
  life plotted against strike price; a volatility surface is implied volatility as a function of both strike price
  and time to maturity. Traders use Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM), but not as originally intended: they let the
  volatility depend on strike and maturity.
  • Put-call parity implication (MR-17 b). p + S0 e−qT = c + Ke−rT rests on a simple no-arbitrage argument
  with no assumption about the distribution of the asset price: true whether or not it is lognormal. It holds for
  BSM prices and for market prices, so pBS − pmkt = cBS − cmkt (17.2): the dollar pricing error is the same
  for a European put and call with the same strike and maturity, so their implied volatilities are equal (put at
  22% implies call at 22%). The smile and the surface are therefore the same for calls and puts; approximately
  true for American options. Example 17.1: FX call at strike 0.59 with implied vol 14.5%; parity with q =
  foreign rate gives put price 0.0419, also 14.5%.
  • Smile shape versus implied distribution (MR-17 c). Read the tails through deep out-of-the-money options:
  if the implied (risk-neutral) distribution puts more probability in a tail than a lognormal with the same mean
  and standard deviation, the option paying off in that tail is priced relatively high, so its implied volatility is
  relatively high; less probability means a relatively low price and implied volatility.
  • FX smile (MR-17 d, e). Implied volatility is relatively low for at-the-money options and becomes progres-
  sively higher as an option moves either into or out of the money: U-shaped. The implied distribution has

  heavier tails than the lognormal (kurtosis) and is more peaked: small and large moves more likely, interme-
  diate moves less likely. Deep OTM calls (high K2 ) and deep OTM puts (low K1 ) are both priced relatively
  high.
  • FX evidence (MR-17 d). Table 17.1 (10 currencies against the USD, 2005 to 2015): daily moves exceed 3
  SD on 1.30% of days versus 0.27% under the lognormal model; 4, 5 and 6 SD on 0.49%, 0.24% and 0.13%
  versus almost never. Business Snapshot 17.1: buy deep OTM calls and puts on many currencies and wait;
  the mid-1980s traders who knew about heavy tails made money until, by the late 1980s, the smile was priced
  in.
  • Why FX is not lognormal (MR-17 d, e). Lognormality needs (1) constant volatility and (2) smooth price
  changes with no jumps. Exchange rate volatility is far from constant and rates frequently jump, sometimes
  in response to central bank actions; both make extreme outcomes more likely. As maturity increases, the
  percentage impact of non-constant volatility on prices becomes more pronounced but on implied volatility
  usually less pronounced; jumps’ impact on both becomes less pronounced (they are averaged out). Net: the
  smile flattens with maturity.
  • Equity implied distribution (MR-17 c, e). Heavier left tail and less heavy right tail than the lognormal. A
  deep OTM call (K2 ) is priced relatively low (low implied vol); a deep OTM put (K1 ) relatively high (high
  implied vol). Hull’s summary: traders assume equity distributions have a heavier left tail and less heavy right
  tail, and exchange rate distributions a heavier right and left tail.
  • Why the equity skew (MR-17 e). Negative correlation between equity prices and volatility: as prices fall,
  volatility rises, making further declines possible; as prices rise, volatility falls. Reasons: (1) leverage: falling
  equity raises leverage and so volatility; (2) volatility feedback: an external rise in volatility makes investors
  require a higher return, so the price declines; (3) crashophobia (Rubinstein): fear of another October 1987,
  supported by the skew steepening when the S&P 500 falls and flattening when it rises.
  • Alternative ways to characterise the smile (MR-17 f). Against K the curve shifts right (left) as the asset
  price rises (falls), so implied volatility is often plotted against K/S0 ; a refinement uses K/F0 , and traders
  often define at-the-money as K = F0 (the risk-neutral expected price at maturity). Against delta: at-the-
  money is a call delta of 0.5 or a put delta of −0.5 (“50-delta options”), which lets smiles apply to options
  other than European and American calls and puts.
  • Volatility surfaces in pricing (MR-17 g). Table 17.2 combines smiles and the term structure; entries with
  reliable market data come from market prices, the rest are interpolated. A 9-month option at K/S0 = 1.05
  interpolates 13.4% and 14.0% to 13.7%; a 1.5-year option at K/S0 = 0.925 uses bilinear interpolation to
  give 14.525%. That volatility goes into BSM or a binomial tree. Plotting against √1T ln(K/F0 ) makes the
  smile much less dependent on maturity.
  • Greeks and the smile (MR-17 h). Standard Greeks assume implied volatility is unchanged when the
  asset price changes, which is not what is expected. The minimum variance delta ∆M V = ∆BSM +
  νBSM ∂E(σimp )/∂S; for equities vega is positive and ∂E(σimp )/∂S negative, so ∆M V < ∆BSM .
  • Role of the model (MR-17 g). BSM can be argued to be a sophisticated interpolation tool: switching to
  another plausible model would change the surface but arguably not the dollar prices. Greek letters, and
  so hedging, do depend on the model; an unrealistic model leads to poor hedging. Models matter most for
  derivatives that do not trade actively (many exotics).
  • A single large jump (MR-17 i). A stock at USD 50 that will move to 42 or 58 on news (takeover, lawsuit): the
  true distribution is bimodal (a mixture of two lognormals). With r = 12%, p = 0.5314: implied volatilities
  58.8% at 44, 69.5% at 48, 69.2% at 50, 49.0% at 56, 0% at 42 and 58. The pattern is a frown, the opposite
  of the currency smile; the volatility implied at strike 50 overprices options at 44 or 56.
  • Implied distribution from the smile (Appendix). Breeden-Litzenberger: g(K) = erT ∂ 2 c/∂K 2 ≈
  erT (c1 + c3 − 2c2 )/δ 2 , the value of a butterfly spread. Example 17A.1 (stock at USD 10, vols 30% at
  strike 6 falling to 22% at 14): probability of 6 to 7 is 0.0057 versus 0.0031 lognormal, of 13 to 14 is 0.0113
  versus 0.0167: heavier left tail, less heavy right tail.

  Shape map. Heavier tail ⇒ OTM option in that tail priced high ⇒ high implied vol. FX: both tails heavy, U-shaped
  smile. Equity: heavy left, thin right, downward skew. Single anticipated jump: bimodal, frown.
  Exam traps
  • Polarity flip (strike): for equity options implied volatility falls as strike rises; the low-strike (OTM put) volatil-
  ities are highest.
  • Polarity and condition (maturity): the smile flattens as maturity increases; the term structure slopes up only
  when short-dated volatilities are historically low, and down when they are historically high.
  • Sibling swap (shape and reason): FX is U-shaped with both tails heavy, explained by non-constant volatility
  and jumps; equity is skewed with a heavy left and thin right tail, explained by leverage, volatility feedback and
  crashophobia. Attributing one market’s explanation to the other is the map’s flagged trap.
  • Lognormal price versus market: with one at-the-money volatility, deep OTM index puts and both FX wings
  are priced below market; deep OTM index calls are priced above market.
  • Scope error (put-call parity): parity equates implied volatilities of a call and a put with the same strike and
  maturity, with no distributional assumption; it says nothing about equality across strikes, and a gap between
  them is an arbitrage even if prices are not lognormal.
  • Jump shape: a single large anticipated jump gives a frown, not a smile.
