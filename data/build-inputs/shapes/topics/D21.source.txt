  Revision notes
  Context (ORR-14 a)
  • Investor protection. Legislation and regulation “aimed at ensuring that investors are fully informed about
  their investments when they enter into financial transactions.” It also protects investors from misrepre-
  sentation by intermediaries, establishes accountability for fraud or insider trading, and supports cross-
  jurisdictional cooperation between regulators and law enforcement. Some of the largest settlements and
  penalties paid by banks and investment firms come from investor protection violations.
  • Where it sits in the taxonomy. Compliance with financial laws usually falls in Basel event type 4, Clients,
  Products & Business Practices (CPBP), Level 2 categories 4.1 Suitability, Disclosure & Fiduciary and
  4.2 Improper Business or Market Practices (see A22 for the Level 3 examples: aggressive sales, account
  churning, market manipulation, insider trading on the firm’s account).
  • Link to AML. Compliance requirements include strict KYC filings so that money flowing into financial
  products is not of doubtful origin; investor protection and AML legislation are therefore connected (see
  A29).
  MiFID, MiFID II and MiFIR (S1, S2, S3)
  • MiFID. Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (2004/39/EC), promulgated 2004, applicable across the
  EU since November 2007, in force 2007 to 2018. It prescribes conduct of business and organisational re-
  quirements for investment firms and authorisation requirements for regulated markets, defines regulatory
  reporting and trade transparency obligations to prevent market abuse, and sets rules for admitting instru-
  ments to trading.
  • MiFID II and MiFIR. Adopted June 2014 after the crisis: a revised directive (MiFID II) and a regulation
  (MiFIR). Besides stronger investor protection, they add public disclosure of trading data and transaction
  reporting to regulators and supervisors. Book list of what MiFIR addresses: remuneration of traders and
  advisors, and conflicts of interest; fair and non-misleading communication to customers; definition and in-
  dependence of investment advice; sales process and product governance; best execution of client trades;
  dealings with eligible counterparties.
  • Directive versus regulation. EU regulations apply directly in all member states. Directives must be turned
  into national law, and each country may be more stringent, but not less, than the directive.
  • Suitability and appropriateness (S1). In the GARP text, suitability appears as Basel category 4.1 and in
  the SR 13-19 example of an inappropriate incentive: variable fees that direct customers to higher-margin
  products without considering whether they suit the customer. Under MiFID II (outside-source): suitability
  applies to investment advice and portfolio management and requires information on the client’s knowledge
  and experience, financial situation (including ability to bear losses) and investment objectives (including risk

  tolerance); appropriateness applies to non-advised services in complex products and checks only knowledge
  and experience; execution-only dealing in non-complex products at the client’s initiative needs neither.
  • Best execution (S2). FINRA Rule 5310: seek the most favourable terms reasonably available for a customer’s
  order; criteria include price improvement and speed of execution. The MiFID II equivalent requires all
  sufficient steps to obtain the best possible result considering price, costs, speed, likelihood of execution and
  settlement, size and nature of the order (outside-source).
  • Conflicts of interest (S3). A specific driver of compliance risk is the conflict of interest of traders who trade
  both for clients’ books and for the firm’s own book; another is the information asymmetry between retail
  investors and the banks and asset managers that sell to them. MiFIR targets remuneration structures and the
  independence of advice; regulators watch every incentive structure that can lead to poor conduct (ORR-5:
  mis-selling driven by aggressive commercial incentives is a rule-based mistake, cured by changing the rule).
  Dodd-Frank and the Volcker Rule (S4)
  • Investor Protection Act. A component of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection
  Act (the book dates it 2009; it was signed in July 2010, outside-source), a response to the 2007 crisis. It
  set up a committee to consult the SEC on new products, fee structures and trading strategies, and increased
  whistleblower protections.
  • Other Dodd-Frank elements. OTC derivatives trading regulation delegated to the SEC and the CFTC;
  firms buying and selling derivatives must use clearinghouses; federal oversight for financial stability, with
  a financial stability oversight council and a reorganisation of financial regulation; the Consumer Financial
  Protection Bureau, an independent regulator for consumer finance (mortgages, student loans, credit cards).
  • Volcker Rule. Contained in Dodd-Frank. It “intends to prevent commercial banks from engaging in specu-
  lative activities and proprietary trading for profit” and limits banks’ investments in hedge funds and private
  equity funds.
  • The exemptions (outside-source). Permitted activities include underwriting; market making-related ac-
  tivity, provided inventory is designed not to exceed the reasonably expected near-term demand of clients,
  customers or counterparties; risk-mitigating hedging of specific, identifiable risks of the bank’s positions;
  trading in government obligations; and trading on behalf of customers. Ownership of covered funds (hedge
  and private equity funds) is limited, broadly to 3% of any one fund and 3% of Tier 1 capital in aggregate. A
  desk that holds positions beyond customer demand, or a “hedge” not tied to identifiable risks, falls back into
  prohibited proprietary trading.
  Compliance risk management and the cases (ORR-14 b)
  • Fraud or error. An intentional compliance breach is a form of internal fraud (unauthorised activity), so
  most of the internal fraud control framework applies. An unintentional breach is usually human error or
  faulty policies; controls against fraud also prevent errors, except deterrent controls. The difference between
  error and fraud is intention.
  • Drivers and controls. General internal-fraud drivers: poor engagement, dissatisfaction or stress, insufficient
  resources, weak ethics culture. Specific drivers: information asymmetry, trader conflicts, volatility spikes
  that raise transaction volumes (more errors, easier to hide abnormal trades or insider trading). Controls:
  monitoring and supervision of employees and trades, strong middle and back office, policies and procedures,
  training, ethics culture, and consistent onboarding, screening and training of employees and third parties.
  • Fines and UBS. US fines for investor protection violations: nearly USD 82 billion in 2000 to 2022 over 6,612
  penalty records. Largest: UBS, USD 11.15 billion (July 2008): buy back USD 11 billion of securities plus
  USD 150 million of penalties for misrepresenting auction rate securities as safe, cash-equivalent products
  when they faced increasing liquidity risk.
  • JP Morgan spoofing. Spoofing: a false impression of demand by rapidly entering and cancelling orders.
  CFTC fine of USD 920 million (September 2020) for spoofing in precious metals and US Treasury markets,
  over at least eight years and hundreds of thousands of spoof orders. Lesson: practices once accepted as
  normal became violations as investor protection regulation evolved.
  • Deutsche Bank Securities (S2). FINRA fine of USD 2 million: from 2014 to 2018 customer market orders
  were first routed through its smart order router’s “SuperX ping” unless customers opted out, causing execution
  delays and lower fill rates; the firm did not change the arrangement or test price improvement from routing
  directly to exchanges, and disclosed trading rebates only vaguely, with no sign they were passed to customers.
  Settled without admitting or denying. Regulators set punitive fines that more than offset the benefits, as a
  deterrent and a signal to peers.

  Likely distractors
  • “The Volcker Rule bars banks from all trading in securities for their own account, including market making for
  clients and hedging of their own positions.”
  True version: it targets speculative proprietary trading for profit and limits hedge fund and private equity in-
  vestments; market making within reasonably expected near-term customer demand and risk-mitigating hedging
  are permitted (exemptions outside-source). Scope/condition error.
  • “A desk qualifies for the market-making exemption whenever it quotes two-way prices, whatever the size of the
  inventory it holds.”
  True version: the exemption depends on inventory being designed not to exceed reasonably expected near-term
  demand of customers; inventory beyond that is proprietary trading (outside-source). Scope/condition error:
  drops the condition.
  • “MiFID II is an EU regulation that applies directly and uniformly in every member state, so no country may set
  stricter rules.”
  True version: MiFID II is a directive (MiFIR is the regulation); directives are transposed nationally and each
  country may be more stringent, but not less. Sibling swap (directive versus regulation).
  • “Best execution is satisfied by routing every order to the venue that pays the broker the largest rebate, provided
  the rebate is mentioned in the firm’s reports.”
  True version: best execution means seeking the most favourable terms reasonably available for the customer
  (price improvement, speed of execution); Deutsche Bank Securities was fined for indirect routing, untested price
  improvement and vague rebate disclosure. Polarity flip (firm’s benefit for client’s benefit).
  • “An unintentional compliance breach caused by a faulty policy is classified as internal fraud, since it is an
  unauthorised activity.”
  True version: only an intentional breach is a form of internal fraud; unintentional breaches stem from human
  error or faulty policies. Sibling swap (error versus fraud).
  • “Dodd-Frank placed consumer finance oversight in a new bureau inside the SEC, and left OTC derivatives
  trading to be regulated by the Federal Reserve.”
  True version: the CFPB is an independent regulator; regulation of OTC derivatives trading is delegated to the
  SEC and the CFTC, with mandatory use of clearinghouses. Role misassignment.
