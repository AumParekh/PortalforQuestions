  Revision notes
  The specifics first: drivers, disintermediation, opacity, stability (CI-3, IM-10)
  • Growth drivers (S1, CI-3 b). Four: (1) low policy rates squeeze bank margins (in the reading, floating-rate
  loan values fall faster than fixed-rate deposits), while PCFs are floating on both sides and avoid the squeeze;
  (2) low banking-sector efficiency, measured by the IMF’s financial institutions (FI) index (banks reluctant to
  lend to SMEs lacking collateral; PCFs use targeted covenants instead); (3) higher bank regulatory stringency;
  (4) rising corporate leverage.
  • BIS regression (S1). 45 countries, 2010 to 2019, dependent variable new private credit origination. FI
  index: coefficient −1.679 to −1.906, a one-standard-deviation decline gives about +33% (largest). Policy
  rate: −0.03, one s.d. decline gives about +12% (second). Regulatory stringency: +0.463 to +0.667, one
  s.d. increase gives about +2% to 4%. Corporate leverage: +0.001, under 1% (weakest). Coefficient size is
  not the ranking: rank by economic effect.
  • Bank disintermediation (S2, IM-10 b). Growth after 2007 to 2009: low rates and stricter rules for banks
  and insurers (insurers entered as their capital charges were lighter than banks’); banks abandoned loans to
  riskier mid-sized firms. About USD 2.1 trillion in loans and undeployed capital in 2023, not much less than
  global high yield; mostly North America. Lenders now reach larger borrowers that used syndicated loans or
  bonds (tailoring, less disclosure, lower cost than a public issue).
  • Private credit vs bank loans (S2, CI-3 c). PCF loans: flexible terms, tailored covenants, faster execution,
  for borrowers with limited tangible collateral, high leverage or low transparency. Cost of capital (US banks
  vs BDCs): BDC cost of equity has stayed below banks’ since the GFC; BDC cost of debt has been marginally
  above banks’ (gap widens in tightening). Banks’ funding advantage narrowed by about 250 bp in 2010 to
  2019: bank CoE rose (market risk, stricter regulation) and BDCs levered up, replacing equity with cheaper
  debt (only a modest rise in their CoE). The two contributed roughly equally; CoD played almost no role.
  Rising policy rates could reverse it.
  • Valuation opacity (S3, IM-10 c). Few comparables, infrequent valuations, no secondary market; no ac-
  counting standard or regulation mandates a method. Prices are less sensitive to shocks than high-yield bonds
  and leveraged loans but carry extra valuation discounts (larger in downturns); NAV can differ from price
  for several quarters. Stale marks can overstate value and returns, inflate manager fees, defer and then bunch
  losses. More frequent valuation is not automatically better (estimation error, short-termism).
  • Financial-stability concerns (S4, IM-10 c). Judged moderate today (reasonable duration matching, rela-
  tively low leverage, low liquidity and interconnectedness risk) but untested by a serious downturn. Credit:
  floating-rate, leveraged borrowers; relief by adding unpaid interest to debt; yet losses historically lower than

  high-yield bonds and similar to leveraged loans (PE sponsors, collateral). Liquidity: semiliquid evergreen
  funds, gates and suspensions, credit-line drawdowns pushed to investors via capital calls. Leverage: layers
  at investor, fund (SPVs, collateralized fund obligations) and borrower level; BDCs regulated with caps but
  rising leverage; most closed-end funds unlevered. Interconnectedness: PE, banks (under 1% of assets, se-
  cured), pensions and insurers (about 3% of AUM); PE-influenced life insurers have weaker solvency ratios.
  Conduct: unsophisticated investors. Underwriting: competition weakens standards and covenants.
  • Policy fixes (S4, IM-10 d). Cross-sectoral and cross-border supervision; US and EU focus on conflicts, con-
  duct, valuation, disclosure. Credit: bank-like monitoring by funds (insurers and pensions have no require-
  ment to monitor loan creditworthiness, so rely on internal valuations and agencies). Liquidity: closed-end
  only, less frequent redemptions, longer notice and settlement, liquidity tools and stress tests, clear disclosure.
  Leverage: banks require PCFs to show stress tests (funding constraints, write-downs, credit-line draws); more
  reporting; caps if needed. Valuation: oversight of methods, more frequent external valuations where signif-
  icant or error-prone, audits. Interconnectedness: data sharing, harmonisation against regulatory arbitrage.
  Conduct: disclosure of conflicts; more frequent redemptions can raise conduct risk (valuations manipulated
  to deter redemptions).
  Rest of IM-10 and CI-3
  • Characteristics (IM-10 a). Nonbank corporate credit from bilateral agreements, excluding public securities
  and commercial banks; historically closed-end funds lending to middle-market firms (revenues USD 100
  million to 1 billion). Costlier than bank loans, more customisation (deferred interest) and covenants, better
  relationship management. Investors: pension funds, insurers, SWFs, family offices via closed-end funds
  (over 80% of the market), BDCs, CLOs. Borrowers: small and mid-sized firms (IT, healthcare), floating
  rate, riskier than high-yield or leveraged-loan issuers, usually PE-backed. Broadly syndicated loan: several
  lenders, one borrower; leveraged loan: syndicated loan to a highly leveraged, non-investment-grade firm.
  • Returns (IM-10 b). Since 2020, private equity has significantly outperformed private credit; private credit is
  similar to natural resources, somewhat better than real estate, well ahead of the S&P 500 and venture capital.
  For about 10 years after the GFC, private credit beat PE and real estate. Growth about 20% a year in the US
  and Asia, 17% in Europe; share of corporate credit about 7%, 1.6%, 0.2%.
  • PCF structure (CI-3 a). Loans held to maturity, closed-end (capital committed five to eight years), unlisted,
  so low liquidity and maturity transformation risk. BDCs are about one-fifth of the US market, often listed with
  open-end features for retail; a growing retail base could bring bank-like asset-liability mismatch. Strategies:
  mainly direct lending (floating rate, extensive covenants), also mezzanine, asset-based, distressed. AUM
  about USD 0.2 trillion to 2.5 trillion; US nearly 90% of originations. Individual PCFs are highly concentrated
  by industry (HHI), far less diversified than banks: specialised screening, but sector risk.
  Private markets investing (IM-11)
  • Motivations (IM-11 a). Six: performance and diversification; control and value creation; broader universe
  (over 300 million private firms vs about 52,000 listed); absolute returns; wider geographic reach (less home
  bias); specialised structures (funds of funds, co-investments). Asset types: PE, private debt, private real
  assets; PIPEs.
  • Structures (S6, IM-11 b). Direct investment: high involvement and control, low diversification, low cost,
  concentration. Private funds: most common (closed-end LP), GP decides, moderate to high diversification.
  Funds of funds: very low involvement, very high diversification, extra fee layer, closest to passive, entry point
  for inexperienced investors. Managed accounts: discretionary or advisory, customised, large commitment.
  SPVs: one asset or deal, often real assets. Securitization: tranches, more liquid than funds, no control.
  • Performance metrics (S5, IM-11 c). IRR: most used, time value, but not risk adjusted, timing sensitive
  (credit lines delaying calls inflate it), assumes reinvestment at the IRR; MIRR mitigates. MOIC: TVPI =
  DPI + RVPI; absolute, size independent, less manipulable than IRR, but ignores time and cannot be annu-
  alised. DPI uses realised cash only, so it is the least manipulable; TVPI and RVPI depend on NAV. PME:
  discount all fund cash flows at index returns; > 1 means outperformance; benchmark sensitive, best for fully
  realised funds; Cambridge Associates mPME. All three measure deployed, not committed, capital (cash drag;
  overcommitment, e.g. commit 110% expecting about 60% called).
  • Risks and measures (IM-11 d). Systematic, illiquidity, credit (recovery and credit-event), settlement, infor-
  mation asymmetry, valuation (stale pricing, denominator effect, IPEV guidelines), SAA, TAA (procyclical;
  vintage diversification over 5 to 7 years), agency, operational, selection (wide top-5% vs bottom-5% disper-
  sion), reporting. Measures: dispersion of pooled TVPI (macro and selection), DPI vs TVPI gap (reporting),
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A28 Private Credit, Private Markets and Their Drivers
  weights vs market-neutral (SAA), VaR and stress tests, vintage-year analysis, due diligence reviews, default
  and loss rates.
  • Co-investing (S6, IM-11 e). Investing directly in a deal alongside a fund, usually by GP invitation. Pros:
  reduced or no management fee and carry (still share transaction costs), deal-level control, higher net returns,
  learning and access. Cons: adverse selection (procyclical flow, deals outside the GP’s core), speed, resource
  needs, concentration, fewer legal protections and a passive role.
  • Intermediated vehicles (S6, IM-11 f). Benefits: expertise, risk reduction through diversification, deal flow,
  cost sharing. Challenges: high fees (management fee plus carry create a hurdle), principal-agent risk, low
  transparency, blind pool risk (capital committed before assets are known; the GP decides), illiquidity (sales
  at a discount to NAV), due diligence demands, conflicts (equity and debt of the same company; co-investment
  allocation).
  • See also IM-13 Distress Symptoms and Remedies (Ippolito Ch.20; Book 5 Reading 95), the workout side
  of private credit. Financial indicators (covenant breaches, late statements, creative accounting, earnings
  enhancements such as sale-leasebacks) vs nonfinancial (complex structure, resignations, fiscal year-end or
  auditor change). Excessive leverage with weak operations is the worst case (cash flow 70% or more below
  the default point). Remedies: restructure, raise cash (asset sales, secured financing, sale-leaseback, equity
  sponsor), cut debt (open market repurchases under about 20% of bonds, direct purchases, cash tender, ex-
  change offers). Out-of-court restructurings are faster but face holdouts; Chapter 11 classes; legal risks: stay
  on claims, preference period, upstream guarantees.

  Exam traps
  • Wrong-input twin in multiples: dividing by committed capital instead of paid-in (called) capital; all three
  metrics measure deployed capital only.
  • Dropped term in PME: discounting only one leg (calls or distributions) at index returns; both legs must be
  index-adjusted. A TVPI above 1 or a high IRR does not mean the fund beat the market.
  • Sibling swap on drivers: the FI index, not the policy rate, has the largest effect (33% vs 12%); the policy rate’s
  coefficient (−0.03) is tiny yet its effect is second, so rank by economic effect, not coefficient size; leverage is
  weakest (under 1%).
  • Polarity flips on cost of capital and returns: BDC CoE is below banks’, BDC CoD marginally above; CoD
  played almost no role in the 250 bp erosion. Since 2020 PE beat private credit; private credit losses are below
  high yield, similar to leveraged loans.
  • Scope and absolute claims on policy: more frequent redemptions add liquidity and conduct risk; closed-
  end structures mitigate liquidity risk; fund-level leverage figures miss investor and borrower layers; regulatory
  arbitrage concentrates risk.
  • Role misassignment in structures: in a blind pool the GP decides which assets are bought; co-investors are
  passive with fewer protections; funds of funds are the most passive route with an extra fee layer.
