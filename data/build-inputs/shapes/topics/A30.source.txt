  Revision notes
  The specific first: liquidity strategies (LTR-5 b)
  • Asset liquidity management (asset conversion). The oldest approach: store liquidity in assets, mainly cash
  and marketable securities, and convert them into cash as needed. A liquid asset has (1) a ready market, (2) a
  reasonably stable price (a deep market), (3) reversibility (principal recoverable with little risk of loss). Op-
  tions: T-bills, Fed funds loans, purchase of securities under RP, correspondent deposits, municipals, agency
  securities, negotiable CDs, Eurocurrency loans. Used mainly by smaller institutions, which find it less risky
  than relying on borrowings.
  • Costs of asset conversion. Lost future earnings on assets sold (opportunity cost), transactions costs (com-
  missions), possible sale into a falling market, a weaker-looking balance sheet (the low-risk securities sold
  gave the impression of strength), and liquid assets carry the lowest returns. Sell the assets with the least profit
  potential first. Holding more liquid assets does not by itself make a firm liquid: the demands made against it
  matter too.
  • Borrowed liquidity (liability) management (purchased liquidity). Borrow immediately spendable funds
  to cover demands: Fed funds, RPs, jumbo (USD 100,000+) negotiable CDs, Eurocurrency deposits, FHLB
  advances, the central bank’s discount window. Used most extensively by the largest banks, which often
  borrow close to 100% of their liquidity needs.
  • Pros and cons of borrowing. Pros: borrow only when needed (no storehouse of low-yield assets); the
  volume and composition of the asset portfolio stay unchanged (selling assets for liability-driven demands
  shrinks the firm); a control lever, the offer rate. Cons: the most risky approach (but the highest expected
  return) because of rate volatility and how fast credit availability can change; uncertain cost, so uncertain
  earnings; a firm in trouble needs borrowed liquidity most exactly when lenders are least willing.
  • Balanced liquidity management. The compromise most firms use: some expected demands stored in assets
  (marketable securities), other anticipated needs backstopped by advance lines of credit; unexpected needs
  met from near-term borrowings; longer-term needs planned and parked in short- and medium-term assets.
  • Guidelines for liquidity managers. Track all departments that use or supply funds; know in advance the
  plans of the biggest credit and funds customers; keep priorities clear (liquidity management now plays a
  supporting role to lending and fee services); analyse needs continuously to avoid both excess (lost income)
  and deficit (hurried borrowing or loss sales).
  Net liquidity position and liquidity needs (LTR-5 a, c)
  • Net liquidity position (LTR-5 a). Lt = supplies (deposit inflows, revenues from nondeposit services, loan
  repayments, asset sales, money market borrowings) − demands (deposit withdrawals, acceptable loan re-
  quests, repayments of borrowings, other operating expenses, dividends). L < 0: deficit, decide when and
  where to raise funds; L > 0: surplus, invest it until needed. A firm is liquid only with access to imme-
  diately spendable funds at reasonable cost at precisely the time they are needed. Two truths: supply rarely

  equals demand, and there is a trade-off between liquidity and profitability. Costs: interest, transactions and
  opportunity costs; risks: interest rate and availability risk.
  • Why depositories face liquidity problems. Maturity mismatch; a high share of liabilities payable imme-
  diately (demand deposits, money market borrowings); sensitivity to interest rates (rising rates bring with-
  drawals and faster drawings on low-rate credit lines); the need to protect public confidence. Most liquidity
  problems arise from outside the firm, from customers.
  • Sources and uses of funds (LTR-5 c). Liquidity rises as deposits increase and loans decrease. Estimated
  deficit or surplus = ∆deposits −∆loans, forecast with trend, seasonal and cyclical components; reserves
  have a planned and a protective component.
  • Structure of funds (LTR-5 c). Hot money (e.g., 95% reserve), vulnerable funds (25 to 30% likely to leave),
  stable or core funds (e.g., 15% or less), each net of required legal reserves, plus 100% of (potential maximum
  loans − actual loans), because all good loans should be made. Book: USD 63.57 million; probabilistic
  version gives an expected requirement of +10 million.
  • Liquidity indicators and market signals (LTR-5 c). Cash position, liquid securities, net Fed funds and
  RP position, capacity ratio (negative indicator), pledged securities ratio (negative), hot money ratio, deposit
  brokerage index, core deposit ratio, deposit composition ratio (a decline means greater stability), loan com-
  mitments ratio (a rise means greater future needs); judge against peers and by changes. Market signals (the
  ultimate standard): public confidence, stock price, risk premiums on CDs and borrowings, loss sales of assets,
  meeting commitments to credit customers, central bank borrowings.
  The investment function (LTR-4 a, b, c)
  • Why banks hold investments. Loans are illiquid, risky, locally concentrated and taxable, so a fifth to a third
  of assets go into securities: the “crossroads account” between cash, loans and deposits. Functions: stabilise
  income, offset loan credit risk, geographic diversification, backup liquidity, reduce tax exposure, collateral
  for public deposits, hedge rate risk, flexibility, dress up the balance sheet.
  • Instruments (LTR-4 a). Money market (within one year, low risk, ready marketability): T-bills (safety, liq-
  uidity, collateral; low yields), short-term T-notes and bonds, agency securities (implied support, yields close
  to Treasuries; taxable), CDs (insured to at least USD 100,000; limited resale for longer CDs), Eurocurrency
  deposits (million-dollar units, not insured, slightly higher yields than domestic time deposits), bankers’ ac-
  ceptances (among the safest; eligible ones discountable at the Fed), commercial paper (poor resale market),
  short-term municipals (TANs, RANs; tax-exempt interest). Capital market (beyond one year, higher returns
  and capital gains): Treasury notes and bonds, municipal GO and revenue bonds (tax-exempt interest, limited
  resale), corporate notes and bonds (higher pretax yields, more attractive to insurers and pension funds than
  banks), asset-backed securities (pass-throughs, CMOs, mortgage-backed bonds; prepayment risk). Newer:
  structured notes, securitised assets, stripped securities (POs more rate sensitive, IOs less).
  • Factors affecting the choice of securities (LTR-4 b). Ten: expected rate of return (YTM or HPY), tax
  exposure, interest rate risk, credit or default risk (banks generally limited to investment grade, Baa/BBB
  or better), business risk (buy out-of-market securities to offset local loan risk), liquidity risk (Treasuries
  most liquid, then agencies, municipals, MBS; buying many liquid securities lowers average yield), call risk,
  prepayment risk (PSA model), inflation risk (TIPS), pledging requirements. Tax: after-tax yield = before-tax
  yield ×(1 − t); TEY = tax-exempt yield /(1 − t); bank-qualified municipals keep 80% interest deductibility;
  tax swapping and portfolio shifting.
  • Maturity strategies and tools (LTR-4 c). Ladder (spaced maturity: steady income, little expertise), front-
  end load (all short term: liquidity), back-end load (5 to 10 years: income, relies on money market borrowing
  for liquidity), barbell (short and long, little in the middle; often smaller firms), rate expectations (shift short if
  rates expected to rise, long if expected to fall; largest firms; biggest potential gains and losses). Yield curve:
  implicit rate forecast, over/underpriced securities, carry trade, riding the curve. Duration: percentage price
  change ≈ −D × ∆r/(1 + r); immunise by setting duration equal to the planned holding period. See also
  A16 (duration gap).
  • See also A27 for the nondeposit sources behind borrowed liquidity (Fed funds, RPs, CDs, Eurodollars, dis-
  count window, FHLB) and the factors in choosing among them.

  Exam traps
  • Strategy polarity flip: asset conversion is the less risky approach used mainly by smaller institutions; borrowed
  liquidity is the most risky (highest expected return) and used most by the largest banks.
  • Strategy sibling swap: leaving the asset portfolio’s size and composition unchanged is a benefit of borrowing;
  selling assets shrinks the firm. The offer rate is the control lever of liability management, not of asset conversion.
  • Absolute claim: more liquid assets do not guarantee liquidity; it depends on the demands made against the firm
  and on access at reasonable cost at the right time.
  • Tax direction: after-tax yield multiplies by (1 − t); TEY divides by (1 − t). A 4.55% tax-exempt municipal is
  worth 7.00% taxable for a 35% taxpayer, not 2.96%.
  • Investment-risk sibling swaps: call risk (issuer calls when rates fall) vs prepayment risk (specific to asset-
  backed securities); business risk (local economy, offset by out-of-market securities) vs credit risk (issuer default,
  investment grade only).
  • Sign and scope in liquidity estimates: Lt < 0 is a deficit; sources and uses surplus is ∆deposits −∆loans;
  structure of funds reserves apply net of legal reserves and 100% of potential loan growth is held; capacity and
  pledged securities ratios are negative indicators.
