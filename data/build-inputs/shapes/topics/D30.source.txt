  Revision notes
  Channels and policy (CI-4 a)
  • Geopolitical risk. Adverse effects on financial markets, trade relations and growth from major conflicts:
  wars, terrorism, and trade disputes involving tariffs or sanctions. Even the threat of rising tension can depress
  asset prices and redirect capital flows.
  • Two channels (S1). The economic channel: disruptions to trade, capital flows, supply chains and production
  capacity (Russia 2022: stock and sovereign bond prices fell, oil and grain rose; sovereign debt burdens can
  rise with military spending or lost revenue). The market sentiment channel: uncertainty, risk aversion and
  lower confidence, which can depress valuations and raise volatility even without direct conflict.
  • Policy responses. Exposed entities should assess, measure and manage the exposures. Countries, especially
  EMs, strengthen resilience through better regulatory frameworks, preserved fiscal and monetary flexibility
  and deeper financial markets. Financial institutions hold adequate capital and liquidity buffers; capital buffers
  alone are not sufficient.
  Who wins and who loses (CI-4 b, c)
  • Countries. Those directly involved or closely linked suffer most (physical damage, sanctions, trade and in-
  vestment disruption). Commodity exporters, especially energy producers, often benefit from higher prices;
  importers face currency depreciation and wider sovereign spreads (European importers after 2022).
  • Sectors and asset classes. Defense and energy gain; banking, retail and utilities decline. Oil and gold
  rise; equities fall, especially in EMs; safe-haven sovereign bonds appreciate (lower yields); currencies of
  commodity importers weaken. Event studies: stocks fall about 3% immediately after major events, up to
  9% for severe ones. Wars cause deeper and longer effects than sanctions or trade restrictions; the 1973 oil
  embargo and the 1990 invasion of Kuwait caused sustained declines.
  • Stock market responses. Average reactions are modest: about −0.3% after a country-specific shock; large
  regional shocks (at least two standard deviations above normal) up to 7x larger and more persistent; global
  shocks up to 20x, persisting up to six months. Domestic events: about −1% within a month, about −5% in
  EMs with military conflict. The VIX and tail risk spike.
  • Cross-border effects. Through trade and financial linkages: countries close to the conflict can see declines
  up to 2.5x larger; firms with revenue exposure, subsidiaries or major shareholders in affected countries fall
  too, more so in EMs.
  • Russia 2022. Defense and energy firms with revenue exposure to Russia and Ukraine fell, while the same
  sectors rose elsewhere; European markets fell more than other AEs (energy imports, links); firms with sub-
  sidiaries there lost about 2.5% within a week. Aggregate exposure to Russia has since declined, but risen for
  some Asia-Pacific and European firms.
  • US-China trade tensions (2018 to 2024). Chinese equities fell about 4% on average after tariff announce-
  ments (nearly 8% after the May 2019 US announcement); US firms about 1.3% on average, 1.6% to 1.8%
  after China’s August 2019 retaliation. The study covers no 2025 tariff events.

  Pricing geopolitical risk (CI-4 d, e)
  • Sovereign risk premiums (S4). Measured by sovereign CDS spreads, which widen after conflicts on fiscal
  and debt-sustainability worries: about 180 bps in EMs within a month vs about 40 bps in AEs. Long yields
  rise in vulnerable EMs; safe-haven yields (US, UK, Germany, Switzerland, Japan) fall, more so the bigger the
  event. Conflicts hitting key trading partners also raise premiums. Below-median reserve adequacy: about
  +100 bps; below-median institutional quality: about +120 bps. High debt-to-GDP, thin fiscal and reserve
  buffers and weak institutions all amplify. See also D16 for high public debt.
  • Sign of the risk premium (S3). Assets that lose value when geopolitical risk rises carry positive risk
  premiums; assets that gain carry low or negative premiums, accepted for their hedging value.
  • GPR betas. Sensitivity of equity returns to geopolitical shocks: energy and defense positive, consumer-
  oriented sectors negative. The energy-versus-other-sectors GPR beta premium moved from negative to pos-
  itive after the 2022 invasion: investors came to prefer hedging stocks.
  • Option markets. Premiums for downside protection (puts) and tail protection (deep out-of-the-money puts)
  rise sharply in major shocks, tail premiums the most. After the 2022 invasion, put premiums rose for
  Russian firms and for European firms with exposure; the 2018 to 2019 tariff weeks raised premiums in the
  US and China but did not spill over meaningfully elsewhere.
  Banks and nonbanks (CI-4 f)
  • Market, credit and liquidity risks rise (S2). Volatility triggers selloffs that erode asset and collateral values
  and tighten lending; capital outflows amplify volatility in less liquid markets; sanctions, capital controls and
  cyberattacks compound the stress. Lending suffers through weaker borrower credit quality: less cross-border
  lending, lower asset values and bank equity, higher rollover risk on foreign debt.
  • Exposures. As of 2024, markets with elevated geopolitical risk were about 8% of cross-border bank claims,
  10% of cross-border bank liabilities and 13% of equity fund assets. Banks have reduced direct exposure
  to Russia and Ukraine since the 2014 annexation of Crimea; investment funds cut direct exposure by about
  60% after 2022.
  • Fund flows. Bond funds with 10% exposure to conflict-affected countries saw a 2.3% drop in flows and
  a 1% fall in returns (equity funds less). Funds with 10% direct exposure to Russia and Ukraine lost about
  6% in a week and 8% over six months after the invasion; the 2018 to 2024 tariff announcements caused no
  meaningful fund losses. Redemption pressure and forced sales, not inflows, follow major events. Remedy:
  buffers, no concentrated exposures, better risk management, and regulation that accounts for geopolitical
  risk.

  Exam traps
  • Sibling swap with CI-5: the two channels are economic and market sentiment, not fiscal and monetary.
  • Role/sibling swap, importers vs exporters: exporters (energy producers) gain from price spikes; importers
  suffer depreciation and wider spreads.
  • Polarity flip on GPR betas and premiums: energy and defense have positive GPR betas (they gain), so they
  act as hedges with low or negative risk premiums; consumer sectors have negative GPR betas.
  • Scope error across cases: Russia 2022 hit funds and European options; US-China tariffs moved US and Chinese
  markets but did not spill over meaningfully and caused no meaningful fund losses.
  • Polarity flip on exposures: banks reduced direct exposure to Russia and Ukraine after 2014; funds cut theirs
