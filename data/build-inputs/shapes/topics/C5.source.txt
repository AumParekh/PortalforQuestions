  Revision notes
  • Why Basel exists (ORR-21 a): private support arrangements (the early Bank of England, clearinghouses)
  failed in panics large enough that only an entity able to print money could help; crises imposed large costs
  on the economy; customers complained of unfairness; and globalization interlinked banks. Herstatt’s failure
  in 1974 (currency settlement across time zones) led to the BCBS in 1974; IOSCO followed in 1983 and the
  IAIS in 1994.
  • Basel I motivations and ratio (ORR-21 a, b): a common G10 interest in banks holding enough equity, and
  a level playing field (avoid a race to the bottom on capital minimums). Published December 1987, agreed
  July 1988, fully implemented by end 1992; no legal standing of its own. A risk-based ratio was chosen over
  a leverage ratio, which would disadvantage low-risk portfolios. Tier 1/RWA ≥ 4%, total capital/RWA ≥ 8%
  (“Cooke” ratios); Tier 2 may be no more than half of total capital.
  • Basel I capital and weights (ORR-21 b): Tier 1 = common equity + disclosed reserves − goodwill; Tier 2 =
  general loan loss reserves (capped at 2%, later 1.25% of RWA), undisclosed reserves, hybrids. Risk weights
  0% (cash, OECD governments), 20% (OECD banks and public sector entities), 50% (uninsured residential
  mortgages), 100% (all other). Off-balance-sheet credit conversion factors 100%, 50%, 20% (commitments
  of one year or more), 0% (under one year). Example 21.1: book assets CAD 500 million, RWA CAD 250
  million.
  • 1995 Amendment: netting (ORR-21 c): original Basel I gave almost no credit for netting (master agree-
  ments untested in bankruptcy courts as of 1988), so offsetting swaps each drew an add-on and hedg-
  ing was disincentivized.

  • 1996 Amendment: market risk (ORR-21 c): standardized approach (five categories, assets assumed per-
  fectly correlated, specific and general market risk) versus the internal models approach (generally smaller
  charges). 10-day 99% VaR from at least one year of data, m ≥ 3 on the 60-day average; specific risk mul-

  • Basel II and the three pillars (ORR-21 d): reaction to Basel I being “not risk-based enough” (the 100%
  weight covered very safe and very risky loans) and to Nordic crises in well-capitalized systems. Four inno-
  vations: credit risk weights from banks’ internal risk measures; operational risk capital; Pillar 2 (supervisory
  review: ICAAP, capital above the minimum, early intervention) and Pillar 3 (disclosure); repeated Quanti-
  tative Impact Studies. Final text 2004.

  • Basel II credit: the standardised versus internal-models split (ORR-21 d, e): the standardised approach
  was intended for banks whose internal risk measures were insufficient to support IRB; Foundation IRB (bank
  supplies PD) and Advanced IRB (bank supplies PD, LGD, EAD). The United States did not implement the
  standardised approach: internationally active banks used IRB. IRB detail: see also A31.
  • Basel II operational risk (ORR-21 g): Basic Indicator Approach = 15% of average annual gross income
  over three years, ignoring negative years; Standardized Approach uses business-line multipliers of 12%, 15%
  or 18% (negatives offset within a year, negative-total years ignored); Example 21.4: SA USD 9.21 billion
  versus BIA USD 9.83 billion. AMA: internal one-year 99.9th percentile less expected losses, four data
  elements (internal, external, scenario, business environment and controls), insurance offset capped at 20%.
  AMA “has lost favor and is no longer permitted”.
  • Solvency II (ORR-21 h): EIOPA; one-year VaR at the 99.5th percentile; three pillars; breach of the MCR
  can stop new business or trigger resolution, breach of the SCR requires a restoration plan; internal models
  must be sound, calibrated to target criteria and used in actual decisions (use test); Tier 1, 2, 3 capital.
  • Basel 2.5 and Basel III (ORR-22 a, d, e): Basel 2.5 (by end 2011) added stressed VaR, the incremental risk
  charge and the comprehensive risk measure (D4). Basel III eliminated Tier 3, split Tier 1 into Core (CET1)
  and Additional Tier 1: CET1 ≥ 4.5%, Tier 1 ≥ 6%, total ≥ 8%; buffers: capital conservation 2.5%, G-SIB
  1 to 3.5%, countercyclical 0 to 2.5%; 3% leverage ratio (A32). CoCos: D25.
  • Why the December 2017 reforms (ORR-23 a): restore credibility in RWA calculations and comparability of
  capital ratios by (i) more robust, risk-sensitive standardised approaches for credit, CVA and operational risk;
  (ii) constraining internal models: IRB input floors and removal of internal models for CVA and operational
  risk; (iii) a leverage ratio buffer for G-SIBs; (iv) a more robust output floor.
  • Revised standardised approach for credit risk (ORR-23 b): mortgage weights vary with LTV (previously
  one flat weight); less mechanistic reliance on ratings (due diligence; SCRA grades A/B/C at 40%/75%/150%
  for banks); corporates 20% to 150% by rating, or 65% investment grade and 100% other where ratings are
  not permitted, SMEs 85%; subordinated debt 150%, equity 250% (400% speculative unlisted); positive CCF
  of 10% for unconditionally cancellable commitments.
  • CVA risk and leverage ratio (ORR-23 b): CVA losses exceeded default losses in some instances during the
  crisis; the revised framework adds the exposure component, removes the internal model and leaves SA-CVA
  (fair value sensitivities, consistent with the market risk framework) and BA-CVA (benchmarked to SA-CVA);
  banks with non-centrally cleared notional of EUR 100 billion or less may use a multiplier of the CCR charge.
  G-SIB leverage ratio buffer = 50% of the risk-weighted higher-loss absorbency requirement (2% gives 1%),
  met with Tier 1, with distribution constraints.
  • Output floor (ORR-23 c): the Basel II floor was 80% of Basel I requirements and was implemented in-
  consistently. The Basel III floor: RWA = max(RWAapproved approaches , 72.5% × RWAstandardised only ), phased
  in 50% (2022), 55%, 60%, 65%, 70%, 72.5% (1 January 2027); national discretion to cap the floor-driven
  increase at 25% of pre-floor RWA during phase-in; the IRB 1.06 scaling factor is removed. Floor mechanics:
  see also A31.
  • SMA: the new standardised approach for operational risk (ORR-24 a): BI = ILDC + SC + FC, each a
  three-year average with absolute values taken year by year first. BIC applies marginal coefficients 12% (up
  to EUR 1 billion), 15% (1 to 30 billion), 18% (above 30 billion); BI of EUR 35 billion gives BIC EUR 5.37
  billion. LC = 15× average annual losses over 10 years; ILM = ln(e − 1 + (LC/BIC)0.8 ), equal to 1 when
  LC = BIC, above 1 when LC > BIC. Bucket 1 banks: ILM = 1. ORC = BIC × ILM; RWA = 12.5× ORC.
  • SMA versus AMA (ORR-24 b): the AMA (internal models) and the three Basel II standardised approaches
  are replaced by one risk-sensitive standardised approach for all banks, because capital proved insufficient for
  some banks’ losses and misconduct and control failures were hard to model. The SMA assumes op risk rises
  at an increasing rate with income and that past losses predict future losses. Supervisors may set ILM = 1 for
  all banks, but loss disclosure still applies.
  • Loss data criteria (ORR-24 c): 10-year window (five years on transition; under five years, BIC only); EUR
  20,000 inclusion threshold (EUR 100,000 at discretion for buckets 2 and 3); record dates of occurrence,
  discovery and accounting, and build the data set on the accounting date; net losses of recoveries only once
  payment is received (receivables do not count, tax effects are not recoveries); include direct charges, external
  costs, provisions, pending and timing losses; exclude maintenance contracts, post-loss upgrades and insur-
  ance premiums; credit-related losses already in credit RWA are excluded, market-related losses are included;
  exclusions need supervisory approval, materiality (for example over 5% of average losses) and a minimum
  retention period.

  • Pre-SCAP versus post-SCAP (ORR-17 a, Table 17.2): pre-SCAP stress testing was mostly single-shock,
  product or business-unit level, static, not usually tied to capital adequacy, and losses only. Post-SCAP: broad
  macro scenario plus market stress, comprehensive and firm-wide, dynamic and path dependent, an explicit
  post-stress common equity threshold, and losses, revenues and costs.
  • Regimes in one line each (ORR-17 a): SCAP 2009, 19 BHCs, 4% T1C, ten required to raise USD 75 billion;
  CEBS 2010, 91 banks, seven raised EUR 3.5 billion, Irish banks passed then needed EUR 24 billion; EBA
  2011, 90 banks, 5% T1C, eight raised EUR 2.5 billion, Basel II weights; CCAR 2011 published only the
  macro scenario. Full comparison, disclosure and design: see also C9.

  Exam traps
  • Wrong accord (sibling swap): three pillars and op risk capital are Basel II; stressed VaR, IRC and CRM are
  Basel 2.5; CET1, buffers, leverage ratio, LCR and NSFR are Basel III; SMA, SA-CVA and the 72.5% floor are
  December 2017.
  • Two floors: the Basel II floor was 80% of Basel I requirements; the Basel III floor is 72.5% of RWA under the
  revised standardised approaches, phased in from 50% in 2022.
  • Direction of the 2017 reforms (polarity flip): internal models were removed for CVA and operational risk and
  constrained for IRB; a statement that they were extended is false.
  • Who uses the standardised approach (role swap): it was meant for banks whose internal measures were
  insufficient; US internationally active banks were required to use IRB.
  • ILM direction and LC scale: LC > BIC gives ILM > 1; LC is 15 times the average annual loss, not the
  10-year sum; bucket 1 banks have ILM = 1.
  • Regime facts: the Irish banks passed CEBS 2010; EBA 2011 used Basel II (rating-sensitive) weights while
  SCAP and CCAR used Basel I; CCAR 2011 published no bank-level results.
