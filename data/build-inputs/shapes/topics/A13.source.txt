  Revision notes
  • Data forms (MR-1 b): P/L, L/P (losses positive), arithmetic returns rt = (Pt + Dt − Pt−1 )/Pt−1 and
  geometric returns Rt = ln[(Pt + Dt )/Pt−1 ] = ln(1 + rt ). Geometric returns assume interim payments are
  continuously reinvested and keep the asset price non-negative; arithmetic returns “should not be used when
  we are concerned with long horizons”. Example 1.1: r = 0.05 ⇒ R = 0.0488; R = 0.05 ⇒ r = 0.0513.
  • Historical simulation VaR (MR-1 a): order the loss observations; with n observations and confidence level
  α, VaR is the (1−α)n+1th highest loss (1000 observations at 95%: 50 in the tail, VaR is the 51st highest loss;
  the book’s example gives 1.704). No distribution is assumed. See also A19 for weighted and bootstrapped
  HS.
  • Parametric approaches (MR-1 b) require us to explicitly specify the statistical distribution of the data; they
  fit a curve through the data and read VaR off it. Both the distribution and the data type must be chosen, and
  in practice VaR is computed from estimates m and s of the parameters (eq. 1.9).
  • Normal P/L, L/P and arithmetic-return VaR are equivalent formulations: with the same underlying as-
  sumptions they give the same VaR (1.645 for mean 0, s.d. 1 at 95%). Example 1.2: normal P/L with mean
  10, s.d. 20 gives 95% VaR 22.9 and 99% VaR 36.52. Example 1.3: arithmetic returns with mean 0.1, s.d.
  0.25 give 0.331 and 0.482.
  • Drawback of normal (arithmetic) VaR: it assigns a positive probability to the asset value Pt becoming
  negative.
  • Lognormal VaR assumes geometric returns are normal (dividends zero or reinvested), so Pt is lognormal:
  never negative, skewed with a long right-hand tail, and VaR is lognormally distributed. Lognormal VaR can
  never exceed the portfolio value. Standardised case (µR = 0, σR = 1): 95% VaR = 0.807. Example 1.4:
  µR = 0.05, σR = 0.20 gives 0.244 (95%) and 0.340 (99%), “quite close” to Example 1.3.
  • Normal versus lognormal (Example 1.5): annual mean 0.10 and volatility 0.40, 1-day 95%: normal 4.12%,
  lognormal 4.04%. They are “much the same” for short holding periods and realistic parameters; with the
  same inputs the lognormal VaR is the smaller.
  • Choosing the volatility input (S1): the VaR is a forecast for the coming holding period, so σR must describe
  the regime that governs that period. Dowd’s volatility-weighting logic (MR-2 c): if current volatility is 1.5% a
  day and was only 1% a month ago, “data a month old understate the changes we can expect to see tomorrow”;
  an unusually quiet data period gives VaR estimates “that are too low for the risks we are actually facing”.
  See also A19.
  • Expected shortfall (MR-1 c): the probability-weighted average of tail losses. Estimate it by slicing the tail
  into n equal-probability slices and averaging the n − 1 tail VaRs: with n = 10 the 95% normal ES estimate
  is 2.0250; the estimate rises with n and converges to the true 2.063 (versus 95% VaR 1.645).

  • Coherent risk measures (MR-1 d) are weighted averages of quantiles qp with a user-chosen risk-aversion
  function ϕ(p); ES gives every tail quantile weight 1/(1 − α) and all others 0. The exponential spectral
  measure (γ = 0.05) converges to about 1.854 but needs a much larger n; double n until the halving error
  falls below a tolerance. An existing VaR engine needs only small adjustments to produce coherent measures.
  p
  • Quantile standard error (MR-1 e): se(q) =  p(1 − p)/n / f (q). It falls as n rises and rises as we
  move further into the tail. Example 1.6 (95% VaR, n = 1000): bin width h = 0.1 gives the 90% inter-
  val [0.6081, 2.6819]; h = 0.2 narrows it to [1.1496, 2.1404].
  • Weaknesses of the quantile-standard-error approach: relies on asymptotic theory and large samples,
  gives wide confidence intervals, depends on the arbitrary bin width, and its symmetric intervals mislead for
  extreme quantiles whose true intervals are asymmetric. ES versus VaR: heavy tails raise the standard errors
  of ES estimators (Yamai and Yoshiba), but Acerbi finds the accuracies roughly comparable in empirically
  realistic ranges; always estimate the standard error alongside the risk measure.
  • QQ plot (MR-1 f): empirical quantiles plotted against a reference distribution’s quantiles. Linear means
  the reference fits (also for a linear transformation, i.e. different location and scale; intercept and slope then
  hint at location and scale). Steeper slopes in the tails mean the empirical tails are heavier than the reference
  (Student-t data against a normal reference). Outliers stand out.
  • The three core issues: which risk measure, which level (portfolio or position), which method (the “VaR
  trinity”: non-parametric, parametric, Monte Carlo simulation).

  Exam traps
  • Wrong formula twin: plugging the inputs into the normal formula −µ + σz instead of 1 − exp(µ − σz). It
  gives a slightly larger number, and GARP places it right next to the correct answer.
  • Stale volatility (scope/condition): when the stem reports a volatility that changed recently (16% rising to 18%
  six weeks ago), the VaR horizon is governed by the current regime. Using the old figure, or an average across
  both regimes, understates VaR.
  √  p  p
  • t scaling errors: scaling σ by h/T instead of h/T , or scaling µ by h/T instead of h/T .
  • Dropped mean: using 1 − exp(−σz) and ignoring µR , which overstates VaR when µR > 0.
  • Wrong critical value: 1.645 for a 99% question, 2.326 for a 95% question, or the two-tailed 2.576 for a one-
  tailed 99% VaR.
  • Sign inside the exponent: the critical return is µR −σR zα , a left-tail value. Writing ln(1+V ) in a reverse-solve
  flips the tail.
