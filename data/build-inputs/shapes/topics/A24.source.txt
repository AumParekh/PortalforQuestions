  Revision notes
  • The hedge fund edge (IM-8 a): the ability to short sell, use leverage, use derivatives and take concentrated
  positions when opportunities arise, where long-only mutual funds are restricted.
  • Equity-based and macro strategies (IM-8 a): equity long/short uses fundamental analysis, long and short
  related securities, an investment horizon per position, no single short above 2% to 3% of capital; returns are
  alpha on both legs, the interest rebate on short-sale proceeds and interest on the liquidity buffer; costs are
  margin financing, borrow fees, dividends on borrowed shares. Dedicated short specialises in overvaluation
  (aggressive accounting, legal issues) against the headwind of rising markets; risks are margin calls, squeezes,
  forced covering. Nonhedged equity is long only with full market exposure, using leverage or a bull call spread.
  Global macro makes large, leveraged, directional bets across equities, rates, FX and commodities and tends
  to trade more liquid instruments; emerging markets adds country-specific risk.
  • Arbitrage strategies (IM-8 b): fixed-income arbitrage bets on convergence (short the on-the-run 30-year
  Treasury, long the cheaper off-the-run 29 12 -year). Convertible arbitrage buys the convertible and shorts the
  stock, delta hedging continuously; value = investment value + conversion value + option value; returns
  from coupon, interest on short proceeds, monetizing volatility and any undervalued embedded call (example:
  hedged position gains USD 1.37 on a 5% fall against a USD 19.47 unhedged loss). Relative value: pairs
  trading, statistical arbitrage (including liquidity provision to large sellers), index futures against constituents.
  • Event-driven and merger arbitrage (IM-8 c): opportunities are strategic (hard catalysts, spin-offs, proxy
  contests, stub trades), financial, operational, legal/regulatory and technical; activists push boards for buy-
  backs, dividends, spin-offs, leverage. Merger arbitrage buys the target and, in a share-for-share deal, shorts
  the acquirer at the exchange ratio; it earns the spread, target dividends, short interest and any higher bid, but
  losses when a deal fails “can far exceed the gains”. The hedge fund edge is legal and antitrust expertise.
  • Distressed securities (IM-8 d): positions in firms entering, in, or emerging from bankruptcy: discounted
  junk debt many institutions cannot hold, bank loan sales, trade claims, capital-structure trades (long senior,
  short junior). Active investors sit on creditor committees, supervise the workout and litigate; passive investors
  stay out and choose simpler situations. Core risk: the restructuring takes longer than expected; also valuation,
  illiquidity, courts, DIP financing, new senior debt.
  • Risks to investors (IM-9 a): portfolio-level risks are benchmarking, survivorship bias and UBTI (debt-
  financed income taxable to tax-exempt investors; an offshore fund mitigates it); investment-level risks are
  counterparty, conflicts of interest, key person, strategy failure, style drift, regulatory change and correlation
  spikes. Five incremental risks: leverage (mostly collateralized), lighter regulation (investors must do their

  own due diligence), short selling (unlimited loss, squeezes such as GameStop 2021), limited transparency
  (secrecy, gates), and higher, fee-driven risk tolerance.
  • Systemic risk (IM-9 b): two channels: a failed strategy unwinding at fire-sale prices (contagion), and losses
  imposed on lenders and counterparties. LTCM 1998: monitor liquidity and leverage; unrelated assets become
  correlated in stress. Amaranth 2006: the Bank of England judged key intermediaries far more systemic than
  hedge funds. Leverage spiral: USD 100 of stock on a USD 75 loan is 3x; a 10% fall makes it 5x; selling USD
  30 restores 3x. Banks bear revenue, credit (margin loan) and counterparty risk; Archegos 2021 (a family
  office using total return swaps) cost Credit Suisse about USD 5 billion and other banks another 5 billion.
  Mitigants: conservative bank lending, lower leverage and more diversification, and reasonable regulation,
  since overly strict rules reduce the liquidity hedge funds supply as investors of last resort.
  • Regulation (IM-9 c): ’40 Act exemptions 3(c)(1) (fewer than 100 investors) and 3(c)(7) (qualified purchasers,
  over USD 5 million; ’34 Act registration at 500 investors); Reg D accredited investors (USD 2.5 million net
  worth or USD 250,000 income; Rule 506 allows 35 nonaccredited); ERISA avoided below 25% benefit-plan
  assets. Dodd-Frank Title IV removed the private adviser exemption: SEC registration, reports to the SEC
  and FSOC, Form PF (annual from USD 150 million, quarterly above USD 1.5 billion). EU AIFMD (2010;
  ESMA 2011 interprets, member states enforce; EUR 100 million reporting threshold; passport); Singapore
  MAS above USD 250 million; Hong Kong SFO licensing; China CSRC bans leverage and limits shorting to
  50 CSI 300 names. Regulators’ three concerns: investor protection, market integrity, financial stability.
  • Structure and tax (IM-9 d): domicile for tax and light oversight (Delaware; Cayman 55%, BVI 15%,
  Bermuda 10%). Open-ended limited partnership: the manager is the general partner, investors are lim-
  ited partners. Master-feeder: trading in the master; the offshore feeder serves non-US and US tax-exempt
  investors. Service providers: administrators, auditors, independent valuers (NAV), prime broker. 2 and 20:
  the management fee is ordinary income (Section 457 deferral ended 2008); the performance fee (carried
  interest) can be taxed as capital gains if assets are held at least a year.
  • Why funds fail (IM-12 a): bad or concentrated investment decisions; accounting, valuation or misappro-
  priation frauds; excessive leverage, tail events; a flood of withdrawals at the worst time; squeezes; weak
  insider-trading supervision; failure of a prime broker such as Lehman or MF Global; liquidity drying up. “If
  it looks too good to be true, it probably is”, but “it’s still about returns”: do not overweight infrastructure at
  the expense of return.
  • The due diligence process (IM-12 b, c): covers the investment process, risk management, the operational
  environment and the business model; quantitative (checklist) and qualitative (open-ended, culture). Themes:
  strategy and edge, concentration, gross and net exposure, stop losses, use of shorts and derivatives, capacity,
  equity ownership of the team, a track record that is comparable, audited, long enough and produced by the
  team still in place (Greenwich Roundtable), and background and reference checks (a basic report costs about
  USD 750; read Form ADV). Meet the actual decision makers.
  • Risk management criteria (IM-12 d): an independent risk function (dedicated risk managers or an indepen-
  dent risk service provider); the same risk system for traders and investor reporting; stress testing; valuation
  policy (who can override prices); leverage and liquidity against peers; tail risk (skewness, kurtosis); and
  whether the terms suit the strategy (a 2 and 20 long-only fund or a one-year lock-up on liquid long/short
  equity are red flags).
  • Operational, business model and fraud risk (IM-12 e, f, g): internal controls, compliance, counterparty
  diversification (Lehman, MF Global and Refco left funds recovering perhaps 30 to 40 percent of cash at
  the dealer); documents consistent across the OM, LPA and Form ADV; an unqualified audit opinion, no
  incentive fees in loss years, and general partner withdrawals as a red flag. Business model: red, yellow and
  green zones; break-even AUM and months of cash. FBI fraud indicators: affiliated broker-dealer execution,
  liquidity complaints, litigation, unusually strong performance, manager-marked illiquid assets, related-party
  valuation. The DDQ covers manager information, trading, compliance, AML, disaster recovery, key man,
  fund terms, service providers, risk controls, financial statements.
  • Madoff: history and claimed strategy (IM-14 a): BMIS began in 1960 as a brokerage; Rule 390; the
  Cincinnati Stock Exchange; payment for order flow; Madoff chaired the NASDAQ board. The advisory arm
  (late 1980s, same building) ran 23 discretionary accounts and about USD 17 billion, mostly through feeder
  funds, so end investors could only diligence the feeders. Split-strike conversion: long a basket correlated
  with the S&P 100, sell OTM S&P 100 calls, buy OTM S&P 100 puts; claimed to sit in cash most of the
  time, positions under 30 days. Reported 8% to 12% a year for about 17 years, no down year, over 95% of
  months positive, returns similar to the S&P 100 with far lower volatility. Collapse: on 10 December 2008
  he confessed to his family because redemptions outran new money; his sons reported him.

  • Madoff: red flags (IM-14 b): operational: no independent service providers (execution, custody, admin-
  istration in-house), broker-dealer and market maker for its own strategies, a three-person auditor (Friehling
  and Horowitz) that told the AICPA it had no audit clients, family in key roles, only brokerage commissions
  (no management or performance fee), secrecy (feeders barred from naming BMIS), late SEC registration
  (2006), paper trade confirmations. Investment: a black box with no negative months, style analysis show-
  ing exposures opposite to the stated strategy, illogical Form 13F filings, an impossible volume of S&P 100
  options and no identified OTC counterparties. Markopolos warned the SEC in 2005.
  • Survivorship and other biases (IM-17 c; IM-7): Ang’s three biases that overstate returns and understate risk
  of illiquid assets: survivorship bias (poorly performing funds stop reporting and fail; only observing the en-
  tire population removes it; at least 1% to 2% for mutual funds, above 4% comparing defunct and live funds),
  infrequent sampling (volatility, correlation and beta too low), and sample selection bias (returns observed
  only when asset values are high: alpha overstated, the fitted SML flatter, beta and volatility understated;
  Cochrane: venture capital alpha over 90% falls to −7% once corrected). Reporting bias: reporting is vol-
  untary, so funds stop when returns are low or never start if the track record is unattractive. BKM: limiting
  a sample to funds alive over the whole period creates survivorship bias; most mutual fund databases now
  include failed funds. Backfill (instant history) bias, where a fund joining a database adds its earlier good
  returns, is the remaining sibling in the map’s list (outside-source).
  Formulas
  Leverage spiral (Stowell, Figure 91.3). Debt-to-equity target k; after a price fall, equity E ′ = V ′ − L; sale needed
  to restore k: s = L − kE ′ . Book: L = 75, V ′ = 90, E ′ = 15, k = 3 ⇒ s = 75 − 45 = 30.
  Distressed capital-structure trade (Book 5 example). Buy a bond at 30 per 100, two 5% coupons and 35 recovery:
  gain 100 + 350 − 300 = 150 per USD 1,000 face; short 100 shares at 3 that go to zero: gain 300; total 450.
  Merger arbitrage, share-for-share (Book 5 example). Offer 2 acquirer shares per target share; target 95, acquirer
  50: buy 100 target, short 200 acquirer; profit at close 2 × 50 − 95 = 5 per target share.
  Exam traps
  • Legs reversed (polarity flip): convertible arbitrage is long the convertible, short the stock; merger arbitrage is
  long the target, short the acquirer; Madoff sold calls and bought puts.
  • Strategy label swapped (sibling swap): long convertible, short stock is convertible arbitrage, not fixed-income
  arbitrage; global macro trades more liquid instruments; activists and active distressed investors engage, passive
  ones do not.
  • Bias siblings: dropping dead funds is survivorship bias; recording returns only when values are high is sample
  selection bias; choosing whether to report at all is reporting bias. Selection bias makes beta and volatility look
  lower, not higher.
  • Role misassignment: the manager is the general partner; investors are limited partners; the offshore feeder
  houses US tax-exempt investors; Madoff confessed and his sons reported him (not the SEC).
  • Absolute claims: “strict regulation always lowers systemic risk”; “reporting bias only comes from funds that
  stop reporting”. Both are false.
  • Case cross-mapping: correlated assets in stress is LTCM; total return swaps and bank losses is Archegos; cash
  stuck at a failed prime broker is Lehman, MF Global, Refco; in-house custody, a tiny auditor and impossibly
  smooth returns is Madoff.
