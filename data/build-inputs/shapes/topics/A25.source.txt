  Revision notes
  • The specific: Vasicek expectation and half-life (MR-14 g). dr = k(θ − r)dt + σdw. The distance
  between the current rate and its goal decays exponentially at the mean-reverting rate, so E[rT ] = r0 e−kT +
  θ(1 − e−kT ): a weighted average of today’s rate and θ, with the weight on today’s rate decaying at speed
  k. Book example (r0 = 5.121%, θ = 15.339%, k = 0.025): distance 10.218% × e−0.25 = 7.9578%, so
  E[r10 ] = 7.3812%. Half-life (time to cover half the distance) = ln 2/k = 27.73 years; larger k, shorter
  half-life.
  • Risk-neutral drift and risk premium (MR-14 f; MR-16 b). If the true process reverts to r∞ and a constant
  risk premium λ enters as a drift, dr = k([r∞ + λ/k] − r)dt + σdw, so θ = r∞ + λ/k. Very many (an
  infinite number of) combinations of r∞ and λ give the same θ and the same prices: expectations cannot
  be distinguished from risk premium by observing security prices. Book: r∞ = 6.179%, λ = 0.229%,
  k = 0.025 give θ = 15.339%.

  • Effectiveness of Vasicek (MR-14 h). Par-rate volatilities decline with term (downward sloping everywhere,
  Figure 14.7), better than Model 1’s flat structure but overstating short-term volatility. A 10 bp rise in the
  short rate lifts the 30-year spot rate only about 7 bps: not a parallel-shift model. The 30-year convexity effect
  is about 75 bps versus 135 bps without mean reversion. Mean reversion measures the length of economic
  news: low k (long half-life) means long-lived news. Calibrated to the 2- and 10-year par rates, the model is
  probably not flexible enough for the range of term structures observed.
  • Vasicek uses (MR-16 b). Simple, with analytic formulae for expectations, variance, forwards and spots; one
  factor explains a large fraction of term structure variability, particularly at longer maturities. Reasonable to

  price, compare and hedge long-term bonds first callable after some intermediate number of years (match 10-
  to 30-year noncallables and the 10-year rate volatility), and for trading long bonds against a model 10-year
  rate. Forward rate volatility is σe−kt : the volatility term structure is downward sloping.
  • Vasicek weaknesses (MR-16 b). Not widely used because too simple: (1) not flexible enough to approximate
  the wide variety of observed term structures; (2) a second factor captures significantly more variability, and
  more than one factor is needed to hedge intermediate- and shorter-term bonds; (3) it cannot capture the term
  structure of volatility that rises quickly with term and then flattens or declines, so it cannot handle volatility
  products spread across the curve.
  • Gauss+ structure (MR-16 a). Cascade of three factors: the short rate r reverts to the medium-term factor
  m (business cycle and monetary policy); m reverts to the long-term factor l (long-term inflation and real
  rate expectations, driven by demographics and production technology); l reverts to the constant µ (long-term
  expectation of the short rate plus a risk premium). Expected speeds: αr > αm > αl .
  • No shock on r (MR-16 a). Equation 16.9 has no volatility term, mirroring a central bank that pegs the policy
  rate and moves the target gradually. This keeps short-rate volatility low, so Gauss+ can match the empirically
  observed hump-shaped term structure of volatility. “Gauss” means rates are normally distributed; “+” means
  three factors (state variables) but only two sources of risk, dW 1 and dW 2 .
  √
  • Correlation and risk premium (MR-16 a, c). The random terms in dm give it volatility σm dt and cor-
  relation ρ with dl (eqs. 16.13 to 16.15). Equations 16.9 to 16.12 are risk-neutral dynamics; the application
  assumes only the long-term factor earns a risk premium.
  • Factor effects (MR-16 a). Larger αr : changes in m and l reach the term structure faster. Larger αm : m
  converges to l faster and affects longer yields less. Smaller αl : the effect of l on all longer-term yields is
  more parallel. The short factor affects only the very short end; the medium factor drives the 2- to 3-year
  region (a “forward shadow rate” that can go deeply negative near the zero bound); l peaks at six to eight
  years and beyond 10 years is the sole driver of forward changes.
  • Estimation in stages (MR-16 d). (1) r = fed funds target each day (repo rates jump idiosyncratically). (2)
  Choose αr , αm , αl to match the coefficients of regressing yield changes on 2-year and 10-year yield changes
  (these depend only on mean reversion parameters). (3) Choose σm , σl , ρ to match the term structure of
  volatility. (4) Choose µ to minimise squared yield errors over the sample. Daily, the factors fit the fed funds
  rate and the one-year rates two and 10 years forward. Much easier than maximum likelihood. Table 16.1
  half-lives: about 8 months, 13 months and 42 years.
  • Trading with Gauss+ (MR-16 b). Popular for proprietary relative value and macro-style trading. Relative
  value: positions hedged against factor changes, e.g. receive a forward that is high to the model and converging
  (signal = 5-day minus 40-day moving average of fitting errors), pool trades to neutralise factor exposure, and
  pair nine- and five-year signals. Macro: views on the factors; with the assumption that expectations stop
  changing past some future date, long forwards split into expectations, risk premium and convexity (the 10-
  year risk premium often moves opposite to rates).
  • See also A7 for Models 1 to 3 and Ho-Lee (Model 3 with matching volatility, decay and drift reproduces the
  Vasicek terminal distribution but stays parallel-shift).

  Exam traps
  • θ versus r∞ (wrong-input twin): the risk-neutral process reverts to θ = r∞ + λ/k. Using the real-world
  long-run rate, or adding λ without dividing by k, understates the pricing expectation.
  • Weights in E[rT ]: e−kT multiplies r0 , (1 − e−kT ) multiplies θ. Swapping them leaves the rate too close to θ
  for short horizons and too far for long ones.
  √
  • Half-life and SD: half-life is ln 2/k, not 1/k. The terminal SD uses e−2kT and 2k; σ T ignores mean reversion
  and overstates it.
  • Shock on r in Gauss+: equation 16.9 has no dW term. Adding a Vasicek-style σdw to the short rate is the
  defining error (and the reason for the “+”).
  • Wrong speed or target (sibling swap): r uses αr and reverts to m; m uses αm and reverts to l; l uses αl and
  reverts to µ. A factor above its target drifts down: the sign is −α(factor − target).
  √
  • Shock scaling:p drifts are annual and need dt; a realised dW already contains dt. The medium factor loads ρ
  on dW 1 and 1 − ρ2 on dW 2 .
