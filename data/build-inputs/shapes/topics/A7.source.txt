  Revision notes
  • The specific: the Ho-Lee tree (MR-14 d). dr = λ(t) dt + σ dw: the drift may change from date to date (the
  book’s illustration:
  √  an annualised drift of −20 bps√over the first month and 20 bps over the second).
  √  Date 1:
  r0 + λ1 dt ± σ dt. Date 2: r0 + (λ1 + λ2 )dt + 2σ dt, r0 + (λ1 + λ2 )dt, r0 + (λ1 + λ2 )dt − 2σ dt. The√top
  node after two months is reached by two up moves, so the change from today is ∆r = (λ1 + λ2 )dt + 2σ dt.
  The tree recombines because σ is constant.
  • Calibration of Ho-Lee (MR-14 d). With dt = 1/12, set r0 equal to the one-month rate; find λ1 so the model
  produces the market two-month spot rate; then λ2 for the three-month spot rate; and so on until the tree ends.
  The free parameters match the prices of securities with fixed cash flows, so the rate curves match all the rates
  that are input into the model: Ho-Lee is arbitrage-free.
  • What Ho-Lee does not change. Just as adding a constant drift to Model 1 does not affect the shape of the
  term structure of volatility nor the parallel-shift characteristic, adding a time-dependent drift does not change
  these features either. The time-dependent drift over each period is still some combination of risk premium
  and expected changes in the short rate.
  • Model 1, no drift (MR-14 a, √  b). dr = σ dw, with σ the annual basis-point volatility and dw normal with
  mean 0 and standard deviation dt. Book example: r0 √  = 6.18%, σ = 1.13%, dw = 0.15 gives dr = 0.17%;
  drift is zero and the standard deviation of the rate is σ dt = 32.6 bps per month. Tree check: E[dr] = 0,
  V [dr] = σ 2 dt. Date-2 nodes 6.832%, 6.180%, 5.528%.
  • Effectiveness of Model 1 (MR-14 a). It is a normal (Gaussian) model; the curve slopes down only because
  of convexity (Table 14.1: −18.8 bps at 10 years, −135.3 bps at 30 years). The term structure of volatility
  is flat at 113 bps while the market’s is humped; the only factor is the short rate, so it is a model of parallel
  shifts. Matching the 10-year par rate leaves every other point off: not enough flexibility for the simplest term
  structure shapes.
  • Negative rates (MR-14 c). In a normal model  √  the probability of a negative short rate increases with the
  horizon (10-year standard deviation 1.13% 10 = 3.573%; a shock of only 1.73 standard deviations pushes
  6.18% below zero). Coupon bonds depend on the average path, so negative rates rarely disqualify a model;
  options asymmetrically sensitive to low rates (a 10-year call on a bond at a 0% yield) are badly mispriced.
  Remedies: change the distribution (lognormal, which may give unacceptable volatilities), or set negative
  rates in the tree to zero: shadow rates versus observed rates, a change localised around zero.
  • Model 2, constant drift (MR-14 a). dr = λ dt + σ dw. Book example r0 = 5.138%, λ = 0.229%,
  σ = 1.10%: drift 1.9 bps a month, standard deviation 31.75 bps a month; 10-year terminal mean 7.428%,

  standard deviation 347.9 bps. The drift raises the mean so negative rates are less likely, but the term structure
  of volatility, the convexity effect and the parallel-shift nature are unchanged. Calibrated to the 2- and 10-year
  par rates it fits much better than Model 1, but λ = 0.229% implies a Sharpe ratio of about 0.21 and the curve
  overshoots the 30-year rate by about 25 bps.
  • Equilibrium versus arbitrage-free (MR-14 e). Models 1 and 2 are equilibrium models: no effort is made
  to match the initial term structure. Ho-Lee is arbitrage-free. The choice depends on the purpose of building
  the model.
  • Uses of arbitrage-free models (MR-14 e). (1) Quoting securities that are not actively traded from more
  liquid ones, e.g. a swap to three years and four months from three- and four-year swaps: interpolation based
  on economic reasoning (expectations, risk premium and convexity) rather than an arbitrary functional form,
  but only as good as the volatility assumption. (2) Valuing and hedging derivatives for market making or
  proprietary trading, taking the underlying (e.g. the 10-year bond under a bond option) as fairly priced.
  • Fitting warnings (MR-14 e). First, a mediocre or bad model cannot be rescued by calibrating it to market
  prices (a parallel-shift model with a fitted drift is no better for an application needing non-parallel moves).
  Second, fitting assumes prices are fair, but supply and demand imbalances, taxes and liquidity distort them:
  a bank dumping seven-year swaps does not mean expected rates in seven years have risen. A fitted drift
  that changes dramatically from one year to the next is likely attributing non-interest-rate effects to the rate
  process.
  • Relative value needs equilibrium models (MR-14 e). Arbitrage-free models conclude by construction that
  all input bond or swap prices are fair relative to one another, so investors choosing among securities, market
  makers selecting hedges and traders hunting temporary mispricings must rely on equilibrium models. In
  practice the two are blended (a deterministic drift for a few years then a constant drift; or taking 2-, 5-, 10-
  and 30-year rates as given).
  • Model 3: time-dependent volatility (MR-15 a). dr = λ(t)dt + σ(t)dw (15.1); if σ(1) = 1.26% and
  σ(2) = 1.20%, the short rate’s volatility is 126 bps per year in one year and 120 bps in two. Model 3 is the
  special case dr = λ(t)dt + σe−αt dw (15.2): volatility starts at σ and declines exponentially to zero. Just
  as a time-dependent drift fits many bond or swap rates, a time-dependent volatility fits many option prices:
  caplets expiring on many dates. Popular with market makers in interest rate options.
  √
  • Standard deviation in Model 3 (MR-15 b). Each step’s perturbation is ±σ(t) dt; with σ = 126 bps
  and α = 0.025 the standard deviation of the terminal distribution rises rapidly with horizon at first, then
  more slowly (Figure 15.1). With the initial volatility and decay rate equal to the Vasicek volatility and mean
  reversion speed, the terminal standard deviations are identical; if the drift also matches the Vasicek average
  path, the terminal distributions are identical.
  • Model 3 is still a parallel-shift model (MR-15 a, c). As for any model without mean reversion, its term
  structure of volatility is flat, at levels that change over time, but always flat. Efficacy: fine for interpolating
  unobservable option prices from known ones. For valuing and hedging, mean reversion is preferred because
  (1) time-dependent volatility relies on the difficult argument that the market forecasts short-term volatility
  in the distant future (fix: time-dependent near term, then constant) and (2) the downward-sloping factor
  structure and volatility term structure of mean reversion capture rate behaviour better than parallel shifts. If
  Vasicek is not good enough for a purpose, a parallel-shift model calibrated to caplets is unlikely to be better.
  • See also A25 for Vasicek (MR-14 f, g, h) and Gauss+; D1 for CIR, lognormal, Salomon Brothers and Black-
  Karasinski models (MR-15 d, e, f).
