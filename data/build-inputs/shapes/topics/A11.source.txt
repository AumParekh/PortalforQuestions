  Revision notes
  • The specific: two cost-of-liquidation constructions
  P (LTR-1 a). Hull’s normal market cost charges half
  the current proportional spread on each position,  si αi /2 (eq. 1.1): this is the map’s “constant-spread”
  approach. The stressed market cost replaces si with µi + λσi , the mean plus λ standard deviations of the
  proportional spread (eq. 1.2): the map’s “exogenous-spread” approach (labels outside-source). Hull’s Ex-
  ample 1.2: stressed cost USD 36.58 million, almost five times the normal-market USD 7.5 million.
  • The confidence parameter λ. λ = 2.326 for a 99% spread if spreads are normal. Spreads are not normally
  distributed in practice, so λ can be set from the empirical distribution (e.g. 3.6 if the 99th percentile is 3.6
  standard deviations above the mean).
  • Two LVaR definitions (Hull eq. 1.3 and its stressed version, from Bangia et al.). LVaR = regular VaR + cost
  of unwinding in a normal market, or regular VaR + cost of unwinding in a stressed market. VaR captures
  mark-to-market price moves; the cost of liquidation assumes market prices do not change.
  • Mid-market price is the fair price. Sells are done at the bid, buys at the offer, so each trade costs half the
  spread, sα/2. Spreads range from 0.05% to 5% or even 10% of mid. Above the market maker’s size limit,
  the bid falls and the offer rises with trade size (in retail markets the pattern can be the opposite).
  • Perfect spread correlation is not over-conservative. Equation (1.2) assumes spreads on all instruments are
  perfectly correlated; “this may seem overly conservative, but it is not”: when liquidity is tight, spreads widen
  together.
  • Diversification and position size. Diversification reduces market risk but does not necessarily reduce liq-
  uidity trading risk. Because si rises with position size, many small positions carry less liquidity risk than a
  few large ones; position-size limits reduce liquidity trading risk.
  • Liquidation horizon. Both cost measures are decreasing functions of the time allowed for liquidation.
  Fast unwind: large spreads, small risk of adverse mid-market moves. Slow unwind: smaller spreads, larger
  market-move risk.
  • Optimal unwinding (Almgren and Chriss). Choose daily trades qi to minimise a liquidity-adjusted VaR
  of the unwind. Over n days trade more than 1/n on day 1, since the longer any part is held the greater the
  risk of adverse moves. Hull’s Example 1.3 at 95%: 48.9, 30.0, 14.1, 5.1, 1.9 million units; at 50% (expected
  cost only) equal amounts of 20 million a day.
  • Other market liquidity measures and transparency. Trading volume per day, price impact of a trade, and
  the Amihud measure (average of absolute daily return / daily dollar volume); expected return rises as liquidity
  falls. Opaque products (ABS CDOs after 2007) lose liquidity quickly: Merrill Lynch sold USD 30.6 billion
  of former AAA tranches for 22 cents on the dollar. Predatory trading (London Whale, LTCM) makes large
  exits costlier.

  • Liquidity black holes (LTR-1 d). Liquidity dries up because everyone wants to sell (a “crowded exit”).
  Negative feedback traders (buy when prices fall) stabilise; positive feedback traders destabilise. Causes of
  positive feedback trading: trend trading, stop-loss rules, dynamic hedging of short option positions, creating
  options synthetically (1987 portfolio insurance), margin calls, predatory trading, LTCM’s forced unwind.
  Basel III LCR and NSFR (LTR-1 c): see A32.
  • Malz’s two liquidity risks (LTR-2 e). Transactions liquidity risk: moving the price adversely in the act of
  trading. Funding liquidity risk (balance sheet risk): creditors withdraw credit or change its terms. Hull’s
  labels for the same pair: liquidity trading risk and liquidity funding risk.
  • Measuring funding liquidity risk (LTR-2 e). Part of bank ALM: track and forecast cash and funding sources
  against cash needs, keep ratios of ready cash and marketable securities. Goldman Sachs’ “Global Core Ex-
  cess” liquidity buffer (about 20% of the balance sheet) pre-funds crisis needs. Hedge fund liquidity sources:
  cash (broker balances carry broker risk; money funds can break the buck), unpledged assets (limited by price
  volatility and collateral eligibility, and subject to transactions liquidity risk), and unused borrowing capacity
  (revocable through haircuts).
  • Market liquidity vocabulary (Malz). Tightness (round-trip cost: bid-ask spread and commissions), depth
  (order size that moves the market), resiliency (how long a lumpy order keeps price from equilibrium). Illiq-
  uidity shows up as bid-ask spread, adverse price impact and slippage. Quantitative transactions-liquidity
  measures are less widely used than funding-liquidity measures, partly because they are not in the regulatory
  framework.
  • Transaction cost liquidity risk (LTR-2 f). Daily changes in the relative spread are normal with zero mean
  and constant variance σs2 ; s̄ is set to the latest observed relative spread, P to the latest midprice. The expected
  transactions cost is the half spread; 21 (s̄ + 2.33σs ) is the 99% spread risk factor, the worst-case bid-ask cost
  of changing a position.
  • Adverse price impact: multi-day LVaR (LTR-2 f). Estimate the days T for orderly liquidation (“time to
  escape”, usually position size vs daily volume). Liquidating in equal parts means holding 1, T T−1 , . . . , T1 of
  √  √
  the position, so T overstates: the LVaR lies between the one-day VaR and one-day VaR × T . T = 5 adds
  48%; T = 10 roughly doubles the overnight VaR.

  Exam traps
  • Constant vs exogenous spread (sibling swap): the normal-market cost uses the current quoted spread; the
  stressed cost uses the spread’s mean and standard deviation. Plugging the current quote into µ + λσ, or adding
  λσ to a normal-market definition, is the reported trap. Read which LVaR definition the stem gives.
  • Full spread instead of half: the cost is half the spread times mid value (Hull), and Malz’s 21 applies to both s̄
  and 2.33σs . Halving only s̄, or halving twice, are the twin slips.
  • λ on the wrong term: µ + λσ, not λ(µ + σ).
  √  p
  • √T trap (scope error): the position shrinks each day, so the multi-day factor is (1 + T )(1 + 2T )/(6T ), not
  T ; T comes from position size vs the volume the firm will actually trade per day.
  • Intermediate results: the cost of liquidation is not the LVaR; Malz’s expected cost 12 s̄P is not the 99% cost;
  the adjustment increment is not the adjusted VaR.
  • Polarity flips in theory: longer liquidation horizon lowers spread cost but raises market-move risk; perfect
  spread correlation is not overly conservative; diversification does not necessarily reduce liquidity trading risk.
