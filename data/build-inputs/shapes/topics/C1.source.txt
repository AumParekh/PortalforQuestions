  Revision notes
  • DV01-neutral hedge and its drawback (MR-11 a, S3, S4): the trader sells face = face hedged × DV01 ratio
  (book: USD 100 million × 0.2124/0.1910 = USD 111.2 million of the 30-year Treasury against 40-year
  JNJ bonds). The hedge “assumes that the yields of the JNJ and Treasury bonds move up or down in parallel”.
  Because the corporate bond trades at a changing spread and 40- and 30-year rates are not perfectly correlated,
  the parallel-shift assumption is questionable.
  • Regression model (MR-11 b): ∆ytJNJ = α + β ∆yt30 + εt . Least squares picks α̂, β̂ to minimise the sum of
  squared residuals; squaring penalises large errors heavily and stops positive and negative errors offsetting.
  Assumptions: the model is a true description, errors are identically distributed, independent of each other
  and uncorrelated with the independent variable; then estimates are linear, unbiased, consistent and efficient.
  • Reading the output (MR-11 c): book β̂ = 0.842 (std error 0.051), α̂ = 0.060 (std error 0.223), R2 = 77.5%,
  standard error 2.00 bp. The 95% interval for α contains zero, so α = 0 cannot be rejected; the interval for
  β, (0.740, 0.944), excludes one, so parallel shifts are rejected. In a one-variable regression R2 is the squared
  correlation (88.0%). The intercept is economically odd if nonzero and does not enter the hedge (S1).

  • Risk weight (MR-11 c): the DV01 of the hedge as a fraction of the DV01 of the hedged bonds equals β̂
  (84.2%). The risk weight of a DV01-neutral hedge is always 100%.
  • Why the regression hedge is better (MR-11 b, S4): it minimises the variance of the hedged P&L
  (Appendix A6.1), whether or not yields move exactly by the regression line. Hedged P&L volatility
  = |F JNJ DV01JNJ /100| × σε : book USD 212,400 × 2 bp = USD 424,800 per day. Whether that is ac-
  ceptable depends on how much and how fast the trade makes money.
  • Estimation window and stability: too short a window gives unreliable estimates, too long includes less
  relevant data; an earlier episode (for example a past Fed tightening) may be more relevant than the recent
  past. Results are in-sample; poor out-of-sample performance questions the stability of the coefficients (book
  out-of-sample standard error 1.5 bp versus 2.0 in-sample).
  • Two-variable regression hedge (MR-11 e): 20-year yield on 10- and 30-year yields (β̂ 10 = 0.465, β̂ 30 =
  0.669, R2 = 94.7%, standard error 1.15 bp). The butterfly buys 20s and sells 10s and 30s: both shorts
  defend against general rate rises, the 10-year short against flattening, the 30-year short against steepening.
  Face amounts F 10 = −F 20 (DV0120 /DV0110 )β̂ 10 and similarly for 30s: USD 79.44 million and USD 50.69

  • Level versus change regressions (MR-11 f): if the least-squares assumptions hold for one specification
  they hold for the other. If error independence fails, estimates are still unbiased and consistent but may not
  be efficient. Level errors tend to be persistent (serially correlated); change-on-change errors can also stay
  positive while a yield trends toward its model value. Alternative: a level model with error dynamics εt =
  ρεt−1 + νt , ρ < 1 (book: −4% yesterday, ρ = 75%, expected error −3% today).
  • Reverse regression (MR-11 g): regressing the Treasury on JNJ gives β̂ = 0.921 (regression standard error
  2.09 bp), a risk weight of 1/0.921 = 108.6% and USD 82.8 million JNJ against USD 93.6 million Treasuries.
  Neither hedge is “wrong”: choosing to hedge USD 100 million JNJ fixes the trade and its risk (about USD
  425,000 versus 374,000). The regression hedge minimises variance given the face amount of the bond being
  hedged; other objectives give other constructions.
  • Why PCA (MR-11 h): regression hedging is ad hoc (different bonds, windows and variable counts across
  traders are different models of the term structure). PCA gives one empirical description usable across a
  portfolio.
  • PCA properties (MR-11 h): (1) the sum of the PC variances equals the sum of the rate variances; (2) the PCs
  are uncorrelated with each other; (3) each PC has the maximum variance given the earlier ones. The first
  three PCs explain an overwhelming fraction (USD swaps: 99.84%; level over 90%, slope about 8%, short
  rate under 1%). Three PCs explain 74.54% of the one-year rate’s variation but over 99% for terms beyond
  three years.
  • Level, slope, short rate (MR-11 h): a one-standard-deviation level move is not particularly level from 1 to
  7 years in the 2020 to 2021 sample; slope moves short rates down and long rates up (or the reverse); the
  short-rate PC (also called curvature) adds explanatory power at the short end. A trader in 8- to 10-year swaps
  can defend a one-factor approach; 3- to 6-year or long-end traders may need two factors; the very short end
  may need three.
  • Hedging with PCA (MR-11 h, S5): shift the curve by each PC, reprice, compute a ‘01 per PC, and choose
  hedges that neutralise them. Book: receive 100 in 20-year swaps, pay 49.56 in 10s and 53.60 in 30s to
  neutralise level and slope; risk weights 27.1% and 74.1% sum to 101.2%. “Only under the assumption of
  parallel shifts do the risk weights always sum to 100%.” With two hedging securities only two PCs can be
  hedged: the residual short-rate exposure was 0.007 per 100, so the trader may skip a third (15-year) swap
  given transaction costs and the relative value view.
  • See also A7 and A25 for term structure models whose factor shapes (parallel versus mean-reverting) give
  the model-based counterpart of these empirical hedges.

  Exam traps
  • Dropped term (beta): the DV01 ratio alone gives the DV01-neutral amount (risk weight 100%), not the re-
  gression hedge. With β̂ < 1 the regression hedge sells fewer hedging bonds.
  • Red herring (alpha): the intercept is reported but does not enter the face amount. Adding it to β̂ is a named
  mistake.
  • Wrong DV01 in the P&L volatility: σP&L uses the DV01 of the position being hedged, not of the hedge leg.
  Using the hedge leg gives β̂ times the right answer.
  • Direction of the regression: the dependent variable is the yield of the bond being hedged. A reverse regression
  gives a different risk weight (108.6% versus 84.2% in the book), not the reciprocal of the same hedge.
  • PCA polarity and absolutes: PCs are uncorrelated; two hedging securities neutralise only two PCs; PCA risk
  weights need not sum to 100% (book 101.2%), only parallel-shift hedges do.
