  • Leverage-adjusted duration gap (eq. 18.21). DA − DL × L/A. Because assets exceed liabilities, liability
  values must change by slightly more than asset values to immunise. Book: 3.047−2.669×275/300 = +0.60
  year.
  • Polarity of the gap. Positive: liabilities change by less than assets, so rates up ⇒ net worth down, rates
  down ⇒ net worth up. Negative: rates up ⇒ net worth up, rates down ⇒ net worth down. Zero: immunised.
  Management expecting rising rates moves toward a negative gap (reduce DA , increase DL ); expecting falling
  rates, toward a positive gap.
  • Immunisation target. Zero leverage-adjusted gap ⇒ DL = DA × A/L (book: 3.047 × 300/275 = 3.324
  years; footnote: 3 × 100/92 = 3.261 years). Close the gap by changing DA , DL , or the relative amounts of
  A and L.
  • Duration (LTR-17 c). A value- and time-weighted measure of maturity: the average time needed to recover
  funds committed. ∆P /P ≈ −D ∆i/(1 + i); price risk is directly proportional to duration (book: D = 4,
  10% to 11% gives −3.64%). Portfolio duration is the market-value-weighted average (assets 3.047 years in
  Table 18.4). Convexity: price moves more at low rates than at high rates, rises with duration, falls as the
  coupon rises.
  • ALM strategies (LTR-17 a). Asset management (sources of funds taken as given by customers), liability
  management (price, the offer rate, as the control lever), and today’s funds management: coordinate control
  of volume, mix and return or cost on both sides. The ALCO meets regularly, sets funding and limits, and
  reports to the board. Two interest rate risks: price risk (rates rise, asset values fall) and reinvestment risk
  (rates fall, incoming funds earn less).
  • Two targets, two tools. Interest-sensitive gap management protects the net interest margin (NIM = net
  interest income / total earning assets; book: (4 − 2.6)/40 = 3.50%). Duration gap management protects net
  worth. Protecting NIM does not mean net worth is protected.
  • Repriceable items (LTR-17 b). Interest-sensitive assets: short-term securities and loans about to mature,
  variable-rate loans and securities. Interest-sensitive liabilities: money market borrowings (fed funds, RPs),
  short-term savings, money market deposits, floating-rate deposits. Nonrepriceable: cash and reserves, long-
  term fixed-rate loans and securities, buildings, demand deposits, long-term savings, equity.
  • Asset vs liability sensitive. Asset sensitive: positive dollar ISGAP, positive relative ISGAP, ISR > 1; NIM
  rises when rates rise. Liability sensitive: negative ISGAP, ISR < 1; NIM falls when rates rise and rises when
  rates fall. Book: ISA 150, ISL 200 gives ISGAP −50, relative ISGAP −0.33, ISR 0.75.

  • Cumulative gap (eq. 18.13). ∆NII ≈ ∆i × cumulative gap. Book: −600 million and +1 point gives −6
  million. The gap measured over 24 hours can have the opposite sign to the gap over a week, so the planning
  horizon is crucial.
  • Aggressive vs defensive GAP management. Aggressive: expect rising rates, go positive (increase ISA,
  decrease ISL); expect falling rates, go negative. Defensive: keep the gap as close to zero as possible to
  reduce the volatility of net interest income. Consistently correct rate forecasting is impossible.
  • Weighted gap and basis risk. A zero gap still leaves risk because asset and liability rates are not perfectly
  correlated (deposit rates lag loan rates): basis risk. The weighted interest-sensitive gap multiplies each item
  by its rate-sensitivity weight; the book’s gap flips from −23 to +75, and a 2 point rise changes NII by +1.50
  instead of −0.46.
  • ISGAP limitations (LTR-17 d). Repricing points are hard to identify (checkable and savings deposits); the
  choice of planning periods is arbitrary and items fall between the cracks; ISGAP ignores the effect of rate
  changes on net worth, and aggressive gapping raises earnings volatility.
  • Duration gap limitations (LTR-17 d). Hard to find assets and liabilities of matching duration; duration
  is less than maturity except for zero-coupon instruments, single-payment loans and T-bills; deposit cash
  flows are ill-defined; prepayments and defaults distort cash flows; assumes a linear price-rate relationship
  (convexity) and small, parallel yield-curve shifts; duration itself drifts as rates change. Research suggests
  duration balancing still works under moderate violations.
  • Fannie Mae reported a negative duration gap of about 14 months at the end of 2002 (target band ±6 months):
  with longer liabilities than assets, falling rates would have kept eroding net worth.

  Exam traps
  • Polarity of the leverage-adjusted gap (the map’s flag): positive DGAP loses net worth when rates rise; neg-
  ative DGAP loses net worth when rates fall. Read the sign only after the leverage adjustment: DA < DL can
  still give a positive DGAP when L/A is low enough.
  • Dropped or misplaced leverage: DA − DL instead of DA − DL (L/A); L/A applied to DA ; multiplying
  instead of dividing by L/A in the immunisation reverse-solve; setting DL = DA .
  • Dropped (1 + i): the rate change is divided by one plus the original rate.
  • ISGAP vs DGAP polarity (sibling swap): a positive ISGAP gains NII when rates rise; a positive DGAP loses
  net worth when rates rise.

  one bucket’s gap and not a shorter horizon.
