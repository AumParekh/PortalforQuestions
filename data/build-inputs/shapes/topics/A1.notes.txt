 Revision notes
 • Economic capital (CR-4 a, g): “an estimate of the overall capital reserve needed to guarantee the solvency
 of a bank for a given confidence level”, set consistent with the bank’s target credit rating. It rises with (i) the
 confidence level (a higher CM) and (ii) the riskiness of the assets (a higher UL). Holding less capital frees
 it to earn returns elsewhere: EC is how the bank balances risk and return. See also D3 for regulatory versus
 economic capital.
 • Three components of EL (CR-4 b): PD (also labelled expected default frequency, EDF), the exposure
 amount EA at the time of default (including all promised interest), and the loss rate LR (also called severity,
 LGD or LIED; LR = 1− recovery rate). PD is borrower-specific (linked to the rating); EA and LR reflect
 the facility (collateral, amortisation, limit versus utilisation).
 • Expected loss (CR-4 c): the actuarial-type long-run average loss “across a range of typical economic con-
 ditions”, not next year’s cyclical forecast. It is the foreseeable cost of doing business: covered by loan loss
 reserves (standard risk costs) and reimbursed through loan pricing. “By definition, EL does not itself con-
 stitute risk.”
 • Unexpected loss (CR-4 d): the standard deviation of credit losses around EL. ULs “cannot be anticipated
 and hence cannot be adequately priced for in a loan’s interest rate”, so they need a cushion of economic
 capital. UL would be zero only if there were no uncertainty in default and none in the recovery rate.
 • Independence assumptions: PD is independent of EA and LR because default is binary; EA and LR are
 usually treated as independent. If they were not, a covariance cross-term would be needed, but its impact on
 UL is small in practice.
 • Variance of PD (CR-4 e): default is Bernoulli, a binomial B(1; PD), so σPD 2
 = PD(1 − PD). For LR, the
 binomial overstates the variance (all or nothing), the uniform makes any loss from 0% to 100% equally likely,
 and the normal is the most reasonable assumption “because of the lack of better knowledge”.
 • Additivity (CR-4 f): EL is linear and additive across a portfolio; UL is not (diversification). Portfolio UL
 needs ρij , the correlation that default or a credit migration (in the same direction) of assets i and j occurs
 over the same horizon.
 • ULC (CR-4 f): the marginal contribution ULMCi = ∂ULp /∂ULi times ULi . The ULCs sum exactly to
 ULp (“the sum of the parts equals the whole”). The marginal contribution depends only on the UL weights
 of the loans, not on the size of the portfolio.
 • Identical-loans case (S2): with equal size 1/n and constant ρ, ULCi = ULp /n, which reduces for large

 • Limits of the shortcut: the full approach needs n(n − 1)/2 pairwise default correlations, impossible for
 thousands of credits and hard to observe (they can be estimated from asset correlations). The identical-loan
 equation ignores different loan sizes and different correlations (industry, geography), so it does not reveal
 concentrations; it can be decomposed by segment. The analytical approach is cumbersome, so banks use
 numerical procedures (Monte Carlo).
 • Capital multiplier (CR-4 g): ECp = ULp × CM and ECi = ULCi × CM: capital at the transaction level
 is proportional to the loan’s contribution to portfolio risk, not to its standalone UL. CM is the distance from
 ELp to the confidence level in units of ULp . At 99.97% (AA target), CM is between 7.0 and 7.5, “far higher
 than the capital multiples for the normally distributed events in market risk”.
 • Loss distribution (CR-4 h): credit losses are not normal but highly skewed (upside limited to the promised
 payments). The beta distribution on [0, 1] is fully characterised by ELp and ULp ; tail fitting combines it with
 Monte Carlo. For high-quality portfolios (ELp > ULp ) the fitted beta has too fat a tail and overestimates EC;
 for lower-quality ones it has too thin a tail.
 • Challenges (CR-4 i): the approach assumes credits are illiquid (internal “betas”, not market-priced correla-
 tions; a value approach would suit traded credit); almost all internal models use a one-year horizon because
 multi-period modelling is cumbersome; and market and operational risk are assumed to be measured and
 managed separately.
 • Economic versus regulatory view: the regulatory approach “assigns roughly 8% equity capital to credits
 on a standalone basis”; the economic approach differentiates capital by borrower quality and credits diversi-
 fication.
 • Country (transfer) risk: same three components, plus an “overlap” adjustment because the bank can only
 lose its money once. It is “lumpy”: higher correlations and fewer diversification benefits.

 Exam traps
 • Omitted capital multiplier (dropped term): ULp and ULCi are volatilities, not capital. Economic capital
 needs × CM; the credit CM (7.0 to 7.5 at 99.97%) is not the normal quantile used for market risk.
 • Standalone versus contribution (intermediate result): when ULC is asked, the single-asset UL is the planted
 distractor; capital for a loan in a portfolio is ULCi × CM, not ULi × CM; n × ULi is the undiversified (ρ = 1)
 portfolio UL.
 √ √ p
 • ρ versus ρ (correlation misuse): ULC tends to UL ρ, but inside ULp = UL n + ρ(n2 − n) the correlation
 enters unrooted.
 • Wrong input twin: the recovery rate where LR belongs, or PD where σPD = PD(1 − PD) belongs; forgetting
 the square root of the binomial variance.
 • EL in capital: economic capital excludes EL. Adding EL double-counts what reserves and pricing already
 cover; subtracting EL again from ULp × CM removes it twice, because CM is already measured from ELp .
