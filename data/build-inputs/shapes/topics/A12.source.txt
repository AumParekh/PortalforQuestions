  Revision notes
  • Delta-normal (covariance matrix) approach (IM-5 a): asset payoffs are linear (delta) functions of normally
  distributed risk factors, so the portfolio return is normal and VaR has a closed form. Jorion: the method “lends
  itself to a simple decomposition of the portfolio VaR”. VaR treats each component as a risk factor and xi as
  the linear exposure to it; x is the vector of currency positions, kept distinct from the total value W (and from
  the weights wi = xi /W ).
  • Diversified, individual and undiversified VaR (IM-5 a): diversified VaR is the portfolio VaR “taking into
  account diversification benefits between components”; individual VaR is “the VaR of one component taken in
  isolation”, ασi |wi |W (absolute value, because a weight can be negative but risk cannot). Undiversified VaR
  is the sum of individual VaRs, or the portfolio VaR when there is no short position and all correlations are
  unity. Example 5.1: individual VaRs USD 165,000 and 198,000 sum to USD 363,000 against a diversified
  VaR of USD 257,738.
  q
  • Correlation (IM-5 b): with ρ = 0, VaRp = VaR21 + VaR22 ; with ρ = 1 and both positions long, VaRp =
  p  √
  VaR1 + VaR2 . With equal weights, risks and correlations, σp = σ 1/N + (1 − 1/N )ρ → σ ρ: at 20%
  asset risk and ρ = 0.5 risk falls to 14.8% at N = 10 and never below 14.1%. For a long/short position (the
  hedge fund long corporates, short Treasuries) the worst case is ρ = −1, so undiversified VaR is then the
  upper bound at correlation −1.
  • Marginal VaR (IM-5 a): “the change in portfolio VaR resulting from taking an additional dollar of expo-
  sure to a given component”; the partial (linear) derivative with respect to the position, unitless. It equals
  α cov(Ri , Rp )/σp = (VaR/W )βi . Because Σx is already an intermediate step of the VaR run, β and
  marginal VaR come almost for free. Under normality marginal VaR is proportional to βi , so ranking marginal
  VaRs needs only the betas (S5).
  • Using marginal VaR (IM-5 c): to lower VaR by cutting every position by the same fixed amount, “rank all
  marginal VaR numbers and pick the asset with the largest ∆VaR because it will have the greatest hedging
  effect”. Table 5.1: the EUR marginal VaR (0.1521) is three times the CAD’s (0.0528), so cutting EUR is far
  more effective.
  • Incremental VaR (IM-5 a): “the change in VaR owing to a new position”, VaRp+a − VaRp (a “before and
  after” comparison). If VaR falls the trade is a hedge; otherwise it is risk-increasing. It differs from marginal
  VaR because the amount added or subtracted can be large, in which case VaR changes in a nonlinear fashion.

  • Full revaluation versus the shortcut (IM-5 a, c): full incremental VaR needs a re-run of portfolio VaR
  (Jorion’s bank: 100,000 trades, 10 minutes per run). The shortcut (∆VaR)′ a uses the marginal VaR of the
  existing portfolio and ignores second-order terms; it trades accuracy for speed, is good when the trade is
  small relative to the portfolio, and “allows real-time trading limits”. Example 5.1: adding USD 10,000 CAD
  gives USD 528 by the shortcut versus USD 529 exactly.
  • Component VaR (IM-5 a): “a partition of the portfolio VaR that indicates how much the portfolio VaR would
  change approximately if the given component was deleted”.
  P CVaRi = ∆VaRi xi = VaR βi wi = VaRi ρi ;
  components sum exactly to the portfolio VaR because  wi βi = 1. Negative components act as a hedge
  for the rest of the portfolio. Adding individual VaRs and taking percentages is not a valid decomposition: it
  ignores diversification.
  • Curve versus tangent (IM-5 a, Figure 5.4): incremental VaR of deleting the EUR position is USD 92,738,
  measured along the VaR curve; component VaR is USD 152,108, measured along the tangent. The linear
  approximation improves when components are small, so the decomposition is most useful for large portfolios
  of many small positions.
  • Reports and Barings (IM-5 a, c): Table 5.2 (global equity, tracking error 1.82%): Japan and Brazil carry
  over 50% of risk although not the largest deviations (Hot Spots, over 5%); cutting Japan by 4.93% lowers
  volatility to 1.48%. Barings: long USD 7.7 billion Nikkei, short USD 16 billion JGB with a negative corre-
  lation, so the bond leg added risk instead of hedging; component VaRs USD 147.15 and 688.01 million sum
  to USD 835.16 million.
  • General
  P  distributions (IM-5 a): VaR is homogeneous of degree one, so Euler’s theorem gives VaR =
  ∆VaRi xi for any distribution. With simulations, component VaR is the expected component return con-
  ditional on the portfolio being at its VaR, averaged over a window of observations because a single data point
  is unreliable.
  • Risk-minimizing position (IM-5 d): the best hedge adds a∗ = −W βi σp2 /σi2 to one asset. Under a fully
  invested constraint, cut where marginal VaR is greatest and add where it is lowest until all marginal VaRs
  (all betas) are equal. Table 5.4: EUR from 33.33% to 14.79%, volatility from 15.62% to 13.85%, VaR from
  USD 257,738 to 228,462.
  • Return-to-risk maximizing position (IM-5 d): with returns in excess of the risk-free rate, maximize SR =
  Ep /σp (or with VaR in the denominator). At the optimum Ei /∆VaRi is constant, equivalently Ei /βi is
  constant: a restatement of the CAPM (Roll 1977). Table 5.5: E/β is 0.1301 for CAD versus 0.0282 for
  EUR, so CAD is increased from 66.67% to 90.21% and the Sharpe ratio rises from 0.448 to 0.551 (both
  ratios end at 0.0771).
  • Risk management versus portfolio management (IM-5 e): marginal and component VaR serve risk con-
  trol (which position to alter, whether a client trade is risk-reducing, in which case the bid-offer spread can
  be adjusted to win it). Portfolio management balances increasing risk against increasing expected return;
  expected returns are additive but risk is not, which is why the VaR tools are needed. See also A18 for the
  Sharpe ratio as a performance measure.

  Exam traps
  • Shortcut used on a large trade (scope error): (∆VaR)′ a is built from the existing portfolio and is accurate
  only for trades small relative to it. Adding a position as large as the book (S1) needs VaRp+a − VaRp .
  • Marginal versus component (intermediate result): marginal VaR is unitless (VaR per unit of position); com-
  ponent VaR is a currency amount, marginal VaR times the position. A “CAD 0.27 million” option is usually a
  marginal VaR reported as money.
  • Individual VaR as a contribution (dropped term): ασi xi ignores correlation; component VaR multiplies it
  by ρi .
  • Cut versus add (polarity flip): cut the position with the largest marginal VaR; add to the one with the lowest.
  For return to risk, grow the position with the highest Ei /∆VaRi using excess returns, not the lowest marginal
  VaR (that is the minimum-risk rule) and not the highest expected return.
  • Diversification benefit as incremental VaR (sibling swap): undiversified minus diversified VaR is the diver-
  sification benefit, not the change in VaR from adding a position.
  • Confidence level: rescale every VaR measure, including marginal and component VaR, by 2.326/1.645 when
  moving from 95% to 99%.
