  Revision notes
  • See also A9 for CR-22 a to g and m: product types, tranching, waterfall, participants and conflicts, interim
  cash flows, overcollateralization account, the custodian’s tests and motivations. This section covers the sim-
  ulation, the impacts table, sensitivities, risk factors and implied correlation.
  • Simulation procedure (CR-22 h). Estimate parameters (each loan’s default probability, physical or risk-
  neutral, and a pairwise copula correlation restricted to ρ ≥ 0); draw I = 1,000 threads of N = 100
  correlated standard normals; map each to a default time t = − log[1 − Φ(z)]/λ with λ = − log(1 − π)
  (π = 0.0225 ⇒ λ = 0.022757); count defaults per year; pass each thread through the waterfall to get
  tranche losses and equity IRRs. New simulations are needed only for more threads, more loans or new
  correlation settings; changing recovery or collateral interest only changes the processing.
  • Impacts table, default probability (CR-22 i, S1). For any given correlation, a higher default rate raises
  bond losses and lowers the equity IRR: it hurts every tranche.
  • Impacts table, correlation (CR-22 i, S1). At low default rates the impact of correlation is relatively low. At
  higher default rates, higher correlation materially raises the equity value and raises the senior bond’s losses:
  “the equity benefits from high correlation, while the senior bond is hurt by it”. Malz: “correlation is more
  decisive for equity risk and value than default probability”.
  • Mezzanine (CR-22 i). Ambiguous: at low default rates higher correlation increases its losses (it behaves
  like a senior bond); at high default rates it decreases them (it behaves like equity). Table 22.3 writedowns:
  at π = 0.0075, 0.00% (ρ = 0) to 4.84% (ρ = 0.9); at π = 0.0975, 103.92% to 52.87%.
  • Why: clustering. High correlation makes defaults arrive in clusters: some threads have very many, some
  very few. The equity distribution turns U-shaped, a “lottery ticket”; with low correlation some defaults hit
  almost every thread, and since equity takes the first loss, low correlation is bad for the equity. The senior is
  safe unless a cluster breaches its attachment point.
  • Convexity (CR-22 i). At low correlations the equity value is positively convex in default rates (“you can’t
  beat a dead horse”), the senior has negative convexity (losses accelerate), the mezzanine is negatively convex
  at low and positively convex at high default rates. At high correlations all tranches respond more nearly
  linearly.
  • Tranche Credit VaR (CR-22 h, i). Equity: par minus the 10th (50th) lowest of 1,000 simulated values at
  99% (95%). Bonds: the 10th (50th) highest loss minus EL. Equity VaR falls at high π and ρ because EL is
  already so high. Senior: 99% Credit VaR of about half of par at high correlation, virtually risk-free when
  defaults are uncorrelated. At (π, ρ) = (0.0075, 0.30) the senior VaR is negative (EL above the 1% quantile
  loss), a simulation artifact that would disappear at 99.5% or 99.9%.
  • Default01 (CR-22 j). −[V (π + 10 bp) − V (π − 10 bp)]/20, the change per 1 bp rise in default probability,
  reusing the same normal draws to avoid simulation noise. Always positive; converges to zero at very high
  default rates; varies most with π when correlation is low; the peak sits where pool losses approach a tranche’s
  attachment point (equity at π = 0, mezzanine before senior). Underestimating default correlation was an

  important factor in the subprime crisis. Lumpy curves show how costly simulation is, and that defaults stay
  “lumpy” even in a 100-loan pool.
  • Risk factors (CR-22 k). Systematic risk (high correlation exposes even a senior bond on a well-diversified
  pool to large tail losses); tranche thinness (conditional on any loss, the loss is large, so 95% and 99% Credit
  VaR are close); granularity (lumpy pools such as CMBS and fusion deals carry concentration risk; a granular
  pool allows a larger senior tranche with no increase in Credit VaR); prepayment, extension and refinancing
  risk.
  • Standard tranches (CR-22 l). CDX.NA.IG: equal notional CDS on 125 investment-grade names (USD 1
  million each per USD 125 million); iTraxx for Europe and Asia; series initiated semiannually; a default cuts
  the notional by 0.8%. Standard synthetic CDO tranches: equity 0 to 3%, junior mezzanine 3 to 7%, senior
  mezzanine 7 to 10%, senior 10 to 15%, super senior 15 to 30%. Bespoke tranches are issued to order.
  • Implied correlation (CR-22 l, S3). Use risk-neutral PDs from single-name CDS spreads (and risk-free
  discount rates); the copula correlation is the remaining unobservable input; the unique correlation at which
  the modelled tranche value equals the market price is the implied correlation. Tied to a tranche’s attachment
  point: base correlation; it varies by tranche: correlation skew. Malz’s example: equity priced at 5.0 with a
  2% CDS-implied PD matches ρ = 0.3; a rise to 5.6 with no change in PD means implied correlation has
  risen to 0.6, an increase in the market’s assessment of systematic risk.
  • As much market as credit risk (CR-22 l). Implied credit correlation is “as much a market-risk as a credit-
  risk concept”. Each standard tranche has risk-neutral partial spread01s to the 125 constituents rather than
  a default01; changes in implied correlation and spreads cause mark-to-market changes, whereas in the cash
  CLO they change fundamental value. Implied credit correlation is the copula correlation inferred from prices
  (see B3 for the six correlation concepts; D14 for compound versus base correlation).

  Exam traps
  • Polarity flip: higher correlation helps the equity and hurts the senior; a rising equity price with unchanged PDs
  means implied correlation has risen.
  • Scope error, mezzanine: it behaves like the senior at low default rates and like the equity at high ones; “always
  like the equity” is false.
  • Sibling swap, inputs: implied correlation uses risk-neutral PDs from CDS spreads and risk-free rates, not
  physical PDs from ratings; it is as much a market-risk as a credit-risk concept.
  • Absolute claim: a well-diversified collateral pool does not make the senior bond safe when correlation is high.
  • Intermediate result: the equity tranche’s Credit VaR can fall as π and ρ rise, because its expected loss is already
  very high.
