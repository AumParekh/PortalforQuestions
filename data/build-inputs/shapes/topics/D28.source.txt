  Revision notes
  • Inputs (IM-4 a): current portfolio, alphas, covariance estimates, transactions cost estimates and an active
  risk aversion. Only the current portfolio is measured with near certainty; alphas are “often unreasonable
  and subject to hidden biases”; covariances and costs are noisy; even risk aversion is uncertain. The standard
  objective is active return minus an active risk penalty.
  • Refining instead of constraining (IM-4 b): any construction process, however sophisticated, can be replaced
  by refining the alphas and then running a simple unconstrained mean/variance optimisation (modified alphas
  α′ = 2λ′A V hP A ). In the Major Market Index example, no short sales and a 5% cap above benchmark pulled
  the alphas toward zero: their standard deviation fell from 2.00% to 0.57%. Constraints are, in part, safeguards
  against poor research.
  • Scale the alphas (S1): α = volatility × IC × score, with score mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so the scale
  is Std{α} = volatility × IC (IC 0.05 and residual risk 30% give 1.5%). Rescale if the inputs do not have this
  scale. Alternative: match the implied IR to the manager’s ex ante IR. Scaling and trimming are applied as
  the final step.
  • Trim outliers (S1): examine alphas larger than about three times the scale; set those based on questionable
  data to zero and pull genuine ones in to three times scale. The extreme version forces alphas into a normal
  distribution, which uses only the ranking information.
  • Neutralisation (IM-4 c): removing biases or undesirable bets from alphas; decided a priori, not by trying
  all possibilities. Benchmark-neutral: the benchmark has zero alpha, so the optimal portfolio has a beta of 1
  and avoids benchmark timing (subtract βn αB ; in the example αB was only 1.6 bp). Cash-neutral: no active
  cash position (both are possible at once). Risk-factor-neutral: neutralise against factors the manager cannot
  forecast. Industry-neutral: subtract the cap-weighted industry average alpha. Neutralisation is not uniquely
  defined.
  • Transaction costs (IM-4 d, S2): the third “rubber chicken”. They turn a one-dimensional trade-off (one
  knob: active risk aversion) into a two-dimensional one, so the scale of the alphas must now be precise. Costs
  occur at a point in time while alpha accrues over the horizon: the annualised transactions cost is the round-
  trip cost divided by the holding period in years. Example: two 4% annual-alpha stocks, costs USD 0.75 per
  trade on USD 100: a 6-month holding costs 3% a year, a 24-month holding 0.75% a year.
  • Practical details (IM-4 e): choose the active risk aversion from the IR and the desired active risk, λA =
  IR/(2ψP ): IR 0.5 and 5% active risk give 0.05 (check percents versus decimals). A higher aversion to specific
  risk reduces bets on single stocks (the biggest losers) and reduces dispersion across multiple portfolios. Alpha
  coverage: stocks forecast but not in the benchmark get benchmark weight zero; benchmark stocks without
  forecasts get α = 0 after the covered alphas are recentred by their value-weighted average.
  • Portfolio revisions (IM-4 f): revise whenever new information arrives, if the trade-off is specified correctly.
  A manager who underestimates costs and revises daily churns the portfolio; a crude but effective cure is to
  revise less often. Shorter-horizon alphas are noisier, but the costs stay the same whether reacting to signal or
  noise.

  • MCVA (S3): the marginal contribution to value added of stock n, MCVAn = αn − 2λA ψP MCARn , mea-
  sures how risk-adjusted alpha changes as the holding rises against cash; MCAR is the marginal contribution
  to active risk.
  • No-trade region (IM-4 g, S3): the current portfolio is optimal while −SCn ≤ MCVAn ≤ PCn . Book
  example: PC 0.50%, SC 0.75%; an MCVA of 0.80% gives a net benefit from buying of 0.30%; an MCVA of
  −1.30% gives a net benefit from selling of 0.55%. Bandwidth = PC + SC = 1.25%. Just after a purchase
  the stock sits at the upper end, so alpha must fall by 1.25% before a sale is considered.
  • Leland and information horizons (S3): around an optimal 60/40 allocation with linear costs there is a no-
  trade region; outside it, trade back to the boundary, not the target, which cuts turnover and costs roughly in
  half with effectively no change in risk. The region’s size depends on costs, risk aversion and the expected
  return and risk of stocks and bonds. After-cost IR falls with one-way cost and with shorter signal half-lives:
  we pay costs, and we “lose by intimidation”.
  • Screens (IM-4 h, S4): rank by alpha, take the top N , equal- or cap-weight; buy/hold/sell lists regulate
  turnover. Strengths: simple, clear cause and effect, easy to computerise, robust (depends solely on ranking, so
  wild alphas do not alter the result). Weaknesses: ignores all alpha information except rankings, no protection
  against biases (no utilities if all utilities rank low), fragmentary risk control.
  • Stratification (S4): “glorified screening”. Split stocks into exclusive categories (e.g. 10 sectors × 3 sizes
  = 30), screen within each, weight categories at benchmark weights. Adds robustness against alpha biases
  across categories; still ignores information, cannot slightly over- and underweight categories, and risk control
  fails if important dimensions are excluded.
  • Linear programming (S4): “space-age stratification”. Characterises stocks along risk dimensions (industry,
  size, volatility, beta) that need not be exclusive; can include explicit costs, turnover limits and position limits;
  maximises alpha less costs while staying close to the benchmark. Uses all alpha information, but has difficulty
  producing a prespecified number of stocks, and risk-control dimensions must not work against the alphas.
  • Quadratic programming (S4): “the ultimate”: explicitly handles alpha, risk and transaction costs and
  includes LP as a special case. Weakness: far more inputs (500 stocks need 500 volatilities and 124,750
  correlations), and the optimiser exploits noise in covariances. True market vol 17%: estimates within 1%
  barely hurt, errors above 3% matter, and an underestimate to 12% gives negative value added. Lesson: get
  good covariance estimates rather than abandon QP.
  √
  • The test (Muller): identical alphas with IC 0.1 on 500 stocks, so the target IR is 0.1 500 = 2.24. QP had the
  highest and most consistent ex post IR (average 1.88, standard deviation 0.40); a stratified portfolio had the
  single highest (2.82) but no consistency; a screen had a negative IR in one period; none reached 2.24, partly
  because of constraints. Alternatives to mean/variance (semivariance, downside risk, shortfall probability)
  add effort without improving results (Kahn and Stefek; Grinold 1999). See also A18 for the IR.
  • Dispersion (IM-4 i): maximum minus minimum return across separate accounts running the same strategy.
  Client-driven dispersion (client constraints) is beyond the manager’s control; dispersion from differing betas
  and factor exposures (betas 0.9 to 1.1 with a 35% market return gives 7%) should be controlled; dispersion
  from holding different assets is partly optimal because of transaction costs. More portfolios mean more
  dispersion for a given tracking error.
  • Managing dispersion: if alphas and risk are constant, dispersion never disappears (a transaction cost bar-
  rier); with changing alphas the accounts converge (five portfolios converged over roughly 5 years, fastest
  with the lowest costs). Example bound: λA = 0.10, round-trip costs 2%, 10% turnover give a tracking error
  of 1%. Dual-benchmark optimisation reduces dispersion only by giving up return.

  Likely distractors
  • Corrupted: “With purchase cost 0.50% and sales cost 0.75%, the no-trade band for a stock’s MCVA is 0.50%
  wide, since only the purchase cost must be recovered.”
  True: the band runs from −SC to +PC, so its width is 0.50% + 0.75% = 1.25%; after a purchase, alpha must
  fall by 1.25% before a sale is considered.
  • Corrupted: “When a stock/bond mix drifts outside Leland’s no-trade region, the optimal strategy is to trade
  back to the 60/40 target.”
  True: trade back to the boundary of the region; this cuts turnover and transactions costs roughly in half with
  effectively no change in risk.
  • Corrupted: “Screens are sensitive to wild alpha estimates because they use the full magnitude of each alpha.”
  True: screens depend solely on ranking, so wild positive or negative alphas do not alter the result; their weakness
  is that they ignore all information except rankings and control risk only fragmentarily.
  • Corrupted: “Linear programming, like stratification, requires the risk dimensions to partition the stocks into
  mutually exclusive categories.”
  True: exclusive categories are the stratification method; LP characterises stocks along several non-exclusive
  risk dimensions (industry, size, volatility, beta).
  • Corrupted: “Quadratic programming needs fewer inputs than the other techniques, so it is the least exposed to
  estimation error.”
  True: QP needs far more inputs (124,750 correlations for 500 stocks) and the optimiser exploits noise in co-
  variances; an underestimate of market volatility to 12% against a true 17% turns value added negative.
  • Corrupted: “The annualised transactions cost equals the round-trip cost multiplied by the holding period in
  years, so a 6-month holding halves the annual cost.”
  True: divide by the holding period: a 6-month holding doubles the annual cost and a 24-month holding halves
  it.
