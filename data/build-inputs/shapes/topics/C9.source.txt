  Revision notes
  • Exposure measures (CR-21 a, BCBS 2005 definitions): current exposure (replacement cost) = max(0,
  market value of the netting set), lost on default with no recovery; peak exposure = a high percentile (95%
  or 99%) of the exposure distribution at a future date; expected exposure = the mean of that distribution at
  a future date; EPE = the time-weighted average of expected exposures (over the first year, or the longest
  maturity if shorter, for minimum capital). See also C8.
  • CCR as credit risk and as market risk (CR-21 b): statistical measures progressed faster than stress testing.
  Credit view (2000 to 2006, the basis of Basel II): PFE limits, collateral, netting, set at inception; at default
  the bank replaces all trades at once. Market view: CVA (FAS 157), hedged dynamically by replacing trades
  in proportion to the counterparty’s PD before default, so default becomes a non-event. Either view alone
  leaves the bank blind to the other risk; a dealer must consider both. The number of stress results is at least
  twice the number of counterparties plus one.
  • Stressing current exposure (CR-21 c): reprice each counterparty’s trades under a scenario (for example
  equity down 25%) and report the top counterparties. Three problems: (i) aggregation: summing stressed
  exposures is the loss if every counterparty defaulted, and running current exposure through a portfolio credit
  model is wrong (expected exposures belong there); (ii) no credit quality (USD 200 million to a start-up hedge
  fund versus to a AAA corporate); (iii) no insight into wrong-way risk.
  P
  • Loan portfolio stress (CR-21 c, d): EL =  pi eadi lgdi ; hold EAD and LGD fixed and stress the PD
  (directly, or through the macro and balance-sheet variables it depends on) to get ELS . The stress loss is
  ELS − EL, not the stressed EL itself.
  • Derivative portfolio in the credit framework (CR-21 c, d): replace EAD by α×EPE, which lets derivatives
  sit in a portfolio credit model with loans; stress the PD (Table 21.2, dotcom: aggregate EL 1.36, stressed EL
  2.63, stress loss 1.27, USD million) or stress EPE through market variables (Table 21.3). PD stresses move
  all counterparties the same way; EPE stresses may raise or lower the aggregate (directional bias, margining).
  Stress losses on loans and derivatives can be added. Joint credit and market stresses are hard to make con-
  sistent; the best wrong-way check is to stress current exposure, find the most exposed counterparties, then
  examine them for wrong-way risk.
  P  P
  • Stressing CVA (CR-21 e, f): CVA = n LGD∗n j EEn∗ (tj ) qn∗ (tj−1 , tj ) with risk-neutral inputs; shock
  market variables instantaneously (exposures EE ∗ re-simulated, marginal PDs from credit spread shocks)
  to get CVAS ; the stress loss is CVAS − CVA. Differences from the EL view: risk-neutral versus physical
  values, life of the trades versus a fixed horizon, market-implied PD. Market-implied PD allows a stress of the
  exposure-PD correlation (Hull and White, 2012).
  • DVA and BCVA in aggregation (CR-21 g): DVA uses negative expected exposure (exposure from the
  counterparty’s side) and the counterparty’s survival probability; CVA terms are weighted by the bank’s own
  survival. Losses can arise because the bank’s own credit quality improves. Stress loss = stressed BCVA −
  BCVA; with the same market shocks it is added to trading-position stress results by simple addition.

  • Common pitfalls (CR-21 h): using stressed current exposure where EE or EPE belongs (for at-the-money
  trades the gap is almost half the shock); delta approximations for highly nonlinear CCR, worst for at-the-
  money portfolios under large moves; too many stresses can hide the risk; combining default stresses and fair
  value stresses into one test remains the true difficulty. CCR is rarely aggregated consistently with loan or
  trading stresses.
  • Why supervisory stress tests (ORR-17 a): three kinds of capital (what you have, what you need, what
  regulators think you need); Bear Stearns, Lehman, WaMu, Wachovia, Fannie and Freddie were adequately
  capitalized by regulatory rules before failing. A credible program needs a credible estimate of the capital
  hole and a credible way to fill it (the Treasury’s Capital Assistance Program backed SCAP; none drew on it).
  • The regimes (ORR-17 a, Table 17.3): SCAP (Mar 2009): 19 BHCs with at least USD 100 billion assets,
  about two-thirds of banking assets; 4% T1C and 6% T1 post-stress; two-year scenario; ten required to raise
  USD 75 billion, raised USD 77 billion within six months; disclosed bank-level stress losses for eight asset
  classes, supervisory estimates. CEBS (Jul 2010): 91 banks, 6% T1, seven raised EUR 3.5 billion; loss rates
  only for retail and corporate; the Irish banks all passed, then needed EUR 24 billion. EBA (Jul 2011): 90
  banks in 21 countries, 5% T1C, eight raised EUR 2.5 billion; downloadable bank-level spreadsheets; five
  of 25 Spanish banks did not pass but needed EUR 0 after provisions and conversions. CCAR (Mar 2011):
  SCAP-19, 5% T1C, macro scenario only published. CCAR (Mar 2012): SCAP-19 plus 11 BHCs over USD
  50 billion; four of 19 did not pass (objection to capital plans); near-SCAP disclosure.
  • Scenario design and coherence (ORR-17 b): the authorities’ risk appetite sets severity, length and the
  post-stress hurdle (SCAP: two years, 4% T1C; Spain bottom-up: three years, 6% core Tier 1, a lower risk
  appetite). Severity is hard to compare (no credible “1 in 100” statements). Coherence: not all exchange rates
  can depreciate at once, high inflation implies a policy rate response, a flight to quality needs safe havens; acute
  for the trading book (tens of thousands of risk factors). SCAP used three macro dimensions plus a historical
  market shock (June to December 2008): loose coherence, but it tests nothing new. EBA 2011 specified over
  70 trading-book factors and eight macro factors for each of 21 countries. Every supervisory test imposed
  one scenario on all banks; CCAR added banks’ own baseline and stress scenarios for risk discovery.
  • Modelling losses, revenues, balance sheet (ORR-17 c): map few macro factors to many intermediate drivers
  (geography: 2011 state unemployment 3.3% North Dakota to 12.9% Nevada; used-car Manheim index up
  4% while new auto sales fell 37%, so auto LGD need not rise; industry distress drives corporate LGD). EBA’s
  Basel II weights required full stress migration matrices; the US tests used Basel I. Trading book: reprice;
  CCR needs two simulations (PD and exposure). Revenues are “a black box” (non-interest income share 25%
  in 1985 to 43% in 2001); median loss coverage by profits 58% (SCAP), 63% (CCAR 2012), 66% (EBA).
  Balance sheet: static or dynamic, never below required ratios in any quarter, T+1 years for end-horizon
  reserves (CCAR horizon nine quarters).
  • Disclosure (ORR-17 a): SCAP “opened Pandora’s box” with projected bank-level losses; CCAR 2011 dis-
  closed nothing bank-level (“symmetric ignorance”); Goldstein and Sapra: in normal times aggregated results
  limit window dressing and preserve private information production; in a crisis detailed bank-level disclosure
  is valuable. Supervisory tests work as a “pop quiz”.
  • Market-driven scenarios (IM-15 a): pre-GFC stress tests were add-ons to VaR or tracking error built on his-
  torical relationships that broke down in the crisis. MDS meets Surowiecki’s three criteria (specialized private
  knowledge, independent opinions, an objective aggregation mechanism): diverse experts propose views, risk
  management aggregates them, outcomes become instantaneous shocks on policy variables, Mahalanobis dis-
  tance checks plausibility, the covariance matrix implies the rest, iterate, then revalue. Subjectivity remains
  but becomes transparent and open to challenge.
  • Conditional framework and z-scores (IM-15 b): shocks to a small set of K policy variables imply shocks to
  the other M − K factors by multivariate regression (equivalent to estimating the covariance matrix); a small
  K reduces arbitrariness; nonlinear positions are fully revalued. Mahalanobis distance: a multivariate z-score
  that identifies outliers. Scenario z-score: severity in standard deviations, normalized so it does not grow with
  the number of policy variables; lower means more plausible; positioned on a risk ruler of historical events.
  Volatility z-score: magnitude, computed with zero correlations. Correlation z-score: consistency with the
  covariance matrix, independent of absolute shock size; near 0 consistent, above 1.0 a warning.
  • Covariance choice and the five steps (IM-15 b, c): slow data (five years weekly), fast data (one year weekly),
  recent data; each gives a different risk ruler. For a crash, use data from similar crash periods, since correla-
  tions among risky assets spike in crises. Steps: (1) define a low-probability but plausible, precise, relevant
  event with a catalyst; (2) select few policy variables (do not add one with adjusted R2 of 90% or more on

  frequency; (4) imply nonpolicy shocks through the covariance matrix; (5) estimate P&L, fully revaluing
  optionality.
  • Brexit example and challenges (IM-15 c, d): Remain, Soft Brexit, Adverse Brexit; Soft Brexit meant risk-off
  and a dovish Bank of England; policy variables FTSE All Share, 10-year gilt yield, GBP; gilts a safe haven
  in Soft Brexit but yields up in Adverse; recent covariance matrix (no comparable history); 60/40 FTSE 100

  versus accuracy (multicollinearity), covariance calibration, judging plausibility, organisational process.

  Exam traps
  • Stressed EL versus stress loss (intermediate result): the stress loss is the difference ELS − EL (or CVAS −
  CVA).
  • Summing stressed current exposures (absolute claim): the total assumes every counterparty defaults; it is
  not a portfolio stress loss.
  • Current exposure versus EE/EPE (sibling swap): portfolio credit models and BCVA aggregation need ex-
  pected exposure; delta shortcuts fail most for at-the-money books under large moves.
  • Z-score siblings: scenario z-score = severity (lower = more plausible); volatility z-score = magnitude with
  zero correlations; correlation z-score = consistency with the covariance matrix.
  • Few policy variables (polarity flip): MDS keeps the policy set small to limit multicollinearity and arbitrariness.
  • Regime swap: SCAP had three macro dimensions plus a historical market shock; the 70-plus factor scenario is
  EBA 2011; Basel I weights are SCAP and CCAR, Basel II weights are EBA.
