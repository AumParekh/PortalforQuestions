  Revision notes
  • Capital Plan Rule (ORR-20 a): all US-domiciled top-tier BHCs with total consolidated assets of USD 50
  billion or more must maintain a capital plan supported by a robust capital adequacy process (CAP). CCAR
  is the supervisory program that assesses the plans (2013: the 18 BHCs from the 2009 SCAP; others via a
  separate Capital Plan Review until 2014). Capital is “the first line of defense against losses”. Supervisory
  scenarios are not all-encompassing: an internal process that simply mirrors the Federal Reserve’s stress
  testing is a weak practice, and leading practices are not a safe harbor.
  • The seven CAP principles (ORR-20 a): (1) sound foundational risk management; (2) effective loss-
  estimation methodologies; (3) solid resource-estimation methodologies (available capital and projected rev-
  enues over the same scenarios); (4) sufficient capital adequacy impact assessment; (5) comprehensive capital
  policy and capital planning; (6) robust internal controls (policies, change control, model validation and in-
  dependent review, documentation, internal audit); (7) effective governance (board and senior management
  oversight, approval of capital decisions).
  • Risk identification (ORR-20 b): systematic and repeatable; covers on- and off-balance-sheet positions in-
  cluding noncontractual support, and tests the enforceability of netting, guarantees and collateral under stress;
  a refreshed firm-wide risk inventory. “Other risks” (reputational, strategic, compliance) belong in net income
  projections or in a cushion above the capital target; a cushion set by a simple rule or unsupported judgment
  is lagging.
  • Internal controls and validation (ORR-20 b): internal audit reviews the end-to-end process, not just com-
  ponents. Independent validation of all models under SR 11-7: conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring
  (process verification and benchmarking) and outcomes analysis; keep an inventory including feeder models;
  since backtesting stress models is hard, compensate with sensitivity analysis and benchmark or “challenger”
  models; validation covers management overlays. Policies reviewed at least annually; robust MIS; full docu-
  mentation.
  • Governance (ORR-20 b): the board has ultimate oversight, approves the capital plan (and the capital policy,
  or a designated board committee does), receives capital information at least quarterly including post-stress pro
  forma measures, and should challenge results knowing they are estimates within a range. Senior management
  implements, ensures scenarios are severe enough, recommends capital goals and distributions, identifies
  weaknesses and remediation plans, and reports both to the board. Board minutes should show how decisions
  were made.
  • Capital policy, goals and targets (ORR-20 b): internal goals for level and composition of capital, distribu-
  tion guidelines (payout ratios), strategies for shortfalls, governance and escalation protocols; reevaluated at
  least annually. Targets sit above goals so capital stays above goals under stress. Weak: goals based solely
  on regulatory minimums. Contingency plan: triggers for baseline and stress, on projected as well as actual
  results and on market indicators (CDS spreads, ratings, stock price); actions ranked by ease of execution and
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D10 BHC Capital Planning and VaR Model Validation
  impact, realistic under stress.
  • BHC scenario design (ORR-20 b): the BHC-designed scenario must reflect its unique vulnerabilities (busi-
  ness model, footprint, portfolio, revenue drivers); a generic recession similar to the supervisory severely
  adverse scenario does not qualify. Stronger: models combined with expert judgment, tailored vendor sce-
  narios, a suite of scenarios (only one is submitted), sufficient variables, clear narratives. Weak: assuming
  the BHC gains market share in stress. Post-stress ratios below the supervisory scenario’s are “not, in and of
  itself, a safe harbor”.
  • Estimation: general expectations (ORR-20 b): models or quantitative methods as the basis; internal data,
  external where internal is short; segment by common risk characteristics; sensitivity analysis and challenger
  models; vendor models understood, customized and validated. Overlays: quantify their impact against un-
  adjusted estimates; “management should always provide both the initial results and the results after any
  judgmental adjustments” to the board; extensive overlays should trigger a review of the models. Conser-
  vatism: no selective exclusion of losses, no foresight of the scenario path, no favourable management actions
  that cannot be assured.
  • Credit loss methods (ORR-20 b): EL approach (PD, LGD, EAD conditioned on the scenario; long-run
  average parameters “are not appropriate for projecting losses under stress”; aggregate weighted-average LGD
  without segmentation is lagging; static EAD common, loan-equivalent and CCF methods stronger); rating
  transition models (four steps; judgmentally picking past stress matrices is weak); roll-rate models (weak
  beyond the near future; mixing two models is weaker); vintage models; net charge-off models (avoid as
  primary for a material portfolio); scalars (immaterial portfolios only). AFS and HTM: test all credit-sensitive
  securities for OTTI.
  • Operational, market and counterparty risk (ORR-20 b): AMA models not required; use complete internal
  loss data (do not exclude legal reserves, acquired-firm or discontinued-line losses); regression on frequency,
  modified LDA at lower percentiles (70th to 98th) with sensitivity of the percentile choice, scenario-analysis
  overlays, historical averages (backward-looking alone). Trading: deterministic scenarios dominate (design,
  translation to risk factors, revaluation); instantaneous shocks are suitably conservative; check plausibility
  (negative rates); generally full revaluation; explicit default of the largest counterparties; mitigating actions
  only if executable with high confidence.
  • PPNR, balance sheet, RWA, aggregation (ORR-20 b): PPNR over the nine-quarter horizon consistent with
  the scenario, balances and RWA (one coherent story); weak: drastic expense cuts, same strategic assumptions
  in baseline and stress. Balance sheet: build from drivers (paydowns, prepayments, new business); avoid
  favourable asset-mix shifts, flight-to-quality deposit inflows, costless shrinkage, margin improvement; no
  RWA reductions from planned model enhancements. Keep ALLL adequate along and at the end of the
  path. Aggregate through a central group; spreadsheets are weak; rising PPNR amid falling balances needs
  investigation.
  • Three validation components and VaR models (MR-6 a): conceptual soundness (data, assumptions, tech-
  niques, sensitivity analysis), outcomes analysis (backtesting) and benchmarking. Historical simulation is
  the most widely used VaR at commercial banks but is not very responsive to recent volatility; GARCH VaR
  (RiskMetrics a special case) and filtered historical simulation (GARCH volatility times the empirical quantile
  of standardized shocks) address that.
  • Conceptual soundness: fit for purpose (MR-6 a): the key purpose of VaR is to show how risk changes
  when positions change. Berkowitz and O’Brien found a GARCH model on actual P&L outperformed banks’
  VaR, but a P&L-based VaR does not fall when a trader cuts positions (Lo’s Capital Decimation Partners
  versus Jorion’s positional VaR), so it is not fit for purpose and fails conceptual soundness. Supervisors spend
  effort checking the pseudo history of P&L.
  • Sensitivity analysis (MR-6 b): VaR is linear homogeneous in positions (Euler): component VaR = VaR ×
  wi βi , marginal VaR = ∂VaR/∂Vi . Challenge: the regression needs a full time series of the position’s value
  changes, exactly what is missing; proxies may be less volatile or less correlated and understate. Tasche and
  Hallerbach: component VaR ≈ the position’s change on the day that sets the HS VaR (or an average of ordered
  observations near it). If data were sufficient the risk would already be in VaR. Supervisors ask for risks not
  in VaR on both component and stand-alone bases (stand-alone is independent of portfolio composition).
  • Confidence intervals (MR-6 c): few BHCs compute them. Jorion’s asymptotic standard error needs the den-
  sity at the VaR, and normality is a mistake. Nonparametric: order statistics (Dowd) and bootstrap (Christof-
  fersen and Goncalves; assumes independence for HS; GARCH bootstraps re-estimate the model on each
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D10 BHC Capital Planning and VaR Model Validation
  sample). Table 6.1: intervals are asymmetric, more data tightens them, HS intervals are particularly wide in
  stress periods, FHS intervals are the narrowest (more efficient).
  • Backtesting (MR-6, outcomes): Kupiec unconditional coverage, Christoffersen independence and condi-
  tional coverage (LRcc = LRuc + LRind ), dynamic quantile, logistic DQ, VaR quantile regression (failed
  19 of 20 BHCs). P&L should exclude fees and commissions; type II errors are expensive; small samples call
  for Monte Carlo p-values. 2013 to 2016: average exceedance 0.4%, maximum 2.1%, five firms with none.
  Detail: see also C2.
  • Benchmarking (MR-6 d): the most neglected component. Two hindrances: changing portfolios make errors
  non-i.i.d., and banks rarely have two models. Fix: a GARCH(1,1) VaR on the bank’s own P&L as a ready
  benchmark. Diebold and Mariano: the loss function matters. The regulatory loss function (Lopez) penalizes
  only exceedances, so it favours conservatism; the check (quantile) loss rewards accuracy; compare with the
  sign test. Results: positional VaR beats P&L VaR under the regulatory loss at every bank; P&L VaR beats
  positional VaR on accuracy at 16 of 19 banks.

  Capital planning anchors (ORR-20). USD 50 billion threshold; nine-quarter horizon; board information at least
  quarterly; policies and capital policy reviewed at least annually.
  Exam traps
  • Board versus senior management (role misassignment): the board approves the capital plan and capital
  decisions; senior management implements, recommends and reports weaknesses.
  • Safe harbors (absolute claim): neither leading practices nor a BHC scenario harsher than the supervisory
  severely adverse one is a safe harbor.
  • Parameters for stress (scope error): long-run average PD, LGD, EAD are for a mix of conditions, not for
  stress; NCO models and scalars are not primary tools for material portfolios.
  • Accurate versus fit for purpose (sibling swap): a GARCH VaR on actual P&L can be more accurate yet fail
  conceptual soundness because it ignores position changes.
  • Confidence interval width (polarity flip): FHS intervals are the narrowest; HS intervals are widest, especially
  in stress periods.
  • Loss function (sibling swap): the regulatory loss function rewards conservatism (positional VaR wins); the
  check loss rewards accuracy (P&L VaR wins at 16 of 19).
