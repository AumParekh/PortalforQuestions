  Revision notes
  • The study (MR-9 a): daily closes of the 30 Dow stocks, January 1972 to July 2017; monthly 30 × 30
  Pearson correlation bins, diagonal removed and averaged: 534 months, 480,600 correlation values. States:
  expansion (GDP growth 3.5% or higher), normal (0% to 3.49%), recession (two consecutive quarters of
  negative growth).
  • Correlation levels by state (MR-9 a): expansion 27.46%, normal 33.06%, recession 36.96%. “The worse
  the state of the economy the higher are equity correlations”; in strong growth, prices react to idiosyncratic
  factors, in recessions macroeconomic factors dominate. Dow correlations reached 96.97% in February 2009.
  • Correlation volatility by state (MR-9 a): expansion 71.17%, normal 83.06%, recession 80.48%. Volatil-
  ity is lowest in expansions; it is higher in normal periods than in recessions, against expectation, because
  “high correlation levels in a recession remain high without much additional volatility”. Correlation level and
  correlation volatility are positively related.
  • Correlation volatility and recessions (MR-9 a): before every recession except 1990-91 a downturn in cor-
  relation volatility occurred, but the relationship between the decline and the severity of the recession is
  statistically non-significant (R2 close to zero).
  • Mean reversion (MR-9 b; S2): “the tendency of a variable to be pulled back to its long-term mean”; present
  if the√change St − St−1 is negatively related to St−1 . Discrete Vasicek: St − St−1 = a(µS − St−1 )∆t +
  σS ε ∆t, with 0 ≤ a ≤ 1: a = 1 pulls fully back to the mean each step, a = 0.5 halfway, a = 0 not at all.
  • Regression for the rate (MR-9 b; S2): with ∆t = 1 and no stochastic term, St − St−1 = aµS − aSt−1 .
  Regress Y = St − St−1 on X = St−1 : the slope β = −a and the intercept = aµS . Dow 1972 to 2017:
  y = −0.7903x + 0.256, R2 = 0.3948: mean reversion 79.03%, long-term mean 0.256/0.7903 ≈ 32.38%.
  • Forecasting with it (MR-9 b; Example 9.1): St = a(µS −St−1 )+St−1 . February 2017 correlation 26.15%,
  mean 32.38%: March forecast 0.7903 × (0.3238 − 0.2615) + 0.2615 = 31.07%.
  • Autocorrelation (MR-9 b; S3): “the degree to which a variable is correlated to its past values”,
  AC(ρt , ρt−1 ) = cov(ρt , ρt−1 )/(σ(ρt )σ(ρt−1 )), algebraically the Pearson coefficient. It is “the opposite
  property” of mean reversion: one-lag autocorrelation 20.97% and mean reversion 79.03% “add up to 1”.
  Positive autocorrelation is also called persistence. Autocorrelation decays with longer lags (the 2-month lag
  was the highest).
  • Equity correlation distribution (MR-9 c; S1): 464,580 daily correlation values, 77.23% positive. Sixty-
  one distributions tested with Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Anderson-Darling and Chi-squared: the four-parameter
  Johnson SB (γ, δ shape; µ location; σ scale) fits best; normal, lognormal and beta fit poorly. Splitting by
  state of the economy changes the fit “slightly but not significantly”.
  • Bond correlations (MR-9 a, c; S1): level 41.67% (higher than equity’s 34.83%), volatility 63.74% (lower
  than equity’s 79.73%), mean reversion 25.79%. The distribution “shows a more normal shape” and is best
  fitted by the generalised extreme value distribution, “and quite well with the normal distribution”.

  • Default probability correlations (MR-9 a, c; S1): level 30.43% (slightly lower than equity), volatility
  87.74% (slightly higher), mean reversion 29.97%. The distribution is similar to equity correlations and
  “can be replicated best by the Johnson SB distribution”.
  • Mean reversion comparison (MR-9 b): equity correlation mean reversion (77.51% in the bond and default
  comparison sample; 79.03% monthly in the main study) is far stronger than bond (25.79%) or default prob-
  ability (29.97%) correlation mean reversion. Bond and default results are preliminary and being verified on
  a larger sample.
  • Implication: higher correlation levels and correlation volatility in economic distress mean higher correlation
  risk in bad times; correlation models should include mean reversion.

  Best fits. Equity: Johnson SB. Default probability: Johnson SB. Bond: generalised extreme value (normal also quite
  good).
  Exam traps
  • Sign of the slope: the regression slope is −a; the mean reversion rate is its absolute value. Plugging β with its
  sign into the forecast pushes the correlation away from the mean.
  • Intercept is not the mean: the intercept equals aµS ; divide by a to get the long-term mean.
  • Mean reversion versus autocorrelation (sibling swap): strong mean reversion means low autocorrelation
  (79.03% against 20.97%); using one in place of the other in the forecast is the classic slip.
  • Three-way best-fit swap: equity and default probability correlations: Johnson SB; bond correlations: gener-
  alised extreme value. Swapping bond and default is the planted distractor.
  • Volatility by state (polarity flip): correlation volatility is lowest in expansions, but it is higher in normal periods
  (83.06%) than in recessions (80.48%).
  • Absolute claims on distributions: “normal, lognormal or beta” do not fit equity correlations; the economic
  state does not significantly change the equity fit.
