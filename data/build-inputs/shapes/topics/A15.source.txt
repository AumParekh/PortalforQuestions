  Revision notes
  • Cumulative default probability (CR-9 d; Table 9.1, S&P 1981 to 2020): the probability of default by the
  end of year t for issuers starting in a given rating (BBB: 0.16% by year 1, 0.43% by year 2).
  • Unconditional default probability (CR-9 e): the probability of default during year t “as seen at time zero”:
  the difference of consecutive cumulative PDs (BBB year 2: 0.43 − 0.16 = 0.27%; CCC/C year 3: 43.42 −
  38.33 = 5.09%). This is the marginal PD.
  • Conditional default probability (CR-9 e): the unconditional PD divided by the probability of surviving to
  the start of the year (CCC/C year 3: 5.09/61.67 = 8.25%). It is always at least the unconditional value.
  • Hazard rate (default intensity) (CR-9 e): λ(t)∆t is the probability of default between t and t + ∆t condi-
  tional on no earlier default; it is the conditional PD for a very short period. 1 − e−λ̄t is the cumulative PD
  it implies, not the hazard rate itself.
  • Rating and PD (CR-9 c, d): for investment grade the marginal PD tends to increase with time in the first
  few years (A: 0.05%, 0.08%, 0.09%, 0.11%); for the lowest grades it can decrease (CCC/C: 28.3%, 10.03%,
  5.09%, 2.94%) because the next year or two are critical and survivors’ health is likely to improve. See also
  A10.
  • Rating migration matrix (CR-9 d; CR-10, Table 10.1): one-year probabilities of moving between ratings.
  Assuming independence across periods, the two-year matrix is the matrix multiplied by itself and the five-
  year matrix its fifth power; six months needs the square root. The independence assumption is not exactly
  true (ratings momentum). Schroeck makes a related point: a counterparty’s PD in the second year of a loan
  is typically higher than in the first year, modelled with Markov migration matrices.
  • Recovery rate (CR-9 f): the price of the bond about one month after default as a percent of principal.
  Table 9.2 (Moody’s, 1983 to 2021): first lien 54.6%, second lien 44.3%, senior unsecured 38.0%, senior
  subordinated 31.0%, subordinated 32.1%, junior subordinated 22.3%.
  • Recovery depends negatively on default rates (CR-9 f): in a low-default year recoveries might be as high
  as 60%, in a high-default year as low as 30%. “A bad year for defaults is doubly bad for a lender.” The same
  link appeared in mortgages in the 2007 to 2008 crisis.
  • CDS mechanics (CR-9 g): insurance against default by the reference entity; the default is a credit event
  (failure to pay, restructuring, bankruptcy; restructuring sometimes excluded in North America). The face
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A15 Hazard Rates, CDS Spreads and Conditional vs Unconditional PD
  value of bonds that can be sold is the notional principal. The buyer pays periodic premiums (usually quarterly
  in arrears) until maturity or the credit event, plus a final accrual payment, and gains the right to sell bonds at
  face value; the seller receives the premiums and pays face value minus the auction value of the cheapest-to-
  deliver bond (cash settlement, now usual) or buys the bonds at face value (physical settlement).
  • CDS quotes and market (CR-9 g, h): the CDS spread is the total paid per year as a percent of notional. A
  market maker’s bid is the price at which it buys protection, the ask the price at which it sells. Banks tend
  to be net buyers, insurers net sellers. Fixed coupons: if the quoted spread is below the coupon, the seller
  pays the PV difference upfront; if above, the buyer pays. Indices (CDX NA IG, iTraxx Europe) hold 125
  investment-grade names.
  • Spread-implied hazard rate (CR-9 h, S3): the credit spread is roughly an average annual loss rate, so
  λ̄ = s(T )/(1 − R) (eq. 9.3): 240 bp with 40% recovery gives 4%. Spreads for several maturities bootstrap
  the term structure (Example 9.3). The approximation works well for CDS spreads, and for bond yield or asset
  swap spreads only when the bond trades near par; otherwise use the more exact bond-pricing calculation
  (Table 9.3, Q = 3.03%).
  • CDS spread and bond yields (CR-9 h, i): the n-year CDS spread should be close to the par yield on an
  n-year corporate bond minus the par yield on an n-year risk-free bond. The implied risk-free rate is higher
  than Treasury rates, close to the short-term borrowing rate of a AA company.
  • CDS-bond basis (CR-9 i): CDS spread minus bond yield spread; in theory close to zero. Bond above par:
  negative; below par: positive; CDS counterparty risk: negative; cheapest-to-deliver option: positive; no
  accrued interest in the payoff: negative; restructuring clause: positive; a non-market risk-free benchmark:
  either way. Positive before 2007 (about 16 bp in 2004 to 2005), very negative at times in the crisis, usually
  small and negative since.
  • Historical versus spread-implied PDs (CR-9 j, Tables 9.4 and 9.5): seven-year hazard rates implied by
  spreads (risk-neutral) are much higher than those from historical default rates (real-world), and the gap widens
  as credit quality declines (A: 0.109% versus 1.145%). Investors are compensated for more than the actuarial
  cost of default.
  • Why the gap (CR-9 j): illiquidity of corporate bonds (perhaps 25 bp in normal markets); traders’ subjective
  depression scenarios (hard to see as a large part); but “by far the most important reason” is that bonds do not
  default independently, so systematic risk earns a premium. Skewed, hard-to-diversify idiosyncratic risk also
  contributes.
  • Which PD to use (CR-9 k): risk-neutral (spread-implied) PDs for valuation; real-world (historical) PDs for
  scenario analysis. Merton-based PDs are covered in A5.

  Exam traps
  • Unconditional versus conditional (intermediate result): the difference of cumulative PDs is the unconditional
  PD; dividing by survival to the start of the year gives the conditional one. Read the stem for “as seen today”
  versus “conditional on no earlier default”.
  • Wrong survival denominator: dividing by survival to the end of the year (1−Ct ) instead of the start (1−Ct−1 ).
  • Cumulative PD is not the hazard rate (sibling swap): 1 − e−λt is the probability of default by t. Feeding a
  cumulative PD into s = λ̄(1 − R) as if it were λ̄ overstates the spread; the hazard needs − ln(1 − Q)/T .
  • Recovery versus LGD (wrong input twin): divide the spread by 1 − R, not by R; and divide, do not multiply.
  180 bp is 0.0180.
  • Forward versus average hazard: the hazard between two maturities needs the T -weighted bootstrap divided
  by T2 − T1 , not the longer average hazard and not a simple average of the two.
  • Continuous versus discrete: Q = 1 − e−λt , not 1 − (1 − λ)t and not λt.
