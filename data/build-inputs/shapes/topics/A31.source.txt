  Revision notes
  • Three Basel II credit risk options (ORR-21 e), included because supervisors feared banks would manipulate
  internal risk measures to reduce capital. Standardized: like Basel I with risk weights somewhat more
  sensitive to credit quality, using external ratings (obligor type and rating, or asset type; retail 75%, mortgages
  35%); intended for banks whose internal measures cannot support IRB. The United States chose not to
  implement it.
  • Foundation IRB (ORR-21 e): the bank provides only PD; the accord specifies EAD and LGD (45% senior,
  75% subordinated; collateral reduces LGD via the comprehensive approach); MA is set to 2.5 in most cases;
  PD can be no lower than 0.0003 for bank and corporate exposures (implicitly zero for sovereigns). Expected
  to be feasible for most large banks, which already had internal rating systems yielding a PD.
  • Advanced IRB (ORR-21 e): the bank provides PD, LGD, EAD (and MA) from its own data, models, esti-
  mates and analysis. Limited EAD and LGD data meant fewer banks were expected to qualify.
  • The model (ORR-21 f; CR-10 d): Gordy’s (2003) asymptotic single risk factor model, i.e. a one-factor
  Gaussian copula. In large, well-diversified (infinitely granular) portfolios, each asset’s contribution to capital
  depends only on its own PD, LGD and EAD; the Committee sets the percentile and the correlation ρ. The
  2014 Large Exposure Framework was needed because real banks had sizable single-name exposures.
  P
  • Calibration (ORR-21 f): one-year horizon, 99.9th percentile. Carey writes capital = [EADi × LGDi ×
  DR99.9,i ] − EL. Loan loss reserves were not Tier 1 but were thought to approximate EL, so capital covers
  unexpected loss only; a shortfall of reserves below EL reduces capital.
  • Vasicek in Hull (CR-10 d): WCDR(T, X) is the Xth percentile of the default rate; for one loan the Xth loss
  percentile is WCDR × EAD × LGD; Gordy extends this to a sum over a large portfolio (eq. 10.2), the basis
  of banking-book capital with T = 1 year and X = 99.9%, sometimes multiplied by a maturity adjustment.
  ρ is roughly the asset (equity) return correlation. Weakness: very little tail correlation; another copula can
  fix it.
  • Wholesale correlation (ORR-21 f): ρ falls from 0.24 to 0.12 as PD rises (Lopez 2004): very high-risk
  borrowers default for idiosyncratic reasons, middle-risk borrowers default together when the macroeconomy
  is distressed. Effect: DR rises somewhat less quickly with PD than at constant ρ (Table 21.4: PD 0.01 with
  ρ = 0.2 gives DR 0.146).
  • Maturity adjustment (ORR-21 f): bank, corporate and sovereign exposures only; assets with more than one
  year of remaining maturity can deteriorate in credit quality without defaulting. Low-PD assets get the largest
  MA (more room to be downgraded).
  • Retail (ORR-21 f): only an Advanced-IRB-like treatment (bank estimates PD, LGD, EAD), no maturity
  adjustment, and three correlations: 0.15 residential mortgages, 0.04 qualifying revolving (credit cards), for-

  mula 0.03 to 0.16 for other retail. Retail correlations are lower than wholesale. Carey: USD 100 million
  mortgages, PD 0.01, LGD 30%: DR 0.09, RWA 30 (Basel I 50; Basel II standardized 35).
  • Credit mitigants (ORR-21 f): credit substitution (guarantor’s rating replaces the obligor’s up to the amount
  covered); the 2005 double-default amendment multiplies capital without the mitigant by 0.15 + 160 PDg .
  • Why revise IRB in 2017 (ORR-23 a): restore credibility of RWAs and comparability of capital ratios. Short-
  comings of IRB: excessive complexity, lack of comparability of internally modelled requirements, lack of
  robustness in modelling certain asset classes. Also: more risk-sensitive standardised approaches, internal
  models removed for CVA and operational risk, a G-SIB leverage ratio buffer, and a new output floor.
  • IRB revisions (ORR-23 b): (i) A-IRB removed for asset classes that cannot be modelled robustly: large
  and mid-sized corporates (consolidated revenues above EUR 500 million) and banks and other financial
  institutions, which move to F-IRB (fixed LGD and EAD remove two sources of RWA variability) or SA; (ii)
  all IRB approaches removed for equities (to SA); specialised lending keeps A-IRB, F-IRB and slotting; (iii)
  input floors; (iv) greater specification of parameter estimation. F-IRB senior unsecured LGD to non-financial
  corporates cut from 45% to 40%; higher haircuts and lower LGDs for non-financial collateral. The 1.06 IRB
  scaling factor is removed. Carey adds: IRB must cover all assets in an asset class (no cherry picking).
  • Input floors (ORR-23 b, Table 23.3): PD floors apply under both F-IRB and A-IRB (5 bp corporate, mort-
  gages, QRRE transactors and other retail; 10 bp QRRE revolvers); LGD and EAD floors apply only under
  A-IRB. Unsecured LGD floors: corporate 25%, QRRE 50%, other retail 30%; secured LGD floors 0% finan-
  cial, 10% receivables, 10% real estate, 15% other physical collateral (mortgages 5%). EAD floor: on-balance
  sheet plus 50% of off-balance sheet exposure using the SA credit conversion factor.
  • Output floor (ORR-23 c): replaces the Basel II floor (80% of Basel I requirements, inconsistently applied).
  RWA = max(RWA under approved approaches, 72.5% of RWA under the standardised approaches only). It is
  a risk-based backstop that keeps a level playing field between modelled and standardised banks. Standardised
  approaches used in the floor: SA credit risk, SA-CCR for counterparty exposure, SA-CVA or BA-CVA (or
  100% of CCR capital), SEC-ERBA, SEC-SA or 1250% for securitisation, SA market risk, SA operational
  risk. Phase-in: 50% (2022), 55%, 60%, 65%, 70%, 72.5% (1 January 2027). National discretion: transitional
  cap on the RWA increase at 25% of pre-floor RWA, removed in 2027.
  • See also B1 (credit VaR and granularity), D20 (Vasicek versus CreditRisk+ versus CreditMetrics) and C5
  (Basel III post-crisis reforms, SA credit, CVA and the SMA).

  Exam traps
  • Dropped term (−PD or −EL): WCDR alone is the stress loss; capital covers unexpected loss only.
  • Capital versus RWA: RWA = 12.5× capital. Read which one the stem asks for.
  • Maturity adjustment misapplied: omitted for a corporate loan, M = 2.5 used when an A-IRB stem gives the
  maturity, or applied to retail (retail has none).
  • Correlation twin and polarity: corporate ρ on mortgages, 0.04 on mortgages, or weights swapped so ρ rises
  with PD. Corporate ρ falls as PD rises.
  • Foundation inputs: the bank’s own LGD (A-IRB only) or a PD below the floor. In 2017, PD floors bind under
  both approaches; LGD and EAD floors only under A-IRB.
  • Output floor base: 72.5% of standardised-only RWA, not of modelled RWA and not 100% of standardised
  RWA; it is a floor (a higher-of), never a cap.
