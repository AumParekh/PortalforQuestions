  Revision notes
  • Chapter verdict: after taking into account biases induced by infrequent trading and selection, it is unlikely
  that illiquid asset classes have higher risk-adjusted returns, on average, than liquid stock and bond markets;
  but there are significant illiquidity premiums within asset classes. Portfolio choice models recommend only
  modest holdings and high required premiums.
  • Liquidating Harvard: HMC held 55% in hedge funds, private equity and real assets; the endowment fell
  22% from July to October 2008, and buyers of its USD 1.5 billion private equity stake asked discounts of
  about 50%. Its “worst failing” was asset-liability management: the duration of its liabilities was shorter than
  that of its assets. It borrowed (USD 2.5 billion in bonds).
  • Sources of illiquidity (IM-17 b, Vayanos and Wang): (1) clientele effects and participation costs, (2) trans-
  action costs, (3) search frictions, (4) asymmetric information (lemons, Akerlof; also a search friction), (5)
  price impact, (6) funding constraints.
  • Characteristics of illiquid markets (IM-17 a): most asset classes are illiquid (infrequent trading, small
  amounts, low turnover; munis trade about twice a year, institutional real estate every 8 to 11 years, art every
  40 to 70 years); illiquid markets are large (bigger in total than public stocks and bonds); investors hold lots
  of illiquid assets (about 90% of individuals’ wealth); liquidity dries up regularly (commercial paper, repo,
  auction rate securities in 2008), with major crises at least once every ten years.
  • Reported returns are not returns (IM-17 c): three biases overstate expected returns and understate risk:
  survivorship bias, infrequent sampling, selection bias. Survivorship: poorly performing funds stop reporting
  (the long-lived smokers analogy); for mutual funds it knocks at least 1% to 2% off expected returns, above
  4% when comparing defunct and live funds; reporting bias adds to it. See also A24.
  • Infrequent trading: volatilities, correlations and betas computed from reported returns are too low (Figure
  19.1: quarterly vol 0.23 versus 0.28 from daily data). This is a “population” problem, not a small-sample
  problem. Geltner: real estate betas understated by a factor of 0.5.
  • Unsmoothing (IM-17 d): Geltner, Ross and Zisler. Reported returns follow an AR(1); the filter inverts out
  true returns, which should then be close to IID. Unsmoothing adds noise (unlike ordinary filtering, which
  removes it), so the variance of true returns exceeds that of reported returns since |ϕ| < 1.
  • Three properties of unsmoothing: (1) it affects only risk estimates, not expected returns (the first and last
  prices are unchanged); (2) it has no effect if observed returns are uncorrelated (ϕ = 0); (3) it is an art: real
  estate has a fourth-order lag from annual reappraisals, so a good procedure fits an ARMA model and needs
  economic knowledge of the market.
  • NCREIF evidence: first-order autocorrelation 0.78; worst reported quarter −5.3% (Dec 1991) becomes
  −22.6% unsmoothed, and −8.3% (Dec 2008) becomes −36.3%; quarterly volatility rises from 2.25% to
  6.26% (stocks about 7.5%); correlation with the S&P 500 rises from 9.2% to 15.8%. Art volatility: 6.5% to
  11.5%.
  • Selection bias (IM-17 c): returns are observed only when values are high (buildings sold when high, buyouts
  taken public when high, “zombie” VC companies). Fitting the SML to observed returns gives a positive alpha

  when the true alpha is zero, a flatter slope (beta underestimated) and lower volatility. Correction: Heckman
  (1979). Cochrane: VC alpha above 90% falls to −7% after correction; real estate means 1.7% to 0.3%,
  standard deviations up by a factor of 1.5.
  • Premiums across asset classes (IM-17 e): the conventional view (Ilmanen’s chart) is flawed because of
  illiquidity biases, because it ignores other risks (NCREIF is beaten by 60/40; average hedge fund and PE
  fund give zero expected excess returns), because there is no investable market index, and because factor risk
  cannot be separated from manager skill.
  • Premiums within asset classes: on-the-run versus off-the-run Treasuries (bonds more than 5% cheaper than
  notes of the same maturity in the crisis); illiquidity explains 7% of investment-grade and 22% of junk yield
  variation; equity premiums 1% to 8% (now diminished), pink sheets almost 20%; PE illiquidity premiums
  typically 3%. Explanation offered: limited integration across asset classes (silos), or investors overpaying
  for illiquid asset classes.
  • Four ways to harvest: (i) passive allocation to illiquid asset classes, (ii) liquidity security selection within
  classes, (iii) market making (DFA in small caps; buying PE secondaries at 30% to 50% discounts in the
  1990s, hedge fund discounts 6% to 8%), (iv) dynamic rebalancing at the aggregate level, which is simplest
  to implement and has the greatest impact. Large owners give up premiums by tracking standard indexes
  (inclusion effects of 3% to 5%).
  • Portfolio choice (IM-17 f): Constantinides: with transaction costs there is a no-trade band (10% costs, 35%
  volatility: trade only outside 0% to 50% around a 25% optimum); premium above 5% for costs of 15% or
  more. Ang, Papanikolaou and Westerfield (liquidity events with Poisson intensity λ): optimal holding 59%
  with continuous trading, 44% (half year), 37% (1 year), 24% (2 years), 11% (5 years), 5% (10 years); required
  premiums 0.7%, 0.9%, 2.0%, 4.3%, 6.0%.
  • Behaviour with illiquid assets: rebalance to a position below the long-run average holding (right skew);
  consume less; no illiquidity “arbitrage” (no plus or minus infinity positions); illiquidity induces time-varying,
  endogenous risk aversion and makes the investor more risk-averse toward both liquid and illiquid assets. Size
  and patience are necessary but not sufficient; the Swensen case rests on one word, “skilled”.

  Exam traps
  • Direction of every bias (polarity): all three biases overstate expected returns and understate risk. Survivorship
  drops poor performers; selection records high values.
  • Unsmoothing changes risk only (scope): volatility, correlation and beta rise; the mean is unchanged.
  • Which correction for which bias (sibling swap): unsmoothing (Geltner, Ross, Zisler) for infrequent trading;
  Heckman selection models for selection bias; only observing the full population removes survivorship bias.
  • Across versus within (polarity): premiums are large within asset classes, not across them.
  • Which of the four harvesting routes (sibling swap): dynamic rebalancing, not passive allocation, is simplest
  with the greatest impact.
  • Rebalancing point (polarity): rebalance illiquid holdings to a level below the long-run average holding.
