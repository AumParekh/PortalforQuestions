  Revision notes
  The specifics first: ES replaces VaR, horizons, two approaches (MR-18 a, b)
  • Timeline. Consultative document May 2012; Quantitative Impact Studies (QISs: banks estimate the effect
  of proposed rules); final rules January 2019; agreed implementation January 2023, with many jurisdictions
  a year or two later.
  • Measure change (S1). Basel I and Basel 2.5 used 10-day VaR at 99%. FRTB uses stressed ES at 97.5%:
  calculations are based on how market variables moved during stressed market conditions, as with stressed
  VaR. Basel I VaR was “current”: based on the immediately preceding one to four years.
  • Why 97.5% ES. For normal losses with mean µ and standard deviation σ: 99% VaR = µ + 2.326σ, 97.5%
  ES = µ + 2.338σ, almost exactly the same. For non-normal losses they are not equivalent: with a heavier
  tail than the normal, 97.5% ES can be considerably greater than 99% VaR. So the change bites exactly
  where tail risk is fat.
  • Liquidity horizons (S2). The fixed 10-day horizon is replaced by horizons reflecting the liquidity of each
  market variable (“risk factor”); changes over the horizon in stressed markets are “shocks”. Five horizons:
  10, 20, 40, 60, 120 days = categories 1 to 5.
  • Table 18.1 by asset class. 10: equity price large cap. 20: sovereign IG credit spread; equity price small cap;
  large-cap equity volatility; energy price; precious metal price. 40: sovereign non-IG spread; corporate IG
  spread; FX volatility. 60: interest rate volatility; corporate non-IG spread; small-cap equity volatility; equity
  other; other commodities price; energy price volatility; precious metal volatility. 120: credit spread other;
  credit spread volatility; other commodities price volatility; commodity other. Ranges: interest rate 10 to 60
  and FX rate 10 to 40 (currency dependent).
  • Two approaches, both always computed (S3). Even a bank approved for the internal models approach
  must also calculate standardized capital, consistent with standardized approaches acting as a floor (December
  2017: total capital at least 72.5% of standardized, reached by 2028 after a five-year phase-in). Trend since
  2008: less reliance on internal models.
  • Desk level. Most calculations are done per trading desk, and internal models permission is granted desk by
  desk: the FX desk can be on internal models while the equity desk is not.
  Internal models approach and the cascade (MR-18 b)
  • Overlapping 10-day changes. All ES calculations use
  √ 10-day changes from the stressed period (Day 0 to
  10, Day 1 to 11, …), not one-day changes scaled by 10 as in Basel I and Basel 2.5. Overlap means trials

  are not independent: no bias, but a smaller effective sample, so noisier results. Method is not prescribed;
  historical simulation is likely.
  • Cascade. ES1 : shock all risk factors. ES2 : shock categories 2 to 5, hold category 1 constant. ES3 : cate-
  gories 3 to 5. ES4 : categories 4 and 5. ES5 : category 5 only. Combine with weights (LHj −LHj−1 )/10 =
  1, 1, 2, 2, 6 (independence of successive periods: an extension of the square-root rule). Also usable for VaR.
  Six desks need 5 × 6 = 30 ES runs. If a desk has no factors in category 1, holding category 1 constant
  changes nothing, so ES2 = ES1 .
  • Stressed period search. Back as far as 2007. A subset of risk factors may be used if it covers at least 75% of
  current factors; ES is then scaled up by (ES, last 12 months, all factors) / (ES, last 12 months, subset). This
  can double the runs from 30 to 60.
  • Weighted ES and capital. Partial ES per desk (shock that
  Pdesk’s factors only); the sum of partial ES always
  exceeds total-portfolio ES. W ES = λEST + (1 − λ) ESP j , λ = 0.5. Non-modelable risk factors:
  fewer than 24 observations a year or more than one month between observations; handled by stress tests
  (NMC). Capital uses 60-day averages and mc ≥ 1.5.
  Standardized approach, including curvature (MR-18 a)
  • Three components. (1) Sensitivity-based risk charge: delta, vega and curvature for each of 7 risk classes
  (general interest rate, FX, commodity, equity, three credit spread classes); (2) default risk charge; (3) resid-
  ual risk add-on. No diversification across risk classes, nor between delta and vega within a class. Correla-
  tions tested at 1.25, 1.00 and 0.75 times the Basel values; the greatest charge is used.
  • Deltas and risk weights. Equity, FX, commodity deltas are per percentage change (1% move gives USD
  3,000, so δ = 300,000); rate and spread deltas are per  √ absolute change (1 bp costs
  √ USD 200, so δ =
  −2,000,000). Risk weight = stressed daily volatility × LH × 2.338, e.g. 0.02 × 20 × 2.338 = 0.209.
  Each vertex of a term structure (e.g. 10 rate vertices from 3 months to 30 years) is a separate risk factor.
  • Curvature risk charge (S6). The standardized charge for gamma risk. Shock factor i up and down by its
  risk weight Wi ; for each direction take the delta-predicted change minus the actual change: Wi δi − ∆V +
  and −Wi δi − ∆V − . The charge is the greater of the two; if negative, it counts as zero. Short options
  (Figure 18.1a) attract it; long options (Figure 18.1b) give zero, since curvature raises value relative to delta
  for both moves. Term structures are shifted in parallel by the largest Wi on the curve, with each point’s own
  δi removing delta.
  • Default risk charge and residual add-on. Jump-to-default: exposure × LGD × default risk weight (senior
  debt LGD 75%; A-rated weight 3%; equity LGD 100%). Residual add-on: notional × risk weight (1% for
  exotic options not decomposable into vanillas). The simplified approach for smaller banks (June 2017, in
  the 2019 rules) drops vega and gamma.
  Boundary, backtesting, P&L attribution, credit, securitizations (MR-18 c)
  • Trading book vs banking book (S4). Trading book gets market risk capital; banking book (held to maturity)
  gets credit risk capital. Banks exploited the gap (credit-dependent instruments parked in the trading book for
  lower capital). FRTB: “intent to trade” is no longer sufficient; the bank must be able to trade and manage the
  risks on a trading desk, and day-to-day value changes should affect equity and pose solvency risk. Assignment
  is at initiation; transfers only in extraordinary circumstances (desk closure, change in fair-value accounting
  standards), and any capital benefit from moving is disallowed.
  • Backtesting. Stressed ES is not backtested (ES is harder to backtest than VaR; a stressed measure cannot
  be backtested at all). Each desk backtests 1-day VaR at 99% and 97.5% on the most recent 12 months:
  more than 12 exceptions at 99% or more than 30 at 97.5% sends the desk to the standardized approach until
  neither condition holds. Regulators may also test that daily p-values are uniform.
  • P&L attribution. U = actual minus model P&L; V = actual P&L (“actual” = hypothetical, no intraday
  trading). Mean(U )/SD(V ) within −10% to +10%; Var(U )/Var(V ) below 20%. Four or more situations
  outside these ranges in 12 months: standardized approach.
  • Credit risk and securitizations. Credit spread risk is treated like market risk (horizons 20 to 120 days;
  spread volatility 120). Jump-to-default risk is treated like banking-book default risk: internal models use
  1-year 99.9% VaR. Securitizations: the Basel 2.5 comprehensive risk measure allowed own models, but
  capital varied too much across banks, so FRTB requires the standardized approach.
  FRTB vs earlier Basel market risk rules (S5; ORR-21 c, ORR-22 a, b, c)

  • Basel I, 1996 Amendment. Standardized or internal√  models approach; internal models: 10-day 99% VaR
  from at least one year of data, usually 1-day VaR × 10; M R = max(V aRt−1 , m × V aRavg ), 60-day
  average, m ≥ 3 (specific risk multiplier 4). Daily backtest of 1-day 99% VaR over 250 days: 10 or more
  exceptions forces m = 4. Tier 3 capital introduced.
  • Basel II. Retained much of Basel I: its innovations were IRB credit risk weights, operational risk capital, the
  three pillars and repeated QISs. Market risk capital stayed on the 1996 Amendment basis.
  • Basel 2.5 (by end-2011). Three changes: stressed VaR (250-day most stressful period in the last seven
  years), so M R2.5 = max(V aRt−1 , mc V aRavg ) + max(SV aRt−1 , ms SV aRavg ), at least twice the 1996
  charge with equal multipliers; incremental risk charge (99.9%, one-year, defaults and downgrades, liquidity
  horizon at least three months); comprehensive risk measure for the correlation book. FRTB then replaces
  VaR plus stressed VaR with one stressed ES, adds liquidity horizons, desk-level approval, the SA floor and a
  firmer book boundary.

  Exam traps
  p
  • Cascade weights (unit/time conversion): scaling by LHj /10 (weights 1, 2, 4, 6, 12) instead of the increment
  p
  (LHj − LHj−1 )/10 (weights 1, 1, 2, 2, 6); or adding the scaled ES linearly (perfect correlation) instead of
  root-sum-of-squares (independence).
  • Missing category (dropped term): a desk with no 10-day factors still has an ES2 term, and it equals ES1 .
  Setting it to zero understates ES.
  • Polarity flip on tails: heavy tails make 97.5% ES greater than 99% VaR, not smaller; under normality they
  nearly coincide (2.338 vs 2.326).
  • Sibling swap on backtesting: FRTB backtests 1-day VaR at 99% and 97.5% (thresholds: more than 12, more
  than 30), never the stressed ES; P&L attribution needs four or more breaches, and both ratio tests apply. The
  1996 rule (10 or more exceptions in 250 days: multiplier 4) is a different test.
  • Absolute claims and scope: internal models approval never removes the standardized calculation; approval is
  desk by desk, not bank-wide; intent to trade alone no longer qualifies for the trading book; re-booking is for
  extraordinary circumstances only and gives no capital benefit.
  • Polarity flip on curvature: short options attract the curvature charge; long options give zero. Wrong accord:
  stressed VaR, IRC and CRM are Basel 2.5, not Basel II.
