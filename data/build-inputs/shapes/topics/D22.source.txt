  Revision notes
  The specifics: controls, RCSA, KRIs, loss data (S1 to S4)
  • The control sibling set (S1; ORR-5 b). IIA classification. Preventive: reduce the likelihood of an incident,
  act before the event on its causes (segregation of duties, the best-known fraud control; verification of records;
  signature reconciliation; limitation of access; levels of authorisation; KYC/CDD). Detective: alert when an
  incident occurs, speed resolution and limit impact (exception reports, smoke and intrusion detectors, card-
  fraud alerts); a detective control becomes preventive when it detects a cause, as a file reconciliation run before
  execution. Corrective: lessen the impact without changing likelihood (back-ups, IT redundancies, continuity
  plans, crisis communication; seatbelts and airbags). Directive: the prescriptions for executing a process
  (policies, procedures, training, guidance, governance structure, roles, supervision, handover procedures);
  often missing from taxonomies but effective when comprehensive.
  • Key controls and automation (ORR-5 b, c). A key (primary) control is sufficient on its own to prevent the
  risk materialising (it can be corrective, as a cloud back-up); a non-key (secondary) control only complements.
  Automation raises reliability but turns human-error risk into technology and model risk: high-likelihood,
  low-impact manual errors become low-likelihood, high-impact systemic breakdowns (the “water mattress”).
  Failure modes: false positives and negatives, controls disabled by downtime, back-ups failing silently on a
  full server. Knight Capital lost nearly USD 460 million in 45 minutes from repurposed code, weak version
  control, insufficient testing, unprioritised alerts and no one empowered to stop trading.
  • Control design and testing (ORR-5 c). Weak designs: optimistic (tick-box sign-offs needing exceptional
  ability or motivation), collective (four-eyes checks dilute accountability; better with manager and subor-
  dinate or different departments), more of the same (adding failed designs). Four tests in rising scrutiny:
  self-certification or inquiry (secondary or low-risk controls only), examination (documentary evidence; suits
  automated controls), observation (real time; key controls), reperformance (strongest: mystery shopping,
  bogus transactions, model replication). Effectiveness depends on tester independence, frequency propor-
  tionate to risk, scope and sample. Testing was historically internal audit’s; the first line increasingly tests key
  controls; the ORM function does not usually test but needs assurance.
  • RCSA (S2; ORR-4 b). Evaluates likelihood and impact of inherent risk (before controls) and residual
  risk (after controls); typically annual, sometimes quarterly; workshops facilitated by the second line or ques-
  tionnaires; a risk assessment unit may run it. Mature firms require evidence of control testing and back-test
  against incidents, which shows participants underestimate likelihood and overestimate impact. Variants:
  RCA (assessment must be evidenced) and RRSA (residual only). Purpose: awareness and prioritisation, not
  precise measurement. Results are signed off by business-line managers; action plans are owned by the first
  line and tracked by the second or third line. Mitigation need not mean more controls: reduce volumes, limit
  access, redesign controls, insure.
  • Scales and heatmap (ORR-4 b). Impact types: financial, regulatory, customer, reputation, often service
  continuity; four- or five-point scales, financial impact expressed relative to revenue or operating income so
  the scale fits units of any size. Likelihood as a percentage or “once in x years”: a 1-in-10-year event has
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D22  Operational Risk Identification, Mitigation and Reporting
  a 10% chance of occurring in the next year; RCSA horizons are one year at most. Heatmap colours
  encode appetite: green within, yellow approaching, orange above (action plan or escalated acceptance), red
  far beyond (urgent plan). Never multiply ratings: a frequent low-impact risk (1 × 4) is not a remote extreme
  one (4 × 1).
  • KRIs, KPIs, KCIs (S3; ORR-4 c). A KRI signals a rise or fall in the level of a risk, in likelihood (trans-
  actions per staff member, falling satisfaction scores, higher sales targets for fraud) or in impact (key-person
  knowledge, data sensitivity on a server, top-10 client concentration). A KPI measures performance (uptime,
  error rates, NPS, share within SLA). A KCI measures control effectiveness in design or performance (missed
  due diligence, lack of segregation of duties, errors missed in four-eyes checks, incomplete ID files). They
  overlap: a failing control is a KPI, a KRI and a KCI at once (overdue confirmations); “a KPI turning amber
  becomes a KRI”. Thresholds express appetite; Tier-1 banks reclassify an issue unresolved after about three
  months as a KRI.
  • Loss data: regulatory criteria (S4; ORR-4 a). BCBS lists eight criteria under the SA; main ones: 10 years
  of data; minimum threshold EUR 20,000; mapping to Basel event types; dates of occurrence and recovery;
  independent review of comprehensiveness and accuracy. Firms often use lower thresholds (commonly 1,000
  or 5,000); the choice must be justified, not cherry-picking. Standard fields (ID, place, event and cause types,
  failed controls, four dates, gross loss, recoveries, net loss = gross loss minus recovery, action plans, links);
  use drop-down labels, not free text, in key fields (NLP now mines old free text).
  • Loss data: what to record (ORR-4 a). Regulators require only direct financial losses; good practice adds
  non-financial impacts, accidental gains and near misses. Direct losses come from the event (remediation,
  compensation, fines); indirect losses from its consequences (attrition, morale, scrutiny, premiums), often
  rated 1 to 4 and monetised only in mature firms. Grouped losses with a common cause are one event (a
  regulatory requirement). Four dates: occurrence, discovery, reporting, accounting; occurrence to discovery
  shows visibility, discovery to reporting shows diligence; material incidents reported within days. Losses
  above USD 100 million at G-SIBs most often took three to five years from reporting to settlement.
  • Boundary events and completeness (ORR-4 a). Credit-related operational losses already in credit RWAs
  are excluded; those not in credit RWAs are included; market-related operational losses are treated as opera-
  tional risk. Completeness is checked against the general ledger (misses indirect effects and forgone revenue),
  P1/P2 IT logs, litigation provisions, complaints and compensation, press, back-office logs. Operational data
  are firm-specific, heavy-tailed, scarce, can stay hidden for years, and are complex to interpret.
  Identification and assessment beyond the specifics (ORR-3, ORR-4 d to g)
  • Top-down and bottom-up (ORR-3 a). Top-down: senior leadership, key risks to strategic objectives, two
  to four times a year; tools are exposures and vulnerabilities, the risk wheel (chains of cause and effect), and
  horizon scanning (WEF, risk.net, ORX reports, PESTLE, emerging risk committees). Bottom-up: local
  staff and processes; internal and external loss analysis (“could this happen to us?”), near misses (“lessons
  for free”, reported only in a no-blame culture), RCSA, process mapping. The two are consolidated, not
  reconciled. Interview the longest-serving staff and recent hires (the “amazement report”).
  • Extreme risk identification (ORR-3 b). Scenario analysis identifies low-probability, high-severity events;
  regulators require repeatable, documented, evidence-based results. Preparation pack: external and internal
  losses, near misses, RCSA, KRIs, audit issues, concentrations. Senior participants, ideally an external ex-
  pert, to counter myopia and the excessive focus on external causes (most large losses had internal causes).
  Generation (silent voting), selection (20 to 30 down to about 15 for a mid-size firm; 50 is common at inter-
  national banks), and comparison with ORX or ORIC lists only afterwards. Taxonomies and Basel Levels 1
  to 3 (ORR-3 c, d): see A22.
  • Factor models (ORR-4 d, e). Fault tree analysis: AND conditions (all must occur) and OR conditions; with
  independence the joint probability is the product, the theoretical minimum; dependence calls for conditional
  (Bayesian) probabilities; multiply by the units of exposure, not by impact. FAIR: asset, threat community,
  threat type, effect; distributions fed to Monte Carlo. Swiss cheese (Reason’s cumulative act model): loss
  only when holes in all layers align, so control independence matters as much as each control’s reliability.
  Bowtie: event in the centre, causes and preventive barriers on the left (“5-why”), impacts with detective and
  corrective barriers on the right (“5-then-what”). Capital modelling (ORR-4 f): see D19 and A17.
  • Operational resilience (ORR-4 g). BCM works by process and RTO; resilience by important business
  service and impact tolerance, the maximum tolerable disruption (FCA). Steps: identify IBS; set impact
  tolerances; map end to end; design severe but plausible scenarios; learn where tolerances are exceeded;
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D22  Operational Risk Identification, Mitigation and Reporting
  communication plans; annual self-assessment signed off by the board. Business impact analysis feeds toler-
  ances; single points of failure (key people, suppliers, systems) are resilience KRIs.
  Mitigation (ORR-5 a, d, e, f, g)
  • Risk responses (ORR-5 a). ISO 31000 four Ts: tolerate (low inherent or well-controlled residual risk),
  treat (controls and action plans), transfer (insurance, outsourcing: accountability not transferable, see D2),
  terminate (remove the exposure and its revenues; last resort). External operational risks cannot be pre-
  vented, only reduced; internal ones (people, processes, systems) are far more preventable through controls.
  Fees partly compensate the bank for the operational risk it bears for clients; operational risk cannot be fully
  eliminated, hence capital.
  • Human error and process design (ORR-5 d). Prevention through design; human error is a symptom of
  poor processes. Slips (involuntary: fix the environment and pace), rule-based mistakes (flawed rules or
  incentives, as mis-selling: change the rule), knowledge-based mistakes (new situations: directive controls,
  training, escalation), violations (deliberate: supervisory controls and culture). Lean Six Sigma removes
  waste (“DOWNTIME”) and variation through DMAIC; quality improvement uses PDSA.
  • New initiatives and M&A (ORR-5 e); limiting impact (ORR-5 f). NPAP/NIRAP: a business case with
  objective, alternatives, expected benefits, commercial aspects and risks; ORM involvement scaled to the
  project’s risk rating (workshops before kick-off, monitoring during, debrief at closure); acquired firms bring
  “skeletons in the closet” and integration risks. Contingency planning and BCM (senior commitment, BIA,
  RTO, regular testing); crisis qualities: speed, competence, transparency; a technical and a communication
  team under a senior manager; phases: crisis, emergency response, recovery (RPO: data lost since the last
  back-up; RTO: tolerable downtime), restoration.
  • Transfer and reputation (ORR-5 g). Insurance suits risks that are predictable (priceable) and transferable;
  fair premiums roughly equal expected loss, so large firms self-insure small losses (captives) and buy cover for
  tail events (cyber, business interruption, class actions). Under the SA, insurance recoveries reduce gross to net
  losses; the insurer’s capacity, willingness and payment delay leave residual and liquidity risk. Reputational
  damage cannot really be insured or outsourced: prevention plus the three Rs (regret, reason, remedy), rapid
  response, transparency and stakeholder differentiation; reputation capital is a buffer that supports resilience
  (Capital One 2019).
  Reporting (ORR-6 a to d)
  • Audiences (ORR-6 a). Board risk committee (effectiveness of the framework, appetite KRIs, large events,
  emerging risks); audit committee (third-line assurance on internal control); ExCo (execution of the ORM
  framework: events, action plans, culture and use-test indicators); central ORM function and operational risk
  committee (aggregated, cross-cutting view); business-line managers and risk champions (their own KRIs,
  plans and events, benchmarked). The “reporting cake”: tier 1 all you need to know to monitor, tier 2 to act,
  tier 3 to decide; high risks, near misses and defective key controls escalate unaltered.
  • Content and challenges (ORR-6 b, c). Seven components: top-10 risks and outlook; heatmap and register;
  risk appetite metrics; KRIs and issues; incidents and near misses (SA tracks losses above EUR 20,000 and
  EUR 100,000); action plans (“zero objective” for overdue plans and audit points: discipline indicators);
  emerging risks. More mature firms report more forward-looking content. Rules of valuable reporting: value
  exceeds cost, known use, influence on decisions. Asymmetry (ORX: losses above EUR 10 million are 0.4%
  of events and 66.6% of value), so isolate large events, “no averages” (use median and quartiles). Qualitative
  ratings are not additive: aggregate by conversion to money, by categorisation (“candle” bar charts), or worst-
  case (prudent but over-alarming). Combined assurance coordinates the three lines.
  • External reporting (ORR-6 d). Pillar 3 (updated 2018): qualitative description of ORM; ten years of
  historical losses by accounting date in prescribed templates; the business indicator and its subcomponents,
  with narrative. “Absence of evidence is evidence of absence”: minutes and reports prove governance. Notify
  regulators of events meeting materiality, reputation, resilience or stability criteria; be “open and honest”.
  Annual reports should look transparent and competent. UK firms had until March 2025 to map and test
  important business services within impact tolerances.

  Likely distractors
  • “Daily data back-ups and continuity plans are preventive controls, because they prevent the loss of data and
  service.”
  True version: they are corrective controls: they lessen the impact when an event occurs and do not change its
  likelihood. Sibling swap.
  • “A detective control can never act as a preventive control, since by definition it operates after an error has been
  recorded.”
  True version: a detective control becomes preventive when it detects a cause; a file reconciliation run before a
  transaction is executed prevents the error. Absolute claim.
  • “Replacing manual checks with an automated control lowers both the likelihood and the potential impact of
  control failures.”
  True version: automation converts high-likelihood, low-impact manual errors into low-likelihood, high-impact
  systemic breakdowns, and adds technology and model risk. Polarity flip.
  • “RCSA action plans for risks above appetite should be owned by the second line, since the risk function facilitated
  the workshop.”
  True version: action plans are owned by the first line (business-line managers), who sign off the RCSA results;
  the second or third line tracks them. Role misassignment.
  • “A 1-in-10-year event is expected about once over a ten-year horizon, so a ten-year horizon is the appropriate
  time frame for the RCSA.”
  True version: it means a 10% chance of occurring within the next year; RCSA horizons are one year at most.
  Scope/condition error.
  • “Operational losses connected to market risk, such as a booking error on a trading position, are excluded from
  the operational loss dataset and left in market risk.”
  True version: market-related operational losses are treated as operational risk for capital; it is credit-related
  operational losses already captured in credit RWAs that are excluded. Sibling swap (market versus credit bound-
  ary).
