 Revision notes
 • Risk capital (ORR-18 a): the cushion that protects against the firm’s risks so it remains a going concern
 even in a near-catastrophic worst-case scenario. Usually treated as identical to economic capital, but the
 chapter later defines economic capital = risk capital + strategic risk capital. Risk capital covers the worst-
 case loss minus EL at the required confidence level over one year (Figure 18.1: 165 bps − 15 bps = 150 bps
 of unexpected loss at 99%).
 • Regulatory capital versus risk capital (ORR-18 a): regulatory capital applies only to a few regulated indus-
 tries (banks, insurers), is set by industrywide rules and formulas, and sets only a minimum; it rarely captures
 the true level of risk, and may differ from risk capital business line by business line even when the firm totals
 are similar. Some practitioners allocate the higher of the two. Under Basel III, regulatory capital may end
 up much higher than economic capital for activities such as securitisation, which then tend to migrate to the
 shadow banking sector.
 • Who sets the parameters: the confidence level and horizon for risk capital are key policy parameters set
 by senior management (or the senior risk management committee) and usually endorsed by the board; the
 confidence level is tied to the target credit rating and the stated risk appetite.
 • Why economic capital matters to banks (Box 18.1): capital absorbs risk (debt-to-equity up to 20 to 1, and
 off-balance-sheet activities draw on risk capital); target solvency is part of the product sold to liability holders;
 banks are opaque, so capital reduces agency costs; capital is expensive, so neither too much nor too little.
 Uses: performance measurement and incentive compensation, entry/exit decisions (with the diversification
 effect), and risk-based pricing via the marginal economic capital requirement. One measure cannot serve all
 four purposes.
 • RAROC (ORR-18 b): proposed by Bankers Trust in the late 1970s; after-tax expected risk-adjusted net
 income / economic capital. It adjusts the numerator (subtracts EL) and the denominator (economic for
 accounting capital). RORAC adjusts only the denominator; RAROA uses risk-adjusted assets. A business
 unit with RAROC above the cost of the bank’s equity is deemed to add shareholder value.
 • Capital budgeting numerator (ORR-18 b, c): expected revenues − costs − expected losses − taxes +
 return on risk capital ± transfers. EL is priced into the spread over funding cost, so no risk capital is held
 for it, yet it is still subtracted in the numerator. The return on risk capital assumes the capital is invested in
 risk-free securities. Transfers include treasury funding charges, hedging costs and head-oﬀice overhead.
 • Strategic risk capital (ORR-18 b): goodwill (the premium paid above the replacement value of net assets
 in an acquisition) + burned-out capital (capital spent starting up a business that may not be kicked off), both
 amortised over time. Some banks also allocate capital to unused risk limits.

 • EVA or NIACC (Box 18.2): after-tax adjusted net income less a capital charge equal to economic capital
 times the after-tax cost of equity. An activity whose RAROC is above the hurdle rate is also EVA positive.
 • Ex ante versus ex post (ORR-18 c): RAROC was first an anticipatory capital budgeting tool on expected
 revenues and losses; for ex post performance evaluation, realized revenues and losses may be used.
 • Horizon (ORR-18 d): all inputs on one horizon; practitioners use one year (business planning cycle; time
 to recapitalise after a major unexpected loss). A longer horizon does not necessarily raise capital, because
 the required confidence in solvency decreases as the horizon is extended. Market

 the core risk level over the time to reduce, assuming an orderly unwind, not 252 (Figure 18B.1: 52.8% of

 • PIT versus TTC PD (ORR-18 d): point-in-time PD (KMV, structural) suits near-term EL and pricing;
 through-the-cycle PD (rating agencies) suits economic capital, current profitability and strategic decisions,
 and reduces the volatility of economic capital. Confidence level: AA target implies a one-year PD of 3 to 5
 bps, i.e. 99.95% to 99.97%; a lower confidence level can significantly reduce risk capital, especially where
 operational, credit and settlement risks dominate.
 • Hurdle rate (ORR-18 e): most firms use a single hurdle rate, the after-tax weighted-average cost of equity
 capital (common and preferred, at market values; common via CAPM; preferred = yield on preferred shares).
 Reset periodically, say every six months, or when it changes by more than 10%. Flaw: one hurdle can lead
 the firm to accept high-risk projects (oil exploration) that lower value and reject low-risk ones (well-managed
 retail banking) that raise it.
 • Adjusted RAROC (ORR-18 f, Box 18.5): ARAROC = RAROC −βE (RM − rf ), stripping out the com-
 pensation for nondiversifiable systematic risk (shareholders assumed well diversified). Accept if ARAROC
 > rf : the hurdle becomes the risk-free rate, the same for all business lines.
 • Diversification (ORR-18 g): firm risk capital < sum of stand-alone capital. No fully integrated VaR model
 exists, so banks run bottom-up models at a lower business-level confidence (e.g. 99.5% versus 99.97%);
 the aggregate lies between the zero-correlation root sum of squares (USD 787 in the book) and the perfect-
 correlation simple sum (USD 1,200). The portfolio effect is usually allocated pro rata to stand-alone capital
 (USD 46 and 54 in Figure 18.2). Stand-alone capital for incentive compensation; fully diversified for solvency
 and minimum risk pricing; marginal for active portfolio management and business mix. Marginal capitals
 sum to less than the BU total (70 versus 100).
 • Best practices (ORR-18 h): (1) senior management commitment (CEO sponsorship; “not how much income
 is generated, but how well the firm is compensated for the risks”); (2) communication and education; (3)
 ongoing consultation via a “parameter review group” (credit parameters over the cycle; market volatilities
 and correlations at least monthly; core risk level and time to reduce annually); (4) integrity of the process (the
 RAROC group owns collection, calculation and reporting; business units and finance own the data they feed
 in); (5) combine RAROC with qualitative factors on the strategic grid (“managed growth” = high return,
 low strategic importance; “investment” = low return, high growth potential); (6) a quarterly active capital
 management process, disagreements arbitrated by the senior executive team. Business units complain capital
 is too high (never too low) and too unstable; the acrimony is over parameters, not the VaR methods.
 • Loan RAROC in Doumpos (CR-5 g): RAROC = loan revenues / capital at risk, with loan revenues (s + f −
 l −c)(1−x). Capital at risk is either the duration-based value change ∆L = −LD ∆i/(1+i) (market-based
 approach) or the unexpected loan loss α × LGD × EAD, with α = 2.6σ at 99.5% under normality (5 to 6
 for skewed losses). The loan is profitable if RAROC exceeds the bank’s cost of capital. No return-on-capital
 term: do not mix it with the ORR-18 form.

 Exam traps
 • Return on risk capital (dropped term or sign): it is added (capital sits in risk-free securities). Dropping it or
 subtracting it each moves the answer by one option; the book taxes it with the rest of the numerator.
 • Wrong denominator twin: regulatory capital, a short-horizon trading VaR, or risk capital without goodwill
 and burned-out capital instead of economic capital; EL never goes into the denominator.
 • Polarity of components (the map’s favourite): higher costs, funding charges or EL lower RAROC; a lower
 confidence level means less capital and a higher RAROC for a profitable unit; “EL has no effect because no
 capital is held for it” is a scope error.
 • Hurdle construction: book instead of market weights, a simple average, βRM instead of β(RM − rf ), adding
 debt (a WACC), or grossing up for tax (the hurdle is already after tax).
 • ARAROC benchmark: compare ARAROC with rf , not with the old hurdle and not with zero. Subtracting
 the whole CAPM cost of equity gives RAROC minus the hurdle, which is compared with zero.
 • Horizon mismatch: multi-year deal totals over one year of capital. Annualise every numerator item.
