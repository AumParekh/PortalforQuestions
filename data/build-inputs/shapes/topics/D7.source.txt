  Revision notes
  • EWIs as a dashboard (LTR-3 a): a turn signal is an intentional risky activity (running a deposit special to
  raise rate-sensitive liabilities); a low oil light means action is needed soon (the LCR has dropped below a
  threshold); a check engine light calls for investigation (a dramatic rise in call center volumes). “EWI triggers
  are used to initiate management discussions and actions that should be formally documented.”
  • Beyond compliance (LTR-3 c): holding liquid assets is a cost-benefit decision; a “set and forget” buffer
  cannot manage risk against a dynamic market. Increasing buffers before stress extends the survival horizon
  cost-effectively, and a strong balance sheet in stress opens market share and acquisition opportunities.
  • BCBS 2008 Sound Principles (LTR-3 b, Table 3.1): EWIs may include rapid asset growth funded with
  potentially volatile liabilities; growing concentrations; increases in currency mismatches; a decrease in the
  weighted average maturity of liabilities; repeated incidents of approaching or breaching limits; negative
  trends in a product line; significant deterioration in financial condition; negative publicity; a rating down-
  grade; stock price declines; rising debt costs; widening debt or CDS spreads; rising wholesale or retail
  funding costs; counterparties requesting collateral or resisting new transactions; a drop in credit lines; in-
  creasing retail deposit outflows; early CD redemptions; difficulty accessing longer-term funding or placing
  short-term liabilities.
  • OCC 2012 and SR 10-6 (LTR-3 b, Table 3.2): the OCC wants EWIs that signal whether embedded triggers
  in products (callable public debt, OTC derivatives) are about to be breached or contingent risks are likely to
  materialise, e.g. reluctance of traditional fund providers, pending regulatory action, CAMELS downgrades,
  rising funding costs in an otherwise stable market, elimination of committed credit lines. The 2010 Intera-
  gency Statement (SR 10-6): tailor indicators to the institution’s specific liquidity risk profile and move into
  “progressive states of readiness” as an event evolves. BCBS 2012 adds intraday monitoring indicators (see
  D27).
  • Where EWIs sit (LTR-3 c, FRB SR 12-7): the framework must be end-to-end. Risks inherent in business
  activities are identified, recorded in a liquidity risk inventory, assessed by the businesses, treasury, indepen-
  dent risk management and others, then measured and monitored through stress tests, EWIs, and a limit and
  response framework, linked to the funding playbook.
  • M.E.R.I.T. (LTR-3 a): Measures, Escalation, Reporting, Integrated systems, Thresholds. Measures without
  escalation are “a mere academic exercise”.
  • Measures (LTR-3 a): BCBS Principle 5 asks for metrics that assess the structure of the balance sheet, project
  cash flows and future liquidity positions, and take into account off-balance sheet risks, spanning business-
  as-usual and stressed conditions over various horizons.
  • Leading and sharp (LTR-3 a, S1): a leading indicator signals stress before the event; lagging indicators
  such as government-reported GDP report what has already happened, so build proxies from internal loan
  portfolio metrics. Sharpness is granularity: total deposit balances is acceptable, but deposits of volatile

  segments (high-net-worth, rate-sensitive products) are sharper. Sharp, leading EWIs give management more
  time to act.
  • Internal and external (LTR-3 a): balance internal measures (customised to the balance sheet; they catch
  internally driven stress before it becomes public, e.g. loan performance, planned accounting adjustments,
  operational losses) with external ones that signal systemic change (sovereign default, banking crisis).
  • Environments and horizons (LTR-3 a): track EWIs in business as usual and add stressed measures and
  limits; typical cycles are daily, weekly or monthly. “An indicator that is not updated for a period of more than
  a month is unlikely to satisfy the ‘early’ aspect”; some are intraday.
  • Escalation (LTR-3 c, S2): breaches go to the level of management with authority to act. Modest responses
  (increasing the liquid asset buffer) usually sit within the corporate treasurer’s delegation; a signal of a poten-
  tially large cash outflow may need ALCO to authorise a check on asset growth; the most extreme cases may
  require the contingency funding plan. Escalation governance must be formalised, documented and included
  in the CFP (see D26).
  • Reporting and integrated systems (LTR-3 a): EWI dashboards are commonly daily, with leaders moving to
  intraday for some measures (especially trading-focused firms); reporting must be broad enough for coverage
  yet specific enough to carry only key messages. Stressed and non-stressed metrics should come from the
  same source system.
  • Thresholds (LTR-3 a, S1): green, amber, red. Bands should not be so wide that movements go undetected,
  nor so narrow that constant investigation desensitises management (“boy who cried wolf”). Calibrate on
  historical volatility over a series long enough to be significant, recent enough to be relevant, ideally including
  a stress period such as 2007 to 2008 (at least one year when available); back-test and allow a judgment-based
  management override (e.g. the FOMC target range).
  • Scoring versus rating (CR-6 a): a credit score is a numerical indicator from automated models, solely for
  internal use; a credit rating is an ordinal grade, each grade tied to an empirically estimated PD, often public,
  and may combine analytical and judgmental assessment. Types: internal versus external; issuer versus issue-
  specific; behavioral and profit scoring.
  • PIT versus TTC (CR-6 b, S3): Basel distinguishes two philosophies. TTC assessments are “primarily of
  interest for major credit rating agencies”, have a long-term orientation covering at least one business cycle,
  are robust to short-term changes and updated less often. PIT assessments are “usually employed by internal
  systems developed by credit institutions and banks”, focus on the current and short-term condition (usually
  no more than one year), are more volatile and updated promptly, and suit short-term transactions and loans.
  Research on their relative predictive power is mixed; default is easier to define PIT, and extending to TTC is
  hard because the time until default must be modeled.
  • Procyclicality (CR-6 d, ORR-18 d, S4): Crouhy: a PIT PD (KMV and structural approaches) suits near-term
  EL and pricing; a TTC PD (largely the agencies’ approach) suits economic capital, profitability and strategic
  decisions. A firm is less likely to stay in the same rating under PIT, so “the TTC approach therefore reduces
  the volatility of economic capital”; compare both in RAROC at a normal and at the worst point of the cycle.
  Calibrating parameters on a shorter period makes capital “more procyclical (capital goes down when the
  credit environment improves and goes up when it deteriorates)”. Doumpos: agencies claim TTC ratings,
  yet critics found them too optimistic in growth and too pessimistic in recessions, i.e. pro-cyclical. ORR-7:
  capital, regulatory or economic, is a TTC concept; stress testing is a PIT exercise.
  Exam traps
  • Polarity flip, PIT versus TTC: PIT is the volatile, promptly updated, more procyclical philosophy; TTC is
  stable and reduces economic capital volatility.
  • Role misassignment, who uses which: PIT belongs to banks’ internal systems and short-term loans; TTC to
  the major rating agencies (and to economic capital).
  • Absolute claim on accuracy: research on PIT versus TTC predictive power is mixed; the choice depends on
  the scope of the analysis.
  • Sibling swap, leading versus lagging: GDP releases are the book’s example of a lagging indicator; EWIs
  should be leading and sharp.
  • Absolute claim on thresholds: neither “as narrow as possible” (boy who cried wolf) nor so wide that move-

  ments go undetected.
  • Role misassignment in escalation: treasurer for modest buffer moves, ALCO for asset-growth checks after a
  large-outflow signal, CFP for the extreme.
