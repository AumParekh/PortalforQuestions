  Revision notes
  • Not a domestic bank run (LTR-15 a): the open-economy run story (depositors convert domestic deposits
  into foreign currency and exhaust the country’s FX reserves) still fits emerging markets, but the GFC dollar
  shortage came from the international operations of the major banking systems and the global interbank and
  FX swap markets they relied on.
  • Balance sheet expansion (LTR-15 a): banks’ foreign claims grew from USD 10 trillion (2000) to USD
  34 trillion (end-2007), year-on-year growth near 30% by mid-2007, driven by structured finance, universal
  banking and prime brokerage to hedge funds. Swiss banks’ foreign claims rose from about five to more than
  seven times Swiss GDP. US dollar and other non-euro positions were more than half of European banks’
  foreign asset growth.
  • Three ways to fund a foreign asset (LTR-15 a): (1) borrow domestic currency and buy the asset spot (no
  later FX need, but unhedged currency risk); (2) borrow domestic currency and convert it with an FX swap;
  (3) borrow the foreign currency (interbank, non-banks, central banks). The second leg of (2) “is not that
  different” from (3): in both, the bank must deliver foreign currency when the contract matures. Funding risk
  is “the risk that funding liabilities cannot be rolled over”; a foreign currency funding gap exists when the
  investment horizon of foreign currency assets exceeds the maturity of the foreign currency funding or swaps.
  • Why foreign currency is special (LTR-15 a, c): at home a central bank can be lender of last resort, but
  “central banks cannot create foreign currencies”; their help is limited by the exchange rate regime or by FX
  reserves.
  • The long dollar book (LTR-15 a): Japanese and major European banking systems ran large net long posi-
  tions in foreign currencies, mostly US dollars, mirrored by net borrowing in their own currency from home
  residents and bridged by FX swaps (78% of FX swap turnover is under seven days). European banks’ long
  dollar positions reached about USD 700 billion by mid-2007 (USD 1.3 trillion with Japanese banks). Spanish
  banks were the exception: short dollars, funding euro lending at home.
  • Measuring the gap (LTR-15 a, b): counterparty type proxies maturity (interbank short, non-bank claims
  the desired investment portfolio). Lower bound: net dollar claims on non-banks, USD 1.0 to 1.2 trillion for
  European banks by mid-2007, met by interbank borrowing (USD 432 billion), central bank deposits (USD
  386 billion) and FX swaps (USD 315 billion). With money market fund paper (about USD 1 trillion) treated
  as short-term: USD 2.0 to 2.2 trillion; all non-bank liabilities short-term: USD 6.5 trillion.
  • How it turned into a shortage (LTR-15 a): from August 2007 counterparty and liquidity concerns compro-
  mised interbank funding; FX swap dislocations made swapped dollars dearer; after Lehman, money market
  funds facing redemptions withdrew from bank paper and central banks withdrew reserve placements (de-
  posits fell USD 257 billion). Assets could not be shed: structured products were hard to sell, off-balance-
  sheet vehicles came back on balance sheet and credit lines were drawn, so dollar claims even rose USD 248
  billion (3%) to Q1 2008. “The effective holding period of assets lengthened just as the maturity of funding
  shortened.” This endogenous rise in maturity mismatch, “difficult to hedge ex ante”, generated the shortage.
  • Crowded trade (LTR-15 a): “What pushed the system to the brink was not cross-currency funding per se,
  but rather too many large banks employing funding strategies in the same direction, the funding equivalent
  of a crowded trade.”

  • Consolidated, not residency, view (LTR-15 b): “bank nationality” and “bank residency” differ. Positions
  booked in one country are a small part of a banking system’s global balance sheet, yet can be large relative
  to that host country’s external position (foreign banks’ offices held nearly 40% of UK external assets; only
  18% of Swiss banks’ foreign claims are booked in Switzerland). A mismatch booked in one office may be
  hedged in another, so vulnerabilities “can only be measured by taking into account the entire balance sheet
  of the consolidated global entity”.
  • Policy response (LTR-15 c): Fed swap lines with the ECB and SNB from December 2007; doubled after
  Lehman with new lines to the Bank of Canada, Bank of England and Bank of Japan (USD 247 billion),
  then extended across continents; unlimited for the BoE, ECB and SNB from 13 October 2008. The Fed
  acted as international lender of last resort, lending to central banks against foreign currency collateral; they
  auctioned the dollars locally, reaching banks with no US subsidiary or eligible collateral. The Fed “assumes
  no credit risk vis-a-vis the ultimate borrowers” and delegates monitoring to national authorities, so the design
  overcomes the resource and moral hazard problems of international lending of last resort. Euro area, Swiss
  and UK FX reserves were only USD 294 billion, an order of magnitude below the gap.
  • Effectiveness (LTR-15 c): usage surged to a USD 583 billion peak in December 2008 and fell to USD 50
  billion by early October 2009; Libor levels and spreads receded, auctions lowered the level and volatility of
  swap spreads and averted wider distress selling of dollar assets. Caveat: public dollar funding “may have
  delayed the necessary restructuring” of balance sheets.
  • CIP and the basis (LTR-16 c): CIP “is the closest thing to a physical law in international finance”. The
  cross-currency basis is the amount by which the interest paid to borrow a currency by swapping it against
  another differs from borrowing it directly in the cash market; a non-zero basis is a CIP violation. Since 2007
  the basis for lending US dollars against the euro and yen has been negative: borrowing dollars through the
  FX swap market became more expensive than in the dollar cash market. For the Australian dollar it has been
  positive.
  • FX swap versus cross-currency swap (LTR-16 a): FX swap: one party borrows one currency and lends
  another; amounts exchanged at spot S, repaid at the pre-agreed forward F ; the implicit return is set by
  F − S and quoted in forward points; mostly short-dated. Cross-currency swap: longer-term (typically above
  one year); periodic interest exchanges during the life (Libor plus the basis b in a basis swap); principal re-
  exchanged at the initial spot rate S.
  • What moves the basis (LTR-16 b): market illiquidity (bid-ask between forward and spot), credit risk in
  the underlying (Libor-OIS spreads, sovereign CDS), a risk premium rp, and FX hedging demand scaled by
  per-dollar balance sheet costs. Three hedgers, all insensitive to the basis: banks (structural; Japanese banks
  fund dollar lending with yen deposits via swaps), institutional investors (Japanese life insurers hedge 60 to
  70%), and non-financial issuers (“reverse yankee” euro bonds by US firms swapped into dollars). Where
  banks compete with insurers for swapped dollars (Japan, euro area) the basis is negative; where banks lend
  dollars in swaps and offset investors (Australia; Sweden against the euro) it is positive.
  • Why it stopped closing after 2014 (LTR-16 c): first-generation explanation: counterparty risk and dollar
  funding strains (GFC, euro debt crisis). The puzzle is persistence after 2014 with healthy banks: hedging
  demand plus tighter limits to arbitrage. “Balance sheet space is rented, not free”: CVA charges, PFE add-ons
  in Basel III and leverage ratios, quarter-end spikes, the shift to repo funding (BoJ purchases made yen collat-
  eral scarce), internal limits on Japanese official sector exposure, and a slowdown in supranational issuance
  arbitrage after the USD swap spread inverted in Q3 2015. ECB and BoJ easing announcements widened it.
  Japanese banks’ own reliance on swaps reduced their capacity to arbitrage. “CIP deviations look to be here
  to stay.”

  Exam traps
  • Sign of the basis (polarity flip): a negative EUR or JPY basis means dollars are dearer through the swap market
  than in the cash market. (Box 17.1 writes the same premium as a positive b paid on the dollar leg; market quotes
  put it on the non-dollar leg, outside-source.)
  • FX swap versus cross-currency swap (sibling swap): re-exchange at the forward F , no interim interest, short-
  dated, versus re-exchange at the initial spot S, periodic interest, typically above one year.
  • Long versus short dollars (polarity flip): the shortage hit banks that were long dollar assets funded short-term;
  Spanish banks’ short dollar book is the exception, not the mechanism.
  • Who can create dollars (role misassignment): home central banks cannot; the Fed supplied them through
  swap lines to central banks, which lent on and bore the credit risk of the ultimate borrowers.
  • Post-2014 cause (scope/condition error): credit risk explains the GFC episodes, but the basis persisted when
  Libor-OIS normalised; hedging demand meeting balance sheet constraints is why it does not close.
  • Residency data (scope): a country’s national balance sheet can show a “mismatch” that is hedged elsewhere
  in the bank, and hide one that is booked abroad.
