  Revision notes
  • The specific: the loss waterfall (CR-18 f, g; S1). Gregory’s typical waterfall (Figure 18.9), in order: (1)
  initial margin of the defaulter; (2) default fund contribution of the defaulter; (3) CCP skin-in-the-game (its
  own equity); (4) default fund of the non-defaulting members; (5) rights of assessment and/or other loss
  allocation methods; (6) remaining CCP capital; (7) liquidity support, or the CCP fails. Steps 1 and 2 are
  defaulter pays; from step 4 onward it is survivors pay.
  • Skin in the game: placed after the defaulter’s resources and before the mutualised fund to give the CCP
  “some incentive to avoid losses beyond the defaulter-pays resources”. It is not in all rule books: in 2014 the
  Korea Exchange mutualised a USD 4.3 million loss to members’ default fund without itself losing anything,
  because its rules put its own loss after the roughly USD 200 million default fund.
  • Beyond the default fund (CR-18 g): rights of assessment are an unfunded obligation to top up a depleted
  fund, typically invoked if a significant fraction (e.g. 25%) is used, and generally capped because unlimited
  calls create moral hazard and hit members at the worst time. Variation margin gains haircutting (VMGH),
  probably the most common alternative, cuts the gains owed to members; it mimics bilateral economics and
  is guaranteed to work if the auction clears at mid-market. Tear-up terminates opposite trades to restore
  a matched book; forced allocation makes members accept positions at CCP prices (cannot be passed to
  clients). Tear-up and forced allocation are potentially infinite.
  • Initial margin of survivors is not in the waterfall: EMIR Article 45(4) bars a CCP from using the margins
  of non-defaulting members; initial margin haircutting has been suggested but not used. A member can still
  lose through the default fund “even if it never traded with the defaulted counterparty, has no net position with
  the CCP”, or is positioned like the defaulter.
  • What a CCP is (CR-18 a): a financial market infrastructure interposed between buyers and sellers. Historical
  path: direct clearing (payment of difference), clearing rings, then complete clearing (from 1891). CBOT
  resisted until 1925 and the LME until 1986, because clearing homogenises counterparty risk. Clearable OTC
  products need standardisation, low complexity, liquidity, no wrong-way risk and enough volume; CDSs are
  arguably the hardest because of WWR.
  • Novation (CR-18 b): the original contract is replaced, the CCP becomes “buyer to the seller and seller to
  the buyer”, and the original parties have no counterparty risk to one another. The CCP runs a matched book
  with no net market risk but has conditional market risk if a member defaults.
  • Netting, multilateral offset, compression (CR-18 c): in Gregory’s three-party example, total liabilities of
  180 shrink and C’s liability nets to 60, removing the domino path from C to A. Compression needs actual
  cash flows or trades to be eliminated; a CCP can offset risk even between near-identical trades. Compression
  of cleared trades still cuts margin multipliers, leverage-ratio capital, operations and auction size.
  • Margin and default funds (CR-18 d): variation margin daily or intraday, usually cash only; initial margin
  covers close-out losses at 99% or more over the MPoR (five days for OTC CCPs versus a minimum of 10 days
  bilaterally; one or two days for exchange-traded), may be securities. The default fund is sized by stress tests to
  Cover 1 (largest member and affiliates), or Cover 2 for systemically important or complex CCPs (SwapClear:
  largest two), and allocated simply, e.g. pro rata to initial margin, updated perhaps monthly.

  • CCP risk management (CR-18 e): membership requirements (creditworthiness, liquidity, operational abil-
  ity), margin as the primary defence (“unlike a bank, which relies largely on capital”), and default manage-
  ment: macro-hedging then auctions by sub-portfolio, run by a default management group with seconded
  member traders and rehearsed in fire drills. Lehman 2008 at SwapClear: 90% of risk hedged in one week,
  66,000 trades auctioned within three weeks, about a third of the initial margin used.
  • Bilateral versus central (CR-18 h, Table 18.2): counterparty is the original party versus the CCP; all prod-
  ucts versus standard, vanilla, liquid ones; bilateral netting versus multilateral netting; bespoke margin versus
  full collateralisation including initial margin; bilateral close-out versus a coordinated default process; loss
  absorbency cost mainly capital versus mainly initial margin and default fund.
  • Initial margin versus default fund (CR-18 i, Table 18.3): higher initial margin and a lower default fund
  mean higher cost, clients paying for their own risk, easier portability and lower moral hazard; a larger mutu-
  alised default fund is cheaper and gives far greater loss coverage per unit contributed, but raises moral hazard
  and makes portability harder. Clients post margin but do not contribute to the default fund.
  • Client clearing: omnibus segregation protects a client from the clearing member’s house losses but exposes
  it to fellow-customer risk; individual segregation protects against both but costs more.
  • Advantages and disadvantages (CR-18 j): transparency, multilateral offset, loss mutualisation, legal and
  operational efficiency, liquidity, orderly default management; against these, moral hazard, adverse selection,
  bifurcation of cleared and non-cleared netting sets, procyclicality, and the CCP as a single point of failure.
  Clearing will not kill CVA: about 85% of a bank’s CVA comes from exempt corporates and governments.

  Exam traps
  • Sequence swap: skin-in-the-game sits after the defaulter’s initial margin and default fund contribution and
  before the mutualised fund. Moving it after the mutualised fund overstates the survivors’ loss.
  • Defaulter’s own default fund first: the defaulter’s default fund contribution is used before anyone else’s, not
  pooled pro rata with the survivors’.
  • Role misassignment: non-defaulting members’ initial margin is never in the waterfall (EMIR 45(4)); their
  default fund contributions are.
  • Scope error: mutualised losses do not depend on having traded with the defaulter.
  • Absolute claim: rights of assessment are generally capped; only tear-up and forced allocation are potentially
  unlimited, so “the CCP can never fail” and “a member’s loss is limited to its default fund contribution” are both
  false in general.
