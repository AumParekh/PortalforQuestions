  Revision notes
  • Expected shortfall (MR-1 c): “the probability-weighted average of tail losses”. For standard normal losses
  the 95% VaR is 1.645 and the 95% ES is 2.063. ES is “the easiest” coherent risk measure to estimate.
  • Which observations enter the tail (MR-1 a, c; S1): with n observations and confidence level α, the tail
  holds (1 − α)n losses and the VaR is the (1 − α)n + 1 th highest loss, “the highest loss observation outside
  the tail”. With 1,000 observations the 95% VaR is the 51st highest loss (1.704) and the ES is “the average of
  the 50 highest losses” (2.196). With 100 observations the 95% VaR is the 6th largest loss and the 96% VaR
  the 5th largest, so the 95% ES averages the 5 largest losses.
  • Dowd’s footnote on the boundary: taking the VaR as the 50th, the 51st or a mix of the two is “just an issue
  of approximation”; the convention adopted throughout is the highest loss outside the tail. Gregory (CR-14)
  words ES as the average loss “equal to or above” VaR; for the MR-1 numerical, follow Dowd and leave the
  VaR observation out.
  • Average-of-tail-VaRs method (MR-1 c, d; S2): slice the tail into n equal-probability slices, estimate the
  n − 1 tail VaRs at confidence levels above α, and average them. With n = 10 at 95%: nine VaRs from 95.5%
  to 99.5%, average 2.0250 against a true 2.063. The estimate rises with n and converges (2.0513 at n = 50,
  2.0626 at n = 10,000): coarse slicing understates ES.
  R1
  • Coherent risk measures (MR-1 d): Mϕ = 0 ϕ(p)qp dp, a weighted average of loss quantiles. ES sets
  ϕ(p) = 1/(1 − α) for p ≥ α and 0 otherwise; spectral measures (e.g. exponential, γ = 0.05) put rising
  weights on higher quantiles and need far more slices to converge (judged by the halving error). An existing
  VaR engine needs only “the last few lines of code” changed to produce them.
  • Standard errors (MR-1 e): quantile standard errors rise as the quantile moves into the tail and fall with
  n. Yamai and Yoshiba found ES estimators have much bigger standard errors than VaR for heavy-tailed
  distributions (“prone to the effects of large but infrequent losses”); Acerbi found the two roughly comparable
  in empirically realistic ranges.
  • Bootstrapped HS (MR-2 a; S1): resample with replacement; each resample gives a VaR and an ES (“the
  average of the losses in each resample exceeding the resample VaR”); the best estimate is the mean of the
  resample estimates (1.669 and 2.114 against basic HS 1.704 and 2.196). The bootstrapped ES histogram is
  better behaved than the VaR histogram because ES is an average of tail VaRs; 90% bootstrap interval for ES
  [1.986, 2.271].
  • Age-weighted HS (BRW, MR-2 c): w(i) = λi−1 (1 − λ)/(1 − λn ); traditional HS is the special case λ → 1;
  more responsive to recent large losses, reduces ghost effects, but shrinks the effective sample size and is ad
  hoc.

  • Volatility-weighted HS (Hull and White, MR-2 c): rt,i  = (σT,i /σt,i ) rt,i ; returns are scaled up when current
  volatility exceeds the volatility at the time; VaR and ES can exceed the largest historical loss. Correlation-
  weighted HS adjusts returns through Choleski matrices of the historical and current correlation matrices.
  • Filtered historical simulation (MR-2 c; S3): a semi-parametric bootstrap. (1) Fit an asymmetric GARCH
  (AGARCH, captures the leverage effect); (2) divide realised returns by the model’s volatility forecasts to
  get standardised, iid returns; (3) bootstrap these and multiply each by the AGARCH forecast of tomorrow’s
  volatility; (4) read VaR off the simulated losses. It can exceed the maximum historical loss, keeps the corre-
  lation structure for multi-asset portfolios, and “can also be extended easily to allow for the estimation of ES”.
  Pritsker’s concern that FHS VaR may not pay enough attention to extremes has the partial response: use ES
  instead of VaR.
  • ES as the more informative measure (MR-2 c): after a crash, an equal-weight HS VaR may not move except
  at very high confidence, but “the increase in risk would show up in ES estimates just after the first shock”.
  • Non-parametric pros and cons (MR-2 d): no distributional assumption (fat tails, skewness accommodated),
  easy to implement and explain, no covariance matrices; but results depend completely on the historical data
  set, are constrained by the largest loss, slow to reflect shifts, and subject to ghost effects. Rule of thumb: at
  least 500 daily observations.
  • Coherence and subadditivity (ORR-19 a, CR-14 j; S4): the four conditions are monotonicity, positive
  homogeneity, translation invariance and subadditivity (“the risk measure of two portfolios, if combined, is
  always smaller or equal to the sum of the risk measures of the two individual portfolios”). VaR is subad-
  ditive for elliptical distributions such as the normal, but not for very skewed losses, asymmetric copulas, or
  independent but very heavy-tailed risk factors; the gap matters more for credit and operational risk. ES is
  coherent, so capital allocation and limits for sub-portfolios stay consistent with the portfolio measure.
  • FRTB (MR-18 a, b): stressed ES at 97.5% replaces 99% VaR and stressed VaR. For normal losses, 99%
  VaR = µ + 2.326σ and 97.5% ES = µ + 2.338σ; with a heavier tail the 97.5% ES “can be considerably
  greater” than the 99% VaR. Liquidity horizons 10, 20, 40, 60, 120 days; overlapping 10-day changes; cascade
  formula

  0.5 ESP,j . See also C2 for FRTB backtesting.

  Exam traps
  • Off-by-one on tail membership (S1): at 95% with 100 losses the tail is 5 losses. Averaging 4 gives the 96%
  ES; averaging 6 drags the VaR observation into the tail.
  • Including the VaR row (S2): in a tail-VaR table the ES at α averages only the VaRs at confidence levels above
  α; and divide by the number of tail VaRs (n − 1), not by the number of slices n.
  • Bootstrap intermediate result: the best bootstrap ES is the mean across resamples; one resample’s ES is only
  an intermediate number.
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  B2  Expected Shortfall and Coherence
  • Normal shortcut misapplied (sibling swap): “97.5% ES ≈ 99% VaR” holds for the normal only; for a heavy-
  tailed book the ES is larger.
  • Scope error on subadditivity: VaR is subadditive for elliptical distributions; claiming it for skewed or heavy-
  tailed losses, or claiming ES fails subadditivity, are the two corruptions.
  • FHS sibling swap: FHS (like Hull and White) can produce VaR and ES beyond the largest historical loss; only
  basic HS is capped by it. FHS scales by tomorrow’s forecast volatility, not the volatility of the day drawn.
