  Revision notes
  • The specific: sovereign CDS as a default measure (CR-8 g; S1). Assuming away counterparty and liquidity
  risk, CDS prices “should provide us with updated measures of default risk in the reference entity”, adjusting
  to current information where ratings are updated infrequently (Greece 2006 to 2010: ratings stagnant until
  late 2009 while CDS and default spreads moved monthly).
  • What the evidence says about CDS: (1) changes in CDS spreads lead changes in sovereign bond yields
  and in sovereign ratings; (2) evidence is emerging that CDS spread changes are better predictors of sovereign
  default than ratings; (3) sovereign CDS spreads rise with economic policy uncertainty, together with currency
  depreciation; (4) CDS prices cluster (emerging market cluster 1: internal correlation 0.516 versus 0.210
  external).
  • Limits of CDS: counterparty and liquidity risk move prices for reasons unrelated to default (much of the
  late-2008 surge followed Lehman’s failure), and the narrow market makes single names prone to liquidity
  squeezes. Damodaran’s verdict: CDS changes provide information, “albeit noisy”, but “there is little to
  indicate that it is superior to market default spreads (obtained from government bonds)”.
  • How a sovereign CDS works (CR-8 g): the buyer pays the spread on the notional; on a credit event (failure
  to pay, restructuring, bankruptcy) the seller pays par for the bond (physical) or par minus market price (cash).
  Colombia example: USD 10 million at 250 bps costs USD 250,000 a year. Two gaps in the protection: no
  credit event means no payment even if the bond price collapses, and the guarantee is only as good as the
  seller. Sovereign CDS are a small slice of a market dominated by corporates, then banks; few players. See
  also A15 and D14 for CDS mechanics.
  • Sovereign default spread (CR-8 b, g): yield on a government’s foreign-currency (e.g. US dollar) bond
  minus the riskless rate in that currency (Brazil, July 2022: 6.19% − 3.02% = 3.17%). It needs a default-free
  security in the same currency; local currency bonds of different governments cannot be compared because
  rate differences reflect expected inflation. Advantages over ratings: more granular (Colombia and Indonesia
  both Baa2, spreads 2.56% and 1.36%) and dynamic (Brazil 3.18% and Venezuela 3.09% in December 2005,
  1.32% and 10.26% by December 2009). Costs: more volatile, moved by liquidity and investor demand.
  • Spreads versus ratings: default spreads correlate with ratings and with actual default; the bond market
  leads the agencies (spreads widen before downgrades and narrow before upgrades); a rating change is still
  an informational event with a price impact, so ratings are not useless.
  • Foreign versus local currency defaults (CR-8 c): foreign currency default is understandable (the borrower
  cannot print the currency). Local currency defaults happen because of the gold standard (pre-1971 limits on
  printing), a shared currency (Greece 2015 could not print euros), and a deliberate trade-off against debase-
  ment and inflation, especially where companies have foreign currency debt funding local assets. Russia’s
  USD 39 billion ruble default and Brazil’s USD 62 billion in 1990 are the large cases; countries increasingly
  default on both at once.
  • History: defaults bunch in eight periods since 1820; Latin America is the epicentre (58 of 81 government
  defaults in 1820 to 1919; Honduras in default 79% of 115 years); governments have defaulted more on
  bank debt than on bonds; recent shift to local currency, non-bank and domestic-law defaults, with faster

  restructurings.
  • Consequences of default (CR-8 d): reputation loss, capital market turmoil, falling real output, political
  instability. Evidence: real GDP down 0.5% to 2%, mostly in year one; ratings one to two notches lower and
  borrowing costs 0.5% to 1% higher, fading over time; bilateral trade down 8% for up to 15 years; banking
  crisis probability 14% (11 points higher); after sharp devaluations, a 45% higher chance of replacing the
  leader and 64% for the finance chief. Total repudiation is rare; most defaults end in exchange or restructuring.
  • Drivers of sovereign default risk (CR-8 b, e): degree of indebtedness (debt to GDP: Venezuela above 300%,
  but Japan, the US and France are high too, so it is incomplete), pension and social service commitments
  (ageing raises risk), revenues and tax base, stability of revenues (diversified economies and sales or VAT
  systems are more stable than income tax systems), political risk (autocracies, central bank independence),
  implicit backing (EU membership).
  • Sovereign ratings (CR-8 e): cover creditworthiness to private creditors, not official lenders; S&P rates prob-
  ability of default, Moody’s probability and severity (recovery). Default = failure to pay on the due date or a
  restructuring. S&P’s factors: political risk, economic structure, growth prospects, fiscal flexibility, govern-
  ment debt burden, offshore and contingent liabilities, monetary flexibility, external liquidity, external debt
  burden. A committee of five to ten analysts votes. Local and foreign currency ratings: notch-up (foreign
  currency anchor) or notch-down (local currency anchor); the gap depends on monetary policy independence.
  Investment grade sovereigns default far less often than speculative grade.
  • Shortcomings of ratings (CR-8 f): upward bias, herd behaviour, too little too late, vicious cycle (overre-
  acting in a crisis), and ratings failures (Bhatia: S&P cut Korea from AA- to B+, ten notches, in about two
  months in 1997). Causes: information from the governments themselves, thin analyst resources (four to five
  sovereigns each in 2003), revenue bias via sub-sovereign ratings, other business lines.
  • Sources of country risk (CR-8 a): the GARP excerpt opens with sovereign default rather than the general
  taxonomy. Damodaran’s broader framework adds risk from a country’s stage in the economic life cycle,
  political risk (continuity, corruption, violence, expropriation), legal risk (property rights and enforcement)
  and economic structure (concentration in one commodity or sector) (outside-source).
  Formulas
  Sovereign default spread. Spread = ysovereign, USD −yUS Treasury  (same currency, same maturity; Brazil: 6.19%−
  3.02% = 3.17%)
  CDS premium. Annual payment = notional × CDS spread  (USD 10 million × 2.5% = USD 250,000)
  Exam traps
  • Lead-lag polarity: markets lead ratings: CDS changes lead bond yields and ratings, and bond spreads move
  before rating changes, not after.
  • Absolute claim: CDS spreads are timely but noisy; there is “little to indicate” they beat bond default spreads.
  “Superior to all other measures” is false; “better predictor than ratings” is the supported claim.
  • Scope error: market default spreads need a default-free bond in the same currency; comparing local currency
  yields across countries measures inflation as well as default.
  • Ratings are not useless: a rating change still has a price impact.
  • Local versus foreign currency: local currency ratings are usually at least as high as foreign currency ratings,
  but not always (India 2010: Ba2 local, Baa3 foreign).
