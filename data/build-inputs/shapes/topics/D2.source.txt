  Revision notes
  The specifics: accountability and concentration (S1, S2)
  • Accountability is not transferable (S1). SR 13-19: the use of service providers “does not relieve a financial
  institution’s board of directors and senior management of their responsibility to ensure that outsourced ac-
  tivities are conducted in a safe-and-sound manner and in compliance with applicable laws and regulations.”
  ORR-5: outsourcing transfers the execution of a process and some of its operational risks, but “account-
  ability, however, is not transferable”; the bank remains accountable to its customers. Reputational damage
  cannot really be outsourced. Increasingly, outsourcing is seen as risk sharing rather than risk transfer.
  • Outsourcing can add or cut risk. Outsourcing to cut cost (offshore call centres) is closer to risk acquisition
  in exchange for margin; outsourcing a non-core activity to a better-controlled specialist (payments, cloud data
  centres) can reduce net operational risk at a higher cost. Either way the firm gains third-party risk: the risk
  of failed controls at the provider. Contracting with providers “does not fully mitigate operational risk”.
  • Concentration risk (S2). SR 13-19 lists six risks from service providers: compliance, concentration (“out-
  sourced services or products are provided by a limited number of service providers or are concentrated in
  limited geographic locations”), reputational, country (foreign-based provider), operational, legal. In the
  cyber-resilience reading, concentration risk in this context means a single firm’s lack of control over one
  provider to which many activities are outsourced, not systemic risk to the industry (see A23; AI provider
  concentration in A14).
  • Resilience link (S2; ORR-4 g). Single points of failure are resilience KRIs: key supplier dependency,
  low substitutability and high switching costs (IT and cloud providers, data custodians, payment platforms,
  utilities). Remove them through back-ups and redundancy, or reflect them in impact tolerances and stress
  tests. Resilience is organised by important business service and impact tolerance; business continuity by
  process and RTO. Contingency planning (SR 13-19): ensure a DR/BCP exists for the contracted service,
  assess the provider’s plan and its alignment with the firm’s own, test it periodically, and keep an exit strategy
  with a pool of comparable providers.
  The SR 13-19 programme (ORR-12 a)
  • Board and senior management. Policies approved by the board (or an executive committee of the board)
  establish the programme: risk assessments, due diligence, contract standards, ongoing monitoring, business
  continuity. Senior management executes the policies and reports adherence to the board.
  • Six core elements. (A) risk assessments; (B) due diligence and selection; (C) contract provisions; (D) incen-
  tive compensation review; (E) oversight and monitoring; (F) business continuity and contingency plans. The
  programme is risk-focused: it concentrates on activities with a substantial impact on financial condition,
  critical to operations, involving sensitive customer information or new products, or posing material compli-
  ance risk. A community bank with a few critical activities outsourced to reputable providers may use fewer
  elements than a bank with thousands of providers.

  • Risk assessment and incentives. Decide first whether outsourcing fits strategy, then weigh benefits, risks,
  provider risk, cost, availability of qualified providers and the firm’s own ability to oversee the relationship;
  update periodically. Incentive compensation in provider contracts must be reviewed: variable fees that steer
  customers to higher-margin products without regard to suitability are inappropriate (see D21).
  • Oversight and escalation. Metrics set by the business line or relationship management; overseers with
  expertise and stature; higher-risk providers get more frequent and stringent monitoring and a designated
  point of contact; lower-risk providers less. Monitor financial condition (and subcontractor controls), and
  for significant relationships the control environment, including SOC 2 reports. Triggers escalate oversight,
  on-site reviews and the right to audit, with criteria for alternatives and termination.
  • Additional considerations. SAR functions (Bank Secrecy Act confidentiality); foreign providers (US law,
  on-site review rights, home-supervisor access to customer data); internal audit outsourcing (Sarbanes-Oxley
  bars the external auditor from certain non-audit services); outsourced risk management, including model
  validation, must follow SR 11-7 (see C6).
  Due diligence and contracts (ORR-12 b, c)
  • Due diligence before engagement. Depth depends on scope, complexity, importance, familiarity and repu-
  tation. Three areas: (1) business background, reputation and strategy (history, principals, background checks
  on employees, references, licences, pending litigation, resiliency of the business model); (2) financial per-
  formance and condition (statements, sustainability, effect of the relationship on the provider, commitment
  for the contract term, insurance, review of subcontractors); (3) operations and internal controls (facilities,
  training, systems security, privacy, records, business resumption, background checks, legal compliance).
  • Contract provisions. Reviewed by legal counsel before execution. Scope (rights and responsibilities, in-
  cluding the ability to subcontract); cost and compensation (no incentives to take imprudent risks); right
  to audit; measurable performance standards; confidentiality and security (NPPI, breach notification, GLBA
  501(b)); ownership and licence (escrow for source code); indemnification; default and termination (with time
  to transfer services and return of data); dispute resolution; limits on liability (the board judges reasonable-
  ness); insurance; customer complaints; provider’s business resumption plan; foreign providers (choice of law
  and jurisdiction); subcontracting: the same provisions apply to the subcontractor and the primary provider
  has overall accountability for all services it and its subcontractors provide.
  TPRM and the cases (ORR-13 a, b)
  • TPRM scope and life cycle. Third parties include vendors, suppliers, partners, contractors; fourth parties
  (subcontractors) and fifth parties extend the chain. “Third-party failure” is a Level 2 category of Basel’s
  seventh event type (EDPM); ORX makes third-party risk Level 1. Shared Assessments life cycle: business
  model decision; evaluation, risk rating and due diligence (proportionate; SIG questionnaires); contracts,
  SLAs and contract management (sign only after open items are remediated; impose standards on vendor out-
  sourcing to address fourth parties; audit rights); continuous monitoring with trigger events for reassessment;
  remediation or termination with an exit strategy. Risk-based vendor rating criteria: process criticality, data
  access, system access, regulated sector, substitutability.
  • Capital One and AWS (2019). About 100 million people affected; a former AWS employee exploited
  a misconfigured web application firewall (SSRF); data were not encrypted because of Capital One’s own
  configuration. OCC fine USD 80 million for “failure to establish effective risk assessment processes prior to
  migrating significant information technology operations to the public cloud”. Lesson: cloud providers offer
  the controls, but the customer must use them; risk management stays with the business.
  • Morgan Stanley data-centre decommissioning (2016, 2019). OCC fine USD 60 million: the bank failed to
  assess the risks of decommissioning hardware, to assess the risk of vendors including subcontractors, and to
  keep an inventory of customer data on its devices; inadequate vendor due diligence and monitoring. Lesson:
  outsourcing may transfer some operational risk, but the accountability is not transferable.
  Exam traps
  • Role misassignment (the standing trap): contracts, indemnities and subcontracting clauses allocate duties
  between firm and provider; they never move the board’s and senior management’s accountability to the provider,
  the subcontractor or the supervisor.
  • Absolute claim: outsourcing does not eliminate or fully mitigate operational risk; it trades it for third-party risk
  and is better seen as risk sharing.

  • Polarity flip on oversight: higher-risk, critical or data-sensitive providers get more frequent and stringent mon-
  itoring, whatever the provider’s reputation.
  • Scope/condition error on concentration: SR 13-19 concentration means few providers or few geographic
  locations for the firm’s services, a firm-level risk; it is not only an industry-wide systemic concern left to super-
  visors.
  • Sibling swap, BCM versus resilience: business continuity works by process and RTO; operational resilience
  works by important business service and impact tolerance. Case cross-mapping: Capital One (cloud miscon-
  figuration, no pre-migration risk assessment) versus Morgan Stanley (vendor and subcontractor oversight in
  decommissioning).
