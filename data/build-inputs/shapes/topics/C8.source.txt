  Revision notes
  • Positive and negative exposure (CR-19 a): on a counterparty default the survivor closes out, nets, and
  applies margin. If the net value is negative it still owes the amount and neither gains nor loses; if positive it has
  an unsecured claim and recovers only a fraction. Positive exposure = max(value, 0) (19.1); negative exposure
  = min(value, 0) (19.2), the exposure the counterparty has to the party’s own default (DVA). Recovery is, by
  convention, not included in exposure.
  • Sold option (S3): a sold option with the premium paid upfront has a value to the seller that can never be
  positive, so max(value, 0) = 0 on every path. Hull (CR-12 a): if the transactions have a negative value to
  the bank there is no exposure. Spot, strike, volatility and a Black-Scholes price in the stem price the option;
  they do not create exposure for the seller. The buyer has the exposure (Hull: a bought European option is the
  special case where exposure equals the no-default value).
  • Exposure as a short option (CR-19 a, b): the payoff is asymmetric, like a short option for the survivor (the
  defaulter has the option not to pay). Two consequences: volatility of the contract value is a key driver, and
  even simple instruments have option-like, complex exposure. A netting set behaves like a basket option;
  margin shifts the strike.
  • The metrics (S1): expected future value (EFV, also called expected mark-to-market or, confusingly, EE) is
  the average of all values and equals current value at time zero; PFE is the exposure exceeded with probability
  no more than 100% minus the confidence level, “an equivalent metric to VAR”; EPE is the average of the
  positive exposures with zero and negative values counted as zero (not dropped), sometimes called expected
  exposure (EE); ENE is the same for negative values, sometimes called NEE. Book example: five scenarios
  70, 50, 30, −10, −30: EFV 22, PFE 70, EPE 30, ENE −8.
  • Sensitivity to volatility: normal future value, mean 2.0: at σ = 2.0, PFE(99%) 6.65, EPE 2.17, ENE 0.17;
  at σ = 4.0, PFE 11.31, EPE 2.79, ENE 0.79, while EFV stays 2.0. ENE rises almost fivefold because it is
  out of the money.
  • Across time: average EPE is the time-weighted average of the EPE profile (a simple average if points are
  equally spaced), often called the loan equivalent: a crude summary that averages out market randomness and
  time, but with a strong theoretical basis for capital and xVA. Maximum PFE is the peak of the PFE profile
  (Hull: maximum peak exposure, the maximum of the peak exposures at all midpoints). Gregory warns that
  Basel definitions differ: Basel’s effective EE is EE made non-decreasing over time, and effective EPE is the
  average of effective EE over the first year (outside-source).
  • Exposure versus VaR (CR-19 b): same quantile problem, but exposure needs (i) many horizons, so ageing,
  cash flows, exercise and margin path dependency matter, and the drift cannot be ignored as it can over 10
  days; (ii) risk mitigants (netting, margin, termination features); (iii) both real-world (risk management) and
  risk-neutral (pricing) calculations.

  Exam traps
  • Sold option red herring (S3): the seller of an option with upfront premium has zero exposure. A Black-
  Scholes value in the stem is a value, not an exposure; never add it to EPE and never use it to offset another

  trade’s exposure.
  • Netting needs a legal agreement: trades aggregate only within an enforceable netting set. Without one, total
  exposure is the sum of standalone exposures; netting scenario by scenario understates it.
  • EPE denominator: EPE averages over all scenarios with negatives set to zero. Averaging only the positive
  scenarios overstates it (in the five-scenario example, 50 instead of 30).
  • Additivity (sibling swap): EFV adds across trades; EPE and ENE do not. A claim that aggregated EPE equals
  the sum of standalone EPEs borrows EFV’s property.
  • Volatility polarity: higher volatility raises EPE, ENE (in magnitude) and PFE; it leaves EFV unchanged.
  • Name collisions: Gregory’s EFV is “sometimes referred to as expected mark-to-market or expected exposure”,
  and his EPE is “sometimes known as expected exposure”; average EPE is “sometimes known simply as EPE”.
  Read the definition in the stem, not the acronym.
