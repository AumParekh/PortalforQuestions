  Revision notes
  The specific: three lines, who does what (ORR-2 c; CR-2 a)
  • Line 1, risk owners. The front line of the business; BCBS (RPSMOR) calls it “business unit management”.
  “Risk is managed where it is generated”: risk is owned by the business, not by the risk function. Better to
  say risk owners than “the business”: the head of IT owns IT risk. Duties: identify and assess the materiality
  of inherent operational risks; establish controls; assess control design and effectiveness; monitor and report
  the unit’s risk profile; report residual risks (loss events, control deficiencies, process inadequacies, breaches
  of tolerance) and report a lack of resources, tools or training to the second line.
  • Line 1.5, risk champions. Risk specialists, “stewards” or “risk correspondents” inside each business de-
  partment (line “1.5” or “1.6”): main correspondent for risk issues, collect and record events and losses, map
  risks and controls to group definitions, follow up control rules, action plans and audit tracking. Ownership
  of operational risk must not be assumed by, or passed to, them.
  • Line 2, the CORF. An “independent corporate operational risk management function”: develops and main-
  tains policies, standards and training; forms an independent view of the business units’ risk identification,
  key controls and respect of appetite; challenges the business’s use of the tools and provides evidence of
  that challenge; reviews and contributes to monitoring and reporting. It is a sounding board for decisions
  that change the risk profile and needs delegated authority to veto decisions that breach regulation or board
  risk appetite. It must not be directly involved in line 1 risk management. In larger banks the CORF reports
  independently of the risk-generating units; at small banks independence may come from separation of duties.
  • Guidance versus challenge. First role of the risk function in young or small units is to educate (what is
  operational risk, how incidents are recognised and reported, benefits, pitfalls). A second-line risk manager
  may run an RCSA workshop yet stay independent by not steering the answers. “The second line owns the
  methodology; the first line owns the risks”: the first line gives the final sign-off on its risk and control
  assessment.
  • Line 3, internal audit. “Independent assurance”; the clearest boundaries. Independent of the risk process;
  assesses the control system and compliance, including of the risk function; builds its own audit universe. IIA:
  internal audit is neither responsible for nor part of risk, compliance or finance; it must in no circumstances
  rely exclusively on their work and always examines its own sample; less intense scrutiny only after evaluating
  that function. Reports in solid line to a non-executive (audit committee chair, senior independent director or
  non-executive chairman). External audit, also independent, gives an opinion on the financial statements.
  • Hybrid functions. In small and medium firms finance, HR, legal, ICT and security functions act in both
  lines. BCBS: document and distinguish the first and second line responsibilities, emphasising second line
  independence. Table 2.4: legal drafts contracts (line 1) vs oversees legal activity in the business (line 2); HR
  administers pay (1) vs oversees people management (2); IT security encrypts data, partitions and monitors

  the network (1) vs verifies ICT controls in business operations and enforces security policies (2); finance
  manages treasury and accounting (1) vs verifies exposures remain under authorised limits (2). What defines
  a line is the role, not the department’s name.
  • IIA Three Lines Model (2021) and “three lines of accountability”. The 2021 model reinforces the ex-
  pertise and support role of the second line, with challenge limited to risk-related matters; the second line is
  part of, and reports to, management. “Three lines of accountability” is a renaming, not a departure from the
  model.
  • CR-2 a version (IIA 2013 paper). First line: business owners, who primarily own and manage risk. Second
  line: ERM, compliance and legal, who establish policies and procedures and serve as management oversight.
  Third line: internal audit, external auditors and special audit committees, giving independent assurance over
  lines 1 and 2. Credit guidelines are owned and maintained by the CRO’s office, sponsored by a senior
  executive (CRO or CFO) and approved by the board; for listed firms the board audit committee reviews
  risk policies, and the board risk committee oversees and approves them.
  Committees, board, Basel expectations (ORR-2 a, b)
  • Committee pyramid. Lowest: business-type or regional risk committees, which escalate significant issues
  and limit breaches to a firm-wide or second-line committee. The group operational risk committee reports
  the consolidated picture to the executive risk committee, the management committee and the board risk
  committee. The board risk committee oversees all operational risk and makes recommendations to the
  full board; members need recent and relevant experience. A small firm may have a single operational risk
  committee reporting to the management committee and board risk committee. Each committee has Terms
  of Reference (charter); minutes record discussions, decisions and their motivation.
  • The board. Ultimately responsible for the firm, including risk management; determines the nature and ex-
  tent of significant risks it will take (sets risk appetite and keeps the firm within it); validates and periodically
  reviews the ORMF; gets assurance that senior management implements it; establishes risk culture; must be
  trained. For resilience (BCBS resilience Principle 1): uses existing governance, oversees senior manage-
  ment’s implementation, ensures financial and technical resources.
  • Basel II and RPSMOR. Pillar 1 for operational risk includes mandatory management principles (the only
  risk type where this is so); Pillar 2 add-ons for concentration, compliance, governance gaps, aggressive
  growth (ICAAP/SREP, ORSA, CCAR); Pillar 3 disclosure. RPSMOR (March 2021) has 12 principles: cul-
  ture led by the board and implemented by senior management (1), sound ORMF (2), board approval and
  review of ORMF (3), board-approved risk appetite and tolerance statement (4), senior management develops
  and implements policies and systems (5), identification (6), change management (7), monitoring (8), control
  environment (9), ICT (10), business continuity (11), disclosure (12). From January 2023 Pillar 1 capital uses
  the SA: ORC = BIC × ILM.
  • Use test. ORM must be embedded in decisions, not rubber-stamped. “Absence of evidence is evidence of
  absence”: committees and management should record issues, discussions and decisions in approved minutes.
  Risk appetite and culture (ORR-2 d)
  • Risk appetite. Statements must be easy to communicate, justify taking, limiting or avoiding risks, align with
  strategy, be monitored by indicators on exposure, control environment and losses, and be forward-looking
  (scenario and stress tested). A claim such as “minimal tolerance for data breaches” must be corroborated
  with strong controls and monitoring. Structure: exposure limits, key controls, tolerated incidents, monitoring
  metrics (“appetite KRIs”). The board ultimately owns the risk limits and validates them periodically; in
  practice it often delegates this to the board risk committee, which uses the risk function to implement and
  report on them. Governance: risk owners (line 1), control owners, metrics owners.
  • Risk culture. Led by the board and implemented by senior management (“tone at the top”); code of conduct
  approved by the board and attested by staff; skin in the game; training; reinforcement without unnecessary
  blame (“no unnecessary blame”); “if you see something, say something”; whistleblowing. Toxic culture:
  overambitious targets, undue pressure, resistance to bad news (Wells Fargo). Most telling sign: talking about
  internal incidents without undue blame.
  The Basel taxonomy (ORR-1 b; ORR-3 c, d)
  • Seven Level 1 event types (ORX 2014 to 2019, frequency / severity): IF internal fraud 2% / 2%; EF external
  fraud 30% / 9%; EPWS employment practices and workplace safety 15% / 5%; CPBP clients, products and
  business practices 22% / 52%; DPA damage to physical assets 1% / 1%; BDSF business disruption and
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A22 Three Lines of Defence and Operational Risk Governance
  system failures 2% / 5%; EDPM execution, delivery and process management 28% / 27%. CPBP’s severity
  is driven by regulatory fines; legal losses cluster in EPWS and EDPM.
  • Levels. Level 1 is the highest category, Level 2 a detailed version of Level 1, and Level 3 provides ex-
  amples. BCBS recognises only Levels 1 and 2 as regulatory categories; regulated firms report using this
  categorisation.
  • Level 2 by Level 1. IF: Unauthorised Activity; Theft and Fraud (“at least one internal party”). EF: Theft and
  Fraud; Systems Security (third party). EPWS: Employee Relations; Safe Environment; Diversity and Dis-
  crimination. CPBP: Suitability, Disclosure and Fiduciary; Improper Business or Market Practices; Product
  Flaws; Selection, Sponsorship and Exposure; Advisory Activities. DPA: Disasters and other events. BDSF:
  Systems. EDPM: Transaction Capture, Execution and Maintenance; Monitoring and Reporting; Customer
  Intake and Documentation; Customer/Client Account Management; Trade Counterparties; Vendors and Sup-
  pliers.
  • Level 3 examples that discriminate. Mismarking of position (intentional): IF / Unauthorised Activity. In-
  sider trading not on firm’s account: IF / Theft and Fraud; insider trading on firm’s account: CPBP / Improper
  Business or Market Practices (with antitrust, market manipulation, unlicensed activity, money laundering).
  Check kiting: under both IF and EF Theft and Fraud, decided by whether an internal party is involved.
  Hacking damage: EF / Systems Security. Account churning, breach of privacy, misuse of confidential infor-
  mation: CPBP / Suitability, Disclosure and Fiduciary. Model errors: CPBP / Product Flaws, whereas model
  or system misoperation: EDPM / Transaction Capture. Utility outage: BDSF / Systems. Workers compensa-
  tion: EPWS / Safe Environment. Outsourcing and vendor disputes: EDPM / Vendors and Suppliers. Failed
  mandatory reporting: EDPM / Monitoring and Reporting.
  • Other taxonomies. ORX reference taxonomy (2019): 14 Level 1 risks versus Basel’s 7, with third party,
  statutory reporting and tax, business continuity, data management, information security (including cyber) and
  model risk elevated to Level 1; ORX causes taxonomy uses PPSE (people, process, systems, external events).
  Impact categories: financial loss, reputational damage, regulatory non-compliance, customer detriment (plus
  service disruption). Control categories: preventive, detective, corrective, directive. Firms place cyber in EF,
  IT risk or a separate Level 1. See also D22 for ORR-3 a, b (identification tools, scenarios).
  Exam traps
  • Role misassignment (the map’s defect): the second line facilitates the RCSA and owns the methodology, but
  the first line signs off; the risk function never “owns” operational risk; risk champions never take ownership;
  internal audit never reports to the CRO.
  • Absolute claim on audit reliance: internal audit may take account of others’ work after evaluating it, but never
  relies exclusively on risk, compliance or finance testing.
  • Sibling swap in the committee pyramid: the board risk committee recommends to the full board; the group
  operational risk committee reports upward to it. The board approves the ORMF and the risk appetite statement;
  senior management implements.
  • Scope error on hybrids and appetite: hybrid functions must document and distinguish their line 1 and line 2
  roles; an appetite statement without key controls and monitoring metrics is not credible.
  • Taxonomy siblings: insider trading on vs not on the firm’s account (CPBP vs IF); model errors (CPBP Product
  Flaws) vs model misoperation (EDPM); internal vs external party for check kiting; utility outage is BDSF, not
  DPA. Level 3 is examples only, not a regulatory category.
