  Revision notes
  The CSA and its terms (CR-17 b, c, g; S1, S4)
  • CSA: an annex to the ISDA Master Agreement in which the parties agree contractual margin-posting; the
  market-standard bilateral margin agreement. It covers valuation method and timing, the margin amount,
  transfer mechanics, eligible margin, substitutions, dispute resolution, remuneration, initial margin, haircuts,
  rehypothecation and triggers (e.g. downgrades). Terms are fixed at signing and change only by mutual amend-
  ment (a CCP can change terms unilaterally). There can be two CSAs (legacy and new) under one Master
  Agreement.
  • Threshold: the amount below which margin is not required, producing undercollateralisation. Zero thresh-
  old: margin always posted (above the MTA); infinite threshold: the party never posts (a one-way CSA).
  Thresholds are increasingly zero and can be non-zero only for parties exempt from the bilateral margin rules.
  • Initial margin (independent amount): the opposite of a threshold (a negative threshold), extra margin
  posted independently of portfolio value, giving overcollateralisation against delays and close-out costs (S3).
  It relates to the variability of value, is increasingly dynamic, is not netted against variation margin, and
  two-way initial margins are paid gross. Threshold and initial margin are not used together.
  • Minimum transfer amount (MTA) and rounding: the MTA is the smallest amount that can be transferred.
  Threshold and MTA are additive for when a call can be made, but the MTA is not deducted from the amount
  due (S2). MTAs create path dependency (Table 17.7). Rounding has a small effect; MTA and rounding are
  generally zero for cash-only margin (variation margin rules, CCPs).
  • Haircuts: a haircut of x% gives a valuation percentage of (1 − x)%; the margin giver accounts for it, so
  haircuts also overcollateralise (S3). They reflect liquidation time, volatility, default risk, maturity, liquidity
  and wrong-way linkage. Cash may carry none except for FX mismatch; assets with significant credit or
  liquidity risk are avoided because haircuts cannot be large enough. Example (eq. 17.1): 99%, two days, 1%
  rate volatility, 10-year bond: about 2%.
  • Credit support amount (CR-17 c): the amount of margin that may be requested at a point in time; because
  of thresholds, MTAs and initial margins it is not the portfolio value. Table 17.6: value 350, threshold 100,
  balance 0, call 250; then value 325, balance 250, amount −25 (return), though 75 is uncollateralised.

  One-way vs two-way CSA, credit-quality links, valuation agent, disputes (CR-17 d, f, h; S5,
  S8)
  • Types of CSA: no CSA is typical bank-corporate (corporates cannot manage the liquidity and operational
  demands). A two-way CSA, where both post, is typical between financial counterparties; two-way CSAs
  with zero thresholds are standard interbank and aim to benefit both parties. A one-way CSA, where only one
  party receives, is additional risk for the margin giver, leaving it worse off than no CSA: triple-A sovereigns
  and supranationals receive but do not post; banks have demanded one-way CSAs in their favour from some
  hedge funds. Funding and capital costs have made one-way CSAs problematic.
  • Rating-linked parameters (Table 17.4): AAA threshold ∞; AA+ USD 100m; AA USD 50m; AA− USD
  25m; A+ zero; A and A− initial margin 1%; BBB+ 2% of notional. Aim: low workload when quality is
  high, tighter terms as it falls. Problems: ratings react too slowly, and triggers create liquidity problems and
  cliff-edge effects (AIG: USD 20bn extra margin after its downgrade; Fed facility up to USD 85bn). Basel
  gives no capital benefit for rating triggers; the LCR requires pre-funding (an A+ bank funds 2% initial margin
  for a three-notch downgrade to BBB+).
  • Valuation agent, disputes and reconciliation: the valuation agent is normally the party calling for delivery
  or return of margin; it calculates the current value including netting, the haircut-adjusted value of margin
  held, the uncollateralised exposure, and the credit support amount. Where both parties act as valuation agent,
  the potential for disputes is significant. Disputes arise from trade population, trade valuation methodology,
  application of CSA rules (thresholds, eligible collateral), market data and close time, and valuation of posted
  collateral. Reconciliations agree valuations and margin requirements across portfolios in advance; most
  large portfolios are reconciled daily; Dodd-Frank and EMIR require more frequent reconciliation; disputes
  can raise capital. Not relevant for centrally cleared trades (the CCP is valuation agent).
  Mechanics, reuse and regulation (CR-17 a, e, i, k)
  • Rationale and transfer: margin mitigates future exposure; on exchanges it is daily settlement, in OTC
  markets security (“collateralised to market”). Variation margin uses the base value; it cannot cover the delay
  to default and close-out costs, hence initial margin. Security interest (New York law, 46.8%) keeps ownership
  with the giver; title transfer (English law, 30.1%) passes it, which favours the receiver. Cash is remunerated
  at OIS; substitutions follow notice, consent where required, delivery, then return.
  • Rehypothecation and segregation: reuse is natural for variation margin (quasi-settlement, reduces demand
  for high-quality collateral); not for initial margin, which should be segregated (Lehman, MF Global: USD
  1.6bn of segregated customer margin moved illegally). Legal segregation (all three holding methods) vs
  operational segregation (third party or tri-party only). Ideal: pledged, unaffiliated custodian, no reuse. Seg-
  regation raises funding costs.
  • Bilateral margin rules (UMR): variation margin daily, zero threshold, MTA ≤ EUR 500,000, reusable;
  initial margin from both parties, gross, 99%, 10 days with stressed data, segregated, no reuse, threshold up
  to EUR 50m, phased in to EUR 8bn (Phase 6). FX swaps and forwards exempt from initial margin. Standard
  schedule net IM = (0.4 + 0.6 × NGR)× gross IM; ISDA SIMM; no cherry-picking between model and
  schedule.
  Risks created by margin (CR-17 j; S6, S9)
  • Margin period of risk: the effective time between a counterparty ceasing to post margin and all trades
  being closed out and replaced (or hedged); market risk over it is uncollateralised. It is counterparty-risk
  specific and not relevant for funding, and is not the margin call frequency (daily calls, 10-day MPoR). Pre-
  default: valuation and margin call, receiving collateral (including disputes), settlement, grace (cure) period.
  Post-default: macro-hedging, close-out, rehedging and replacement (one-to-one or portfolio), liquidation of
  margin securities.
  • MPoR values: Basel II minimum 10 business days (daily calls; add days if calls are less frequent); Basel
  III 20 days or more in some cases (over 5,000 trades, illiquid margin or exotics, a history of disputes); CCPs
  about 5 days; the ISDA Resolution Stay Protocol (24 or 48 hours) may lengthen it. It is a simple effective
  catch-all, not the literal close-out time: risk declines post-default (10 days constant equals 31 days linearly
  declining), while higher post-default volatility argues for longer (20% more volatility means about 44% more
  time). It is the primary driver of the need for initial margin.
  • Other risks: liquidity risk falls on the holder that must sell margin after a default (bid-offer, price falls,
  large positions; concentration limits of 5% to 10%); FX risk from currency mismatch; wrong-way margin
  (own sovereign’s bonds, LTCM’s Russian bonds); operational risk (missed calls, failed deliveries, human

  error), which Basel III answers with a longer MPoR; legal risk (Citigroup and Lehman, MF Global); funding
  liquidity risk for the party that must post: margin converts counterparty risk into funding liquidity risk (AIG,
  Ashanti: USD 280m of calls on a USD 450m loss). Margin, like netting, redistributes risk to other creditors
  (no margin: 67% recovery each; with VM: 100% vs 50%; with IM used: OC 25%).
  Collateralised CVA (CR-20 j; S9)
  • Effect on CVA: margin only changes EPE, so eq. 20.3 applies with collateralised EPE; 10  pdays has become
  standard even for accounting. Best case with variation margin only: risk falls by about maturity/MPoR
  (CR-17: 11 times for 5 years at 10 days); CR-20 quotes about 8.4 times for its 10-year swap. Reality is worse,
  so only initial margin makes risk negligible. Table 20.6 pay-fixed: CVA −28.9 to −3.0 bps, BCVA −21.5
  to −1.5. High thresholds take CVA to the uncollateralised value, high initial margin to zero. Collateralised
  DVA is controversial (stopping posting 10 days before own default). Segregated initial margin posted does
  not change CVA but creates MVA.
  Netting, close-out, compression and termination (CR-16; S7)
  • ISDA Master Agreement and close-out netting (CR-16 a, b): the market-standard contract for OTC deriva-
  tives; it gives the contractual basis for close-out (right to terminate and stop payments) and netting (offset
  values into one amount, owed or claimed in bankruptcy); legal opinions in 54 jurisdictions. Payment netting
  reduces settlement risk; close-out netting reduces pre-settlement (counterparty) risk. 1992 ISDA: market
  quotation (at least three quotes) or loss; 2002 ISDA: a single close-out amount, commercially reasonable
  (2009 protocol). Walkaway clauses (Drexel, Enron/TXU, Lehman) create moral hazard. Set-off can extend
  offset to other agreements (loans).
  • Effectiveness and side effects (CR-16 c): close-out netting is the single biggest counterparty risk mitigant
  and now cuts exposure by close to 90%; netted positions are more volatile, encourage concentration with one
  counterparty, and reduce panic when MTM is negative. Central clearing gives multilateral netting (Table
  16.3: 360 to 120 bilateral to 60) but partial clearing can increase exposure (210). Netting redistributes value
  from other creditors (derivatives recovery 77.5% vs 60%; others 25%).
  • Trade compression (CR-16 d, e): multilateral tear-up of redundant trades that reduces total gross notional
  while keeping each participant’s market risk (value and risk neutral); it lowers operational, legal and reg-
  ulatory (leverage ratio) costs, but does not necessarily cut any individual party’s notional, has diminishing
  returns, needs fungible standard contracts and interchangeable counterparties. Example (Table 16.1): long
  150 and 25 with A, short 75 with B and 50 with C, so a net long of 50 (175 − 125) held with Counterparty A.
  Trilateral netting in Figure 16.7 cuts 1,250 to 890 (final 130 or 110). EMIR: firms with over 500 uncleared
  contracts consider compression twice a year.
  • Termination features (CR-16 d, f; Gregory 17.1): break clauses are mandatory, optional, or contingent
  on an event; the most common Additional Termination Event is a rating downgrade (for unrated parties
  NAV, market cap or key person). Termination is at a base MTM, often via a panel of banks. Table 17.1:
  mandatory breaks give counterparty risk reduction, CVA reduction, capital relief and LCR relief; optional
  breaks give partial counterparty risk reduction and capital relief (if rigorously exercised); rating-based breaks
  give partial counterparty risk reduction only (Basel rules out capital relief; the LCR forces pre-funding).
  Dangers: systematic deterioration, slow ratings, cliff-edge effects (AIG), modelling difficulty, and moral
  hazard from sales staff. Resets settle the MTM periodically and are easier to model.

  Exam traps
  • MTA deducted (dropped condition): the MTA only decides whether a transfer occurs; once passed, the full
  amount moves. If the amount is below the MTA, nothing moves, even with every input supplied.
  • Credit support balance ignored (intermediate result): max(value − K, 0) is the total margin required, not
  the call; subtract (or add back, with the sign) margin already held.
  • Whose threshold: when the party is out of the money, its own threshold KP applies; deducting both thresholds
  is wrong. After a downgrade use the new rating-linked threshold.
  • Role misassignment (CR-17 j): liquidation risk sits with the margin holder; funding liquidity risk with the
  party that must post; the valuation agent is the party calling. A one-way CSA hurts the margin giver.
  • MPoR vs margin call frequency (sibling swap): daily calls do not mean a one-day MPoR; and the MPoR is
  not used for funding.
