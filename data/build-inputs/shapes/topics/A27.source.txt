  Revision notes
  The specifics first: overall cost of funds (LTR-12 d)
  • Historical average cost approach. Looks at the past: what funds has the firm raised to date and what did
  they cost? Weighted average interest expense = all interest paid / total funds raised (book: 65/1,000 = 6.5%).
  Break-even cost rate on borrowed funds invested in earning assets = (interest + other operating costs) / all
  earning assets (book: (65 + 10)/750 = 10%): the rate the firm must earn just to meet the total operating
  costs of raising funds. Overall cost of capital = break-even + [after-tax cost of stockholders’ investment /
  (1 − t)] × (stockholders’ investment / earning assets) (book: 10% + 12%/0.65 × 100/750 = 12.5%).
  • Pooled-funds approach. Looks at the future: what minimum rate of return must be earned on any future
  loans and investments just to cover the cost of all new funds raised? Pooled deposit and nondeposit funds
  expense = all expected operating expenses / all new funds (book: 30/300 = 10%). Hurdle rate of return over
  all earning assets = all expected operating costs / dollars available to place in earning assets (book: 30/200
  = 15%, before taxes). New stockholders’ investment is one of the pooled sources. Rose’s end-of-chapter
  problem calls it the “pooled-funds marginal cost”.
  • Effective cost rate of a single source (LTR-12 d). (Current interest cost on amounts borrowed + noninterest
  costs to access the funds) / net investable funds raised. Net investable funds = total borrowed less legal
  reserves, deposit insurance assessments and funds placed in nonearning assets. Book: Fed funds (1.5 +
  0.0625)/24 = 6.51%; CDs (1.75 + 0.1875)/24 = 8.07%, or 8.10% after deducting the USD 0.0675 million
  insurance fee (25 × 0.0027).
  The sources (LTR-12 a)
  • Customer relationship doctrine and liability management. The first priority is to make every loan ex-
  pected to earn positive net earnings: lending decisions precede funding decisions; if deposits fall short, seek
  the lowest-cost borrowed funds (limitation: poor-quality loans, subprime 2007 to 2009). Liability manage-
  ment: buying funds, mainly from other financial institutions, with the offer rate as the control lever. Flexible
  (the borrower chooses amount and term, unlike deposits) but interest sensitive: suppliers are highly rate elas-
  tic. Nondeposit borrowings support about 16% of assets at the largest banks and about 4% at the smallest.
  See also A30.
  • Federal funds (most popular domestic source of borrowed reserves). Short-term borrowings of immediately
  available money; overnight (unwritten), term, or continuing contracts (small respondents with large corre-
  spondents); no legal reserve requirement on Fed funds borrowings; usually unsecured. Key advantage: ready
  availability; key disadvantage: a volatile rate.
  • Repurchase agreements (RPs). Often viewed as collateralised Fed funds: temporary sale of high-quality
  securities (starting leg) with an agreement to buy back at a set price and date (closing leg); reduces the
  lender’s credit risk but may take longer to arrange. Interest cost = amount × RP rate × days/360. GCF
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A27  Non-Deposit Liabilities, Pooled Cost of Funds and Liquidity Transfer Pricing
  RPs (1998) allow collateral substitution and netting at the FICC. Bear Stearns and Lehman could not find
  adequate RP support. See also D6.
  • Discount window and FHLB advances. Fed loans need acceptable collateral. Primary credit: sound in-
  stitutions, usually overnight, slightly above the target Fed funds rate, no need to exhaust other sources first.
  Secondary credit: about 50 bp above primary, monitored, not for asset expansion. Seasonal credit: small
  and medium institutions with intrayear swings. Secondary carries the highest rate, seasonal the lowest. An
  above-market Lombard rate acts as a ceiling on overnight rates. Undercapitalised institutions: no more than
  60 days in each 120. FHLB advances (12 regional banks, 1932): mortgage-collateralised, stable, below-
  market, overnight to 20+ years; the FHLB is first in line, ahead of the FDIC, in a failure.
  • Negotiable CDs, Eurocurrency deposits, commercial paper, long-term funds. Negotiable CDs: legally
  a deposit, practically an IOU; USD 100,000+, multiples of USD 1 million, 7 days to 1 to 2 years (mainly 1 to
  6 months), bearer form; domestic, Euro CDs, Yankee CDs, thrift CDs; 360-day interest-bearing basis; zero
  reserve requirement. Eurocurrency: the largest unregulated market, centred in London, priced off LIBOR; tap
  vs tranche CDs; FRCDs and FRNs of 1 to 20 years. Commercial paper via a holding-company affiliate: high
  volume, moderate cost, volatile capacity, credit risk. Capital notes and debentures (5 to 12 years) supplement
  equity and act as a barometer of perceived default risk.
  How much and which source (LTR-12 b, c)
  • Available funds gap = current and projected loans and investments desired − current and expected deposit
  inflows and other available funds. Book: (150+75+135)−(185+100) = USD 75 million; most institutions
  add a small cushion.
  • Five factors in choosing a source (LTR-12 c): (1) relative costs; (2) risk, i.e., volatility and dependability;
  (3) length of time funds are needed; (4) size of the borrowing institution; (5) regulations. Fed funds are
  among the cheapest (the shortest T-bills can be lower); CDs and Eurocurrency slightly above; commercial
  paper slightly above both; primary credit among the highest. Two risks: interest rate risk (the shorter the
  term, the more volatile the rate, so overnight Fed funds are the most volatile) and credit availability risk
  (CD, Eurodollar and commercial paper markets are especially sensitive). Size: the trading unit is USD 1
  million; Eurodollars usually only for money-centre firms with the highest ratings; the discount window and
  Fed funds serve small institutions. Term: funds needed this afternoon point to Fed funds or the discount
  window. Nondeposit funds cost more than checkable deposits, less than thrift deposits, and are more volatile
  year to year.
  Liquidity transfer pricing (LTR-14 a, b, c, d)
  • What LTP is (LTR-14 a). A process that attributes the costs, benefits and risks of liquidity to business units,
  transferring them to a centrally managed pool: it charges users of funds (assets, loans), credits providers of
  funds (liabilities, deposits) and recoups the cost of the liquidity cushion by charging contingent commitments
  on their predicted use. Before the GFC many banks treated liquidity as a free good. BCBS Principle 4:
  incorporate liquidity costs, benefits and risks in internal pricing, performance measurement and new product
  approval.
  • Better-practice governance (LTR-14 a). An LTP policy that defines LTP and its purpose; LTP managed
  centrally in group or subsidiary treasury with wholesale funding confined to it; trading book funding policies
  at the most granular level (desk, not book); independent oversight by risk and financial control, with regular
  ALCO meetings; LMIS able to attribute costs at a granular level; remuneration and profit pools adjusted for
  the cost of liquidity; charges attributed to the business activities, not borne by treasury.
  • Challenges found in the survey (LTR-14 b; 38 banks, nine countries). Most lacked an LTP policy; decen-
  tralised funding centres led to inconsistent regimes, manual off-line updates and internal arbitrage; trading
  book policies assumed holding periods of 180 days or less, so long-term charges applied only when assets
  rolled into the banking book (highly rated but illiquid CDO tranches funded overnight to 90 days); weak
  oversight; simplistic, inflexible LMIS; bonus pools taken as a share of accrued revenues; cushions sized on
  idiosyncratic scenarios only and funded short term.
  • Three approaches (LTR-14 c). Zero cost (liquidity as a free good, rate off the swap curve only): the worst
  practice, which maximises maturity transformation. Pooled average cost: one average rate on interest ex-
  pense across existing funding sources; simple, easy to understand, workable with basic LMIS, less net interest
  income volatility; but it ignores the higher liquidity risk of longer-term assets and lags the market cost of
  funds, so it promotes maturity transformation and distorts profit assessment (separate average rates for costs
  and benefits are better but still flat). Matched-maturity marginal cost: current best practice; swap fixed-rate
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A27  Non-Deposit Liabilities, Pooled Cost of Funds and Liquidity Transfer Pricing
  wholesale costs to floating, the spread over the swap-curve reference rate is the term liquidity premium,
  charged to assets and credited to liabilities by maturity.
  • Worked charges (LTR-14 c). Panel B premiums 5, 10, 18, 28, 40 bp for 1 to 5 years vs an 8 bp average: a
  five-year bullet is undercharged 32 bp (USD 3,200 per USD 1 million). Five-year linearly amortising loan:
  blended premium (1 × 5 + 2 × 10 + 3 × 18 + 4 × 28 + 5 × 40)/15 = 26.1 bp (5.9 bp pre-GFC). Mortgages:
  charge on the behavioural maturity (WAL), e.g., four years gives 28 bp. Deposits: sticky (term) money gets
  larger credits than hot money; core parts of demand deposits credited on modelled behavioural maturity.
  • Contingent liquidity risk (LTR-14 d). Cushion size from stress tests covering idiosyncratic, systemic and
  combined scenarios; cushion assets charged at longer-term rates, not overnight; the cost of carry (funding
  cost of cushion assets minus their return) is a cost of doing business recouped from the businesses that create
  the need, by predicted usage, not averaged across all assets (FTP = base rate + term liquidity premium
  + liquidity premium). Line of credit: limit USD 10 million, drawn 4 million, drawdown likelihood 60%,
  cushion cost 18 bp: 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.0018 = 6.48 bp, i.e., USD 6,480; at a 0.5 bp overnight rate the book states
  USD 1,800, but the arithmetic gives 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.00005 × 10m = USD 180 (source slip).

  Exam traps
  • Two “pooled” approaches (sibling swap): Rose & Hudgins’ pooled-funds approach is forward looking and
  costs all new funds; Grant’s pooled average cost of funds LTP approach averages the interest expense of existing
  sources and so lags the market. The backward-looking measure in Rose is the historical average cost approach.
  • Intermediate result vs hurdle: expected costs over all new funds is the pooled expense (10% in the book); the
  hurdle divides by the dollars placed in earning assets (15%). Effective cost rates divide by net investable funds,
  not gross borrowed.
  • Polarity flips: the shorter the term, the more volatile the rate; a pooled average charge undercharges long-term
  assets and under-credits long-term deposits, so it encourages maturity transformation; the lending decision
  comes before the funding decision.
  • Rank and size swaps: secondary credit is the highest discount window rate, seasonal the lowest; Eurodollars
  and large CDs need money-centre size and top ratings, while Fed funds and the discount window serve small
  banks.
  • Role misassignment in LTP: cushion costs are attributed to the business activities that create the need, not
FRM Part II · Volume 1: Tier A  A27  Non-Deposit Liabilities, Pooled Cost of Funds and Liquidity Transfer Pricing
  absorbed by treasury; wholesale funding is centralised in treasury, not raised by business units; oversight is by
  independent risk and financial control.
  • Scope errors: RPs are collateralised, Fed funds usually not; CDs, Eurodollars and commercial paper are es-
  pecially exposed to credit availability risk; cushion assets must be charged at longer-term rates, not overnight
  rates.
