  Revision notes
  • Regulatory versus economic capital (ORR-18 a, S1): regulatory capital applies only to a few regulated
  industries (banking, insurance) where regulators protect small depositors or policyholders; it is “calculated
  according to a set of industrywide rules and formulas and sets only a minimum required level of capital
  adequacy”. It rarely captures the true level of risk, and the gap to risk capital “can be quite wide”. Even if
  the two are similar at firm level, they may differ by business line; some practitioners allocate the higher of
  the two. Under Basel III, for activities such as securitization regulatory capital may end up much higher than
  economic capital, and the activity is then likely to migrate to the shadow banking sector.
  • Economic capital defined (CR-4 a, Box 4.1): “an estimate of the overall capital reserve needed to guarantee
  the solvency of a bank for a given confidence level”, with the confidence level set consistent with the bank’s
  target credit rating. Crouhy: risk capital is often called economic capital, and the two are generally treated
  as identical.
  • The two drivers of EC (CR-4 a): the confidence level and the riskiness of the assets. A bank targeting a
  very high rating chooses a very high confidence level (e.g. 99.97%), which means a higher capital multiplier
  (CM); a riskier asset book has a higher UL and needs more EC for the same confidence level. Holding less
  capital frees it to earn returns elsewhere, so EC balances risk and return.
  • Expected loss (CR-4 c): EL = PD × EA × LR. It is the foreseeable cost of doing business, covered by loan
  loss reserves (standard risk costs) and adequate loan pricing; it is the long-run average loss across typical
  economic conditions, not next year’s cyclical forecast. “By definition, EL does not itself constitute risk.”
  • The three factors (CR-4 b): PD is borrower-specific, linked to the rating, usually annual (migration matrices
  give multiperiod PDs; second-year PD is typically higher for initially low PDs); EA is the expected exposure
  at default including all outstanding payments and interest; LR (severity, LGD, LIED) is largely a function of
  collateral and equals 1− recovery rate.

  • Deriving economic capital (CR-4 g, S2): EC is the distance between the expected outcome and the unex-
  pected outcome at the chosen confidence level, ECp = ULp × CM and ECi = ULCi × CM: a loan’s capital
  is proportional to its contribution to portfolio risk. Schroeck’s footnote: “contrary to the regulatory approach

  that assigns roughly 8% equity capital to credits on a standalone basis”, this reflects both borrower quality
  and diversification.
  • Modeling the loss distribution (CR-4 h): credit losses are highly skewed (upside capped at the promised
  payments), so the crucial task is the choice of distribution for the tail. The beta distribution (a continuous
  approximation of the binomial) is fully characterised by ELp and ULp ; tail fitting combines it with Monte
  Carlo simulation. At 99.97%, CM is between 7.0 and 7.5, far above the multiples for normally distributed
  market risk. The beta overestimates EC for high-quality portfolios (ELp > ULp ) and has too thin a tail for
  lower-quality ones.
  • Challenges (CR-4 i): the approach treats credits as illiquid (a value approach would suit traded credit better);
  multi-period modeling is cumbersome, so almost all practical models use a one-year horizon; other risk types
  are assumed to be measured separately. Country risk is “lumpier”, with fewer diversification benefits.
  • Capital adequacy ratio (CR-5 c, S3): CAR = Capital/RWA ≥ α, with α = 8% under Basel II; capital
  is Tier 1 plus Tier 2. Tier 1 is core capital (equity and reserves), the primary source for unexpected losses;
  Tier 2 is supplementary and “not as secure”. Doumpos: Basel III imposes higher standards (CAR ≥ 10.5%),
  which matches ORR-22’s 8% total capital plus the 2.5% capital conservation buffer. See also D25 for the
  Basel III tiers.
  • Risk-weighted assets (CR-5 c): under the standardized approach, prescribed risk weights set by the supervi-
  sor apply to all cases and risk assessment relies on external models; it is costlier because capital requirements
  are higher to account for its ambiguity. Under IRB, PD comes from internal rating/scoring systems; LGD is
  set by supervisors (foundation) or modeled internally (advanced); RWA = K × 12.5 × EAD, with K from
  the ASRF model, which assumes a completely diversified portfolio so only systematic risk matters. Asset
  correlation falls with PD and rises with firm size; the maturity adjustment rises with maturity and is larger for
  low-PD borrowers; granularity adjustments (positive or negative) address residual concentration. See also
  A31.
  • CAMEL (CR-5 a): Capital, Assets, Management, Earnings, Liquidity; the most widely used empirical evalu-
  ation system in early credit risk management. It was soon realised that such empirical systems cannot provide
  a solid and objective basis, which led to analytical models, encouraged by regulators through relaxed capital
  requirements for sophisticated models.
  • Elements of credit risk modeling (CR-5 b): EL = PD × EAD × LGD over (usually) one year; the stan-
  dard default definition is a payment delay of at least 90 days; EAD depends mostly on the loan rather than
  the obligor (fixed for a bond, variable for a credit card); LGD = 100%− recovery rate, increasingly from
  statistical models since Basel II.

  Exam traps
  • Sibling swap, regulatory versus economic: “industrywide rules and formulas” and “only a minimum” describe
  regulatory capital; “own estimate at a confidence level tied to the target rating” describes economic capital.
  • Polarity of the target rating: a higher target rating means a higher confidence level, a higher CM and more
  economic capital, not less.
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  D3 Regulatory vs Economic Capital and Capital Adequacy
  • EL is not in EC (scope error): EL is covered by pricing and loan loss reserves; EC is the distance from EL to
  the confidence level, ULp × CM.
  • Dropped 12.5 (dropped term): K is a capital percentage; K × EAD is capital, not RWA. Forgetting 12.5
  shrinks RWA and inflates the CAR.
  • Wrong numerator or denominator: the CAR uses Tier 1 plus Tier 2 over RWA. Tier 1 alone gives the Tier 1
  ratio; dividing by unweighted total assets gives a leverage-style ratio (ORR-22 f sibling).
  • IRB exposure at face value (wrong input): an IRB book enters RWA through K × 12.5, not at a 100% weight
  on EAD.
