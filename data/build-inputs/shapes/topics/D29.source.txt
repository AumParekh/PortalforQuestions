  Revision notes
  • Crypto assets and DLT (CI-6 a): digital assets that use cryptography and rely on distributed ledger technol-
  ogy (DLT), a sequenced, standardised, cryptographically secured record on a typically decentralised network,
  distributed simultaneously to all participants. Blockchain is the best-known DLT; records are generally im-
  mutable.
  • Unbacked crypto and the six categories (CI-6 a): unbacked crypto assets’ value is set by supply and de-
  mand; they are not money (not generally used as medium of exchange, store of value or unit of account),
  are highly volatile with low intrinsic value, and are mostly speculative. No single international classifica-
  tion exists; six broad categories: unbacked crypto assets, NFTs, security tokens, utility tokens, stablecoins,
  CBDCs.
  • Issuers (CI-6 b): decentralised issuance gives anonymity, liquidity and broad access but limits disclosure and
  monitoring. Pre-mining and ICOs expose participants to price manipulation and pump-and-dump schemes.
  Regulatory response: white papers, notification, suitability tests for retail users, fair marketing, consumer
  education.
  • Exchanges and wallets (CI-6 b): exchanges give direct retail access, trade from their own inventory and
  offer custody, which exposes them to operational and cyber risk; IOSCO’s framework stresses governance,
  regulated access, controls, market integrity, transparency, AML/CFT, product offering rules, conflicts of in-
  terest and off-chain transactions. Wallets hold private keys: custodial wallets reduce loss risk but add cyber
  risk; hot (online) wallets are faster but hackable; cold (offline) wallets resist cyber risk but can be physi-
  cally lost. Regulation: segregation and safekeeping, cyber resilience, record keeping, AML/CFT, prudential
  requirements, cyber insurance, wind-down arrangements, conflicts of interest.
  • Validators, technology and institutions (CI-6 b): private blockchains reduce interoperability and can frag-
  ment and concentrate; public blockchains avoid single points of failure but carry user-behaviour risks. Reg-
  ulation aims to be technology neutral. Banks’ direct exposures call for capital deductions and higher risk
  weights; indirect exposures come through derivatives, loans and services. A crypto provider that is “sys-
  temic” may be designated an FMI, but users can switch exchange or go on-chain, so close supervision is still
  needed.
  • National approaches and bans (CI-6 c): EU (stablecoins, licensing), Switzerland (progressive; ledger-
  based securities), UK (security tokens first, broader later), Albania (licensing; legal for trading since 2020),
  Nigeria (not legal tender; banks barred), Japan (bespoke provider rules; stablecoins since 2020). Bypassing
  via unregulated exchanges and VPNs is common (Egypt, Nigeria; mining in China despite the 2021 ban).
  The IMF and BCBS judge a blanket ban ineffective; targeted restrictions (crypto derivatives in Japan and the
  UK, crypto as payment) can work in the short term.
  • BCBS treatment (CI-6 c): banks’ crypto holdings split into lower-risk assets (tokenised traditional assets

  with risk weights similar to the underlying; stablecoins possibly lower) and higher-risk assets (unbacked
  crypto assets), with a proposed 1,250% risk weight applied to the maximum of long and short positions.
  (The BCBS standard calls these Group 1 and Group 2: outside-source.)
  • Bali Fintech Agenda (CI-6 d): IMF and World Bank framework; legal certainty to build investor confi-
  dence. Considerations: monitoring (web searches, horizon scanning, innovation offices), prioritising re-
  sources, scope (all key-function entities; heavier where unbacked crypto and stablecoins are supported by
  centralised entities; user education where crypto is niche), domestic collaboration (who owns AML/CFT),
  continuous risk assessment, with cross-border cooperation.
  • Tokens and tokenisation (CI-7 a): tokens are assets or representations of assets on a digital ledger, a digital
  certificate of ownership; tokenisation is a collection of tools, not a single technology. Native tokens exist
  only on the ledger (Bitcoin, Ether, digital art); non-native tokens represent off-ledger assets (equities, bonds,
  real estate); assets can also migrate between ledgers, destroyed on the source and reissued.
  • Trust, sharedness, programmability (CI-7 a): trust (assets cannot move without consent; reliable exe-
  cution), sharedness (accessible to all relevant parties, ownership transferable and updated on the ledger),
  programmability (smart contracts: immutable code that automates instructions, e.g. coupon-paying bonds,
  qualified-investor restrictions).
  • Lifecycle frictions (CI-7 b): information asymmetries, search frictions, transaction costs, counterparty risk,
  across issuance, trading, servicing and redemption; intermediaries (investment banks, CSDs, clearinghouses,
  CCPs) reduce them for fees. Tokenisation: issuance (no separate record-keeping or custody; embedded data,
  e.g. BNP Paribas 2022 bond with ESG data), trading (simultaneous or instantaneous settlement; fewer search
  frictions in OTC markets), servicing (automated coupons and dividends). It cannot fully remove counterparty
  risk between initiation and execution: a CCP or locked-up collateral is needed, at an opportunity cost.
  • Externalities, internalities, market power (CI-7 b, c): shock transmission rises with speed, leverage
  (cheaper debt issuance), deposit migration to wholesale funding, rehypothecation and automated smart-
  contract trading (flash crashes). Infrastructure investment matters more as users grow (single point of fail-
  ure). Network externalities raise liquidity, but non-interoperable ledgers fragment liquidity and raise search
  frictions and costs. Knowledge externalities from open code can weaken incentives to innovate.
  • Retail and brokers (CI-7 d): direct retail trading amplifies internalities (trading poorly understood complex
  assets to avoid advisory fees, under-diversification). Instant broker switching and no clearinghouse reduce
  brokers’ market power; but privately owned ledgers create new market power (monopoly rents, monetised
  data, free access then fees once dominant).
  • Ledger models and trust failures (CI-7 e): sharedness via single ledger, common ledger (escrowed non-
  native assets with escrow certificates) or compatible ledgers (standardised technology, interoperable). Trust
  via single-operator systems (operator credibility and legal protection; risk even higher than permissioned),
  permissionless DLT (attack on consensus, e.g. more than 50% of PoW computing power; fee manipula-
  tion, front running, sandwich attacks; hard to enforce compliance) or permissioned DLT (collusion among
  authorised participants, checked by reputation and regulation).
  • Cyber versus ICT risk (CI-8 a): cyber risk is loss of confidentiality, integrity and availability (CIA) from a
  cyberattack; ICT risk is disruption to information and communication systems with similar effects that does
  not stem from a cyberattack. Some institutions rank cyber risk above market risk; loss data are too scarce for
  reliable estimates, which has hindered cyber insurance.
  • Cloud computing (CI-8 a): adoption has decreased cyber risk but increased ICT risk, and moves some
  but not all cyber risk to providers; cyber and ICT risk are now integrated with possible systemic effects. All
  major banks use multiclouds (less than half of asset managers and insurers), at the cost of weaker stewardship.
  About 80% of cloud customers had an outage in three years; about 20% of those were significant.
  • From cyber to financial risk (CI-8 b): the key question is how technology risks lead to financial risks
  (illiquidity, excessive leverage, loss of trust). Severity is lower for confidentiality and higher for integrity and
  availability. Contagion is non-linear, so agent-based models (still in their infancy) are proposed, also as a
  basis for cyber stress tests. A disrupted bank may see no withdrawals while depositors run on other banks.
  Institutions must be reliable enough to recover quickly, which need not mean 100% reliability.
  • Policy measures (CI-8 c): certification and standardisation as a starting point; consolidation of cybersecu-
  rity bodies; forward-looking, principles-based operational resilience rules (better for single entities than for
  systemic risk); ESRB proposal for EU-wide incident reporting; DORA covers ICT third-party (cloud) risk
  and penetration testing.

  • Macroprudential tools (CI-8 c): circuit breakers, a temporary pause on normal rules after a major attack,
  complementing deposit controls (challenge: proving the attack is force majeure); cooperative arrangements,
  from tick-the-box to judgment-based standards, ICT buffers such as Sheltered Harbor (customers of an at-
  tacked co-owner bank transact at another co-owner bank); structural measures for systemically important
  technological institutions (SITIs), multicloud or private clouds, “forced diversification” (penalties raise en-
  forceability issues). For the other CI readings see A14 (CI-1, CI-2), A28 (CI-3), D30 (CI-4) and D16 (CI-5).
  Likely distractors
  • Corrupted: “Banks’ heavy adoption of cloud computing has reduced both their cyber risk and their ICT risk.”
  True: cloud adoption has decreased cyber risk but increased ICT risk, and has merged the two with potential
  systemic implications.
  • Corrupted: “A systemic cyber shock is most severe for data confidentiality and least severe for data availability.”
  True: severity is lower for confidentiality and higher for integrity and for the availability of data and systems.
  • Corrupted: “Sheltered Harbor, which lets customers of an attacked co-owner bank transact at another co-owner
  bank, is an example of a circuit breaker, a long-term substitute for deposit controls.”
  True: Sheltered Harbor is a cooperative arrangement (an ICT buffer); circuit breakers are a temporary pause
  that complements deposit controls.
  • Corrupted: “A permissioned DLT is most exposed to an attacker acquiring more than 50% of the network’s
  computing power.”
  True: that is the consensus attack on a permissionless proof-of-work ledger; a permissioned DLT fails if autho-
  rised participants collude to alter the ledger.
  • Corrupted: “Fragmented, non-interoperable tokenised ledgers mainly amplify the transmission of financial
  shocks.”
  True: fragmentation reduces network externalities and liquidity and raises search frictions and transaction costs;
  shock amplification comes from speed, leverage and interconnectedness.
  • Corrupted: “The IMF and BCBS favour a blanket ban on crypto activities, and the BCBS assigns tokenised
  traditional assets a 1,250% risk weight.”
  True: a blanket ban is judged ineffective (targeted restrictions are preferred); the 1,250% weight is proposed for
  higher-risk unbacked crypto assets, while tokenised traditional assets get risk weights similar to the underlying.
