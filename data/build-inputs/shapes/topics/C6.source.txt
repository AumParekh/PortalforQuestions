  Revision notes
  The specific: the investor and rating agency friction is model error (S1)
  • Frictions taxonomy. The subprime securitisation chain is often described as a set of frictions between pairs
  of parties (Ashcraft and Schuermann, 2008): originator and borrower (predatory lending), arranger and third
  parties (adverse selection), servicer and mortgagor or third parties (moral hazard), asset manager and investor
  (principal-agent), and investor and credit rating agency, labelled model error, because the investor relies on
  the agency’s rating model (outside-source). The GARP text that carries this is the ORR-16 Gaussian copula
  case.
  • Gaussian copula and CDO pricing (ORR-16 c). Li’s approach used CDS prices, not observed historical
  correlations, to infer correlation. CDSs had about a decade of history: a short, benign sample (rising house
  prices, record-low defaults), so implied correlations were very low and extremely sensitive to house prices.
  The method was adopted “by everybody from bond investors and Wall Street banks to ratings agencies and
  regulators”; warnings were ignored. The copula was valid and accurate in normal conditions; when house
  prices reversed, implied correlations shot up. The problem was not the formula itself but that banks did not
  update it with the new correlations and kept pricing on old assumptions. Blame falls on quants who should
  have warned and users who relied on a black box.
  • Two other cases (ORR-16 c). Barclays and Lehman: an Excel file of assets with 179 trading contracts hidden
  rather than deleted; converted to PDF by a junior associate, the rows reappeared; spotted on 1 October after
  the deal was approved. A simple implementation error in a tool that is not even a model, yet it nearly
  cost millions. NASA Mars orbiter (USD 125 million): English versus metric units, an error in the model’s
  “assumptions”; in finance, the wrong currency or discount factor. It is impossible to know in advance which
  benign-looking mistakes will be catastrophic.
  What a model is, and how model risk arises (ORR-15 a; ORR-16 a)
  • Model. “A quantitative method, system, or approach that applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathe-
  matical theories, techniques, and assumptions to process input data into quantitative estimates”; also covers
  approaches with partly or wholly qualitative or expert-judgment inputs, provided the output is quantitative.
  Three components: information input, processing, reporting. Industry consensus now stresses the uncertain
  nature of the estimate rather than the technique.
  • Model risk. “The potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused model
  outputs and reports”: financial loss, poor decisions, reputational damage. Two reasons: (1) fundamental
  errors anywhere from design to implementation, including shortcuts, approximations and poor inputs; (2)
  incorrect or inappropriate use, even of a sound model, especially outside the environment it was designed
  for (new products or markets, or changed conditions). ORR-16 names the same pair execution risk (the
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  C6  Model Risk Management
  model does not do what it was designed to do: coding, implementation, wrong data) and conceptual errors
  (wrong assumptions or techniques, often matters of judgment, where MRM’s role is transparency).
  • Size and aggregation. Model risk increases with complexity, input and assumption uncertainty, broader use
  and larger potential impact. Consider it per model and in aggregate: common assumptions, data or methods
  can hit several models at once. It is a tail risk, so it is often underestimated; most errors are benign, which
  breeds misplaced confidence.
  • Effective challenge. The guiding principle: critical analysis by objective, informed parties. It depends
  on incentives (stronger with greater separation from development, and with compensation and culture),
  competence and influence (explicit authority, stature, support from senior management). Model risk cannot
  be eliminated; other tools are limits on use, monitoring, revision and supplementary analysis.
  The three components (S2; ORR-15 b, c)
  • Component 1: development, implementation and use. Clear statement of purpose; documented design,
  theory and logic; comparison with alternative theories; rigorous data assessment (proxies identified and
  justified, especially for external or vendor data); testing of accuracy, robustness, stability, extreme inputs
  and the impact on downstream models. Any single test is rarely sufficient. User challenge is useful but
  tends to be weak, partial and asymmetric (users rarely challenge favourable outcomes). Report ranges, not
  spurious point estimates. Conservatism must be defined and measured; an extreme point on a misestimated
  distribution is not conservative.
  • Component 2: validation (ORR-15 d). Three core elements: conceptual soundness (including develop-
  mental evidence, sensitivity analysis, stress testing of inputs); ongoing monitoring (process verification of
  data, code and systems, which starts when the model enters production; benchmarking; analysis of overrides,
  where a high override rate signals redevelopment); outcomes analysis (including back-testing on data not
  used in development, at a frequency matching the forecast horizon; parallel outcomes analysis for adjusted
  models; early-warning metrics for long-horizon models). All components, in-house or vendor, are validated.
  Periodic review at least annually; material changes validated.
  • Component 3: governance, policies and controls. The board is ultimately responsible and delegates exe-
  cution to senior management, who report on significant model risk, individually and in aggregate. Policies
  approved by the board or its delegates and reviewed annually. Roles: ownership (business units; model
  owner accountable for use and performance), controls (risk-control staff with authority to restrict model
  use), compliance. A firm-wide model inventory and documentation sufficient for someone unfamiliar with
  the model.
  Validation independence (S3) and the MRM function (ORR-15 d; ORR-16 b)
  • Independence. Validation is generally done by people not responsible for development or use, with no stake
  in the result. Independence is not an end in itself; it may be supported by separate reporting lines but is
  judged by actions and outcomes, for example models actually changed after validation. Some validation
  work may be done best by developers and users, provided it is critically reviewed by an independent party
  who adds further work. The developer is an important source of information but not an objective or sole one.
  • Before and after first use. Rigor matches the risk of use. Significant deficiencies: do not use, or use
  only under tight constraints; too severe: reject. If validation cannot be completed before use (data paucity),
  document it, tell users and senior management, and apply compensating controls. Validation is an important
  check in benign periods, when risk estimates turn optimistic.
  • Vendor models. Same principles. Require developmental evidence and testing from the vendor; the bank
  validates its own use, relying more on sensitivity analysis and benchmarking where code is proprietary;
  document customisation; keep knowledge in-house and a contingency plan if the vendor disappears. See
  also D2 for the outsourcing of model risk management services.
  • Internal audit. Assesses the overall effectiveness of the framework (both types of model risk, individual and
  aggregate); does not duplicate MRM. Staff who perform validation work must not assess the overall frame-
  work. Verifies inventory, policy compliance, timeliness of validations, data reliability, and the objectivity,
  competence and standing of validators. External resources: a designated internal party verifies scope, tracks
  issues and integrates results.
  • MRM function in practice (ORR-16 b). Sits in the second line, independent of development; sets docu-
  mentation, data and versioning standards. Tiering by materiality, complexity, client-facing use and regula-
  tory use: top tier full-scope validation every 2 to 3 years, mid tier every 4 or 5, low tier more rarely; every
  model has an annual review. Monitoring reports from model owners (quarterly or annual for capital models,
FRM Part II · Volume 2: Tiers B to D  C6  Model Risk Management
  monthly or weekly for fraud). Move from point-in-time to continuous MRM. Developers and owners are
  the first line and own the risk; mature firms add a first-line QA/QC team. The ORX taxonomy makes model
  risk a Level 1 risk (design, implementation and application errors); Basel lists “model errors” as a Level 3
  example (see A22).
  Exam traps
  • Scope/condition on the copula: it was accurate in normal markets; it failed when correlations spiked and
  users did not update or understand it. “Always wrong” and “the formula itself was the problem” both drop the
  condition.
  • Sibling swap, execution versus conceptual: Barclays (hidden rows) is an implementation error in a non-model
  tool; the copula is a conceptual, assumption-driven failure; NASA is a unit assumption. Case cross-mapping
  uses one case’s lesson for another.
  • Polarity flip on incentives: effective challenge is stronger with greater separation from development, not with
  proximity.
  • Role misassignment: the model owner (first line) is accountable for use and performance; risk-control staff
  hold the authority to restrict use; internal audit evaluates the framework and must not both validate and assess
  it; the board is ultimately responsible.
  • Absolute claims: validation does not eliminate model risk; independence does not mean developers may do no
  validation work; a separate reporting line alone does not prove independence.
