  Revision notes
  The specifics first: high public debt and the fiscal-monetary tension (CI-5 c)
  • Interest-growth differential (S2). The difference between interest rates and economic (GDP) growth. When
  rates are below growth, as before the pandemic, public finances can thrive; when rates rise above growth,
  the economy can slow and public spending is squeezed. The Book 5 summary defines it with policy rates;
  the BIS original frames it as the interest rate paid on public debt minus growth (outside-source).
  • Three primary challenges. (1) Public finances are strained by record-high public debt, pressure to maintain
  spending, higher interest rates and slowing growth prospects; (2) high public debt can work against monetary
  tightening when fighting inflation; (3) the level of public debt introduces financial stability risks.
  • Spending pressure. Cutting spending is hard: rising costs, inflation-linked wages and transfers, age-related
  spending expected to rise by 4% to 5% of GDP over 20 years, the costly global push for sustainability, and
  higher defense spending as geopolitical tensions rise. Inflation makes fiscal balances look better than reality
  (nominal revenues respond quickly). Post-GFC high debt has lowered sovereign ratings, more sharply in
  AEs.
  • Amplifiers of policy rates on public borrowing. Post-GFC central bank purchases reached 25% to 50%
  of outstanding government debt in major AEs; this shortened the effective maturity of public debt, reduced
  borrowing costs and lowered remittances from central banks to governments. Debt service is estimated to
  grow to 6% of GDP, worse if medium-term growth disappoints (slower labor force growth, EME slowdown).
  History shows higher inflation leads to higher public debt.
  • Countercyclical effects. Large deficits support demand but raise inflation; the normal response is higher
  policy rates, which weaken the economy against the aim of fiscal policy, more acutely the longer inflation
  persists. In EMEs fiscal stimulus feeds inflation more, through exchange-rate depreciation.
  • Fiscal dominance (S1). Monetary policy is constrained by fiscally imposed constraints: political spending
  pressure places a floor on fiscal flexibility, and rate rises could spark a sovereign debt crisis. Remedy: strong
  institutional safeguards for central bank independence so fiscal authorities cannot “hijack” monetary policy.
  • Financial stability channel. Sovereign debt is a store of value, collateral, the most liquid asset and a pricing
  benchmark; the government is the ultimate backstop (the central bank as lender and market maker of last
  resort), so banks enjoy higher ratings with it. Banks rely on the state for liquidity and on sovereign bonds as
  collateral: a downgrade at one can spiral into the other, most pronounced in EMEs. High debt makes sharp
  yield moves more likely; long-term yields rise more in high-debt countries when policy tightens; governments
  locked in long-term debt at ultra-low rates, and long bonds are the most rate-sensitive.
  • Policy implications. Monetary policy should stay focused on price stability and tolerate inflation below target
  while watching for overheating; fiscal policy should be as conservative as possible to keep headroom; relying
  on inflation to erode debt is counterproductive. Central banks should minimize balance sheets: smaller
  footprint and no crowding out of private lenders, less budget reliance on central bank transfers, room to
  expand in a future crisis. Prefer high-quality, growth-enhancing spending (infrastructure, education) over
  transfers; keep central banks independent; use guardrails such as fiscal rules or oversight panels.

  Channels and the region of stability (CI-5 a)
  • Two channels. Fiscal: a direct impact through government spending, taxes, the production of some ser-
  vices, and transfer payments. Monetary: the money supply via open market operations and policy rates,
  spilling into bond yields, borrowing costs, asset prices and exchange rates. They overlap: higher rates raise
  government borrowing costs and slow tax revenue; central bank profits flow to the government; too much
  fiscal stimulus forces a monetary response.
  • Region of stability. The joint policy stances that deliver stable inflation and stable growth. Its boundaries
  vary by country, cannot be captured in a single metric, change in shape, size and locus (gradually, e.g.
  demographics, or rapidly, e.g. a commodity spike or pandemic), and can be altered by regulation and FX
  intervention. Keep a margin of safety: do not push policy to the outer limit.
  Breaching the boundaries (CI-5 b)
  • AEs, phase 1 (1960s to mid-1980s). Belief in fine-tuning; very high inflation fought with very high policy
  rates; a surge in public debt and a shift to prioritize price stability.
  • AEs, phase 2 (mid-1980s to the pandemic). Financial liberalization and globalization (cheap EME labor)
  lowered inflation; boundary testing moved to risk-taking and financial imbalances; inflation-induced re-
  cessions gave way to longer-lasting financial recessions. Rates were cut in downturns and not raised in
  recoveries, enabling massive debt build-up. The pandemic stimulus then produced elevated inflation on top
  of record debt and high asset prices.
  • Why policy kept drifting. Loss of traction: rate changes have less effect at low rates (50 or 75 bp moves
  needed), and fiscal stimulus weakens when debt is high. Self-reinforcing interaction: lower rates relax
  fiscal constraints and encourage debt; high debt makes monetary normalization harder.
  • EMEs. Liberalization brought financial cycles and crises (Southern Cone early 1980s, Tequila 1994, Asia
  late 1990s); Latin America had hyperinflation with defaults. FX dependency means: a smaller region of
  stability; breaches bring capital outflows and depreciation; AE (especially US) policy spills over; hence
  the shift to flexible exchange rates and inflation targeting (Latin America tightened earlier and harder in the
  pandemic). See also D30 for how weak fiscal buffers raise sovereign risk premiums after geopolitical shocks.
  Exam traps
  • Role misassignment on fiscal dominance: it is monetary policy that is constrained by fiscal policy, not the
  other way round.
  • Polarity flip on the differential: rates below growth help public finances; rates above growth strain them.
  • Polarity flip on central bank purchases: QE shortened the maturity of public debt and lowered remittances
  to governments; limiting the balance sheet does the opposite.
  • Sibling swap AEs vs EMEs: the region of stability is smaller in EMEs; the bank-sovereign loop is most pro-
  nounced in EMEs; EMEs moved to flexible exchange rates and inflation targeting.
  • Polarity flip on traction: with high debt, fiscal stimulus is less powerful and monetary policy needs more
  extreme moves.
  • Absolute claim: “inflating away the debt” is counterproductive; the government backstop raises bank ratings,
  not lowers them.
