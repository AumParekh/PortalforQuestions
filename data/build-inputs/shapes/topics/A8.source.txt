  Revision notes
  The specifics first: correlation swaps and quantos (MR-8 c, d)
  • Correlation swap structure (MR-8 d). A fixed (known) correlation is exchanged for the correlation that
  actually occurs, the realised or stochastic (unknown) correlation. Realised correlation is the average of the
  pairwise Pearson correlations of the assets’ log-returns over the swap life, using only the i > j cells of the
  matrix (Equation 8.6). Payoff to the fixed-rate payer at maturity: N (ρrealised − ρfixed ) (Equation 8.7). Example
  8.1: pairs 0.5, 0.3, 0.1 give ρrealised = 0.3; fixed 10%, USD 1,000,000 notional, payer receives USD 200,000.
  • Buyer and seller. Paying fixed is “buying correlation”: the present value rises for the correlation buyer if
  realised correlation increases. The fixed-rate receiver is “selling correlation”.
  • Uses. When stock prices decrease, correlation between the stocks typically increases, so a fixed correlation
  payer protects themselves indirectly against a stock market decline. Another way of buying correlation: buy
  puts on an index (e.g., the Dow) and sell puts on its individual stocks, because correlation and volatility are
  positively related. The London Whale (Bruno Iksil, 2012) ran the mirror trade in credit: sold CDS on the
  CDX.NA.IG.9 index and bought single-name CDS; a short squeeze cost JP Morgan over USD 2 billion.
  • Valuation caveat. There is no industry-standard valuation model for correlation swaps; there is no term
  structure of correlation; traders use historical data or emerging stochastic correlation models.
  • Quanto option (MR-8 c). Lets a domestic investor exchange a foreign-currency option payoff back into the
  home currency at a fixed exchange rate, so it protects against currency risk (an American buying a Nikkei
  call but worried about a falling yen). The seller does not know (1) how deep in the money the call will be,
  i.e., how much yen must be converted, nor (2) the exchange rate at maturity. With X = domestic per unit
  foreign (USD per 1 yen): a positive correlation between the Nikkei and X means a rising Nikkei comes with
  a rising yen, which favours the seller (fewer yen buy the dollar payoff), so the more positive the correlation,
  the lower the quanto price; a negative correlation makes it more expensive.
  • Other multi-asset (rainbow, mountain-range) options (MR-8 c). Better of two, worse of two, call on the
  maximum of two, exchange option, spread call, better of two or cash, dual strike call, basket option. Except
  for the option on the worse of two and the basket option, the lower the correlation, the higher the price. The
  exchange option price is close to 0 at high correlation and USD 15.08 at −1; its implied volatility of S2 /S1
  takes the covariance as input (Equation 8.5a).
  The specific: concentration ratio (MR-8 g)
  • Concentration risk and the concentration ratio. Concentration risk is the risk of financial loss due to a
  concentrated exposure to a specific group of counterparties. Concentration ratio = 1/n for n loans of equal
  size (ten equal loans: 0.1; one loan: 1). The lower the ratio, the more diversified the default risk, assuming

  default correlation is smaller than 1. Sector concentration: lending to more sectors reduces default risk
  because intra-sector defaults are more highly correlated.
  • Example 8.3. One USD 10 million loan to W with PD 10%: ratio 1, worst case EL USD 1,000,000. Two
  USD 5 million loans to X and Y (PD 10% each): ratio 1/2; pthe worst case,pjoint default, shrinks if 0 < ρ < 1.
  Joint default of two binomial events: P (X ∩ Y ) = ρXY PX (1 − PX ) PY (1 − PY ) + PX PY (Equation
  8.11): 10% at ρ = 1 (no benefit from the lower ratio), 5.5% at 0.5, 1% at 0, 0.1% at −0.1. Both a lower
  concentration ratio and a lower correlation reduce the creditor’s worst case; higher copula correlation raises
  credit VaR.
  • Systemic risk (MR-8 g). The risk that a financial market or an entire financial system collapses (credit
  markets frozen in September 2008; the Dow fell 53.54% from 14,093 to 6,547 by March 2009; only Walmart
  of the Dow 30 rose). In a systemic decline correlations between stocks increase sharply: Dow correlation
  rose from a non-crisis average of 27% to over 50% (96.97% at the February 2009 peak), so “well diversified”
  portfolios suffered unexpected losses.
  Surrounding material (MR-8 a, b, e, f)
  • Financial correlation risk (MR-8 a). The risk of financial loss due to adverse movements in correlation
  between two or more variables, financial or non-financial (Mexican and Greek bonds in 2012; commodities
  and interest rates; sovereign debt and the euro hurting US exporters). Static correlations measure association
  at a point in or within a period (VaR, copula for CDOs, Lucas binomial); dynamic correlations measure
  co-movement in time (pairs trading, Heston, stochastic correlation). Five areas: investments, trading, risk
  management, the GFC, regulation.
  • Wrong-way risk. Investor hedges a Spanish bond with a CDS bought from BNP Paribas: positive default
  correlation between reference entity and counterparty is wrong-way risk. For correlation above about −0.3,
  the higher the correlation, the lower the CDS spread; at 1 the CDS is worthless. From −0.3 to −1 the spread
  also falls slightly: the relationship is non-monotonic.
  • Investments. Lower (preferably negative) correlation raises the portfolio return/risk ratio, one of the few
  “free lunches” (Figure 8.3: close to 250% at high negative correlation, 50% at high positive). Table 8.1:
  ρXY = −0.7403, σP = 16.66%.

  • The GFC (MR-8 b). Causes: benign 2003 to 2006 environment, speculation and bubbles, new structured

  • Market risk and credit risk (MR-8 f). VaR and ES take the covariance matrix as input, so they implicitly
  incorporate correlation risk. Credit risk = migration risk + default risk. Default correlations (Table 8.3)
  are higher within sectors than between them (energy is the exception, often negative), so a sector-diversified
  loan book is advised. Default term structure rises over time for investment grade (A-rated) and is typically
  inverse for distressed (CC-rated) names. See also B3 (joint PD and copulas) and D11 (empirical correlation
  behaviour).

  Exam traps
  • Wrong normaliser or wrong cells: averaging all n2 cells (diagonal 1s included), using 2/n2 or 1/(n2 − n),
  or summing both triangles and still applying 2/(n2 − n). Only the i > j cells, once each.
  • Party flip (role misassignment): the fixed-rate payer buys correlation and gains when realised exceeds fixed;
  the receiver sells correlation and has the mirror payoff. Read whose payoff the stem asks for. Volatilities and
  maturity are red herrings in Equation 8.7.
  • Dropped strike: N × ρrealised is not the payoff; the fixed correlation must be subtracted.
  • Quanto polarity: the more positive the correlation between the underlying and the exchange rate (domestic per
  foreign), the cheaper the quanto; positive correlation favours the seller. Rainbow-option exceptions: worse of
  two and basket options get cheaper as correlation falls.
  • Concentration ratio direction and scope: the ratio is 1/n, not n; a lower ratio helps only if default correlation
  is below 1 (at ρ = 1 the two loans default together and the worst case is unchanged). In Equation 8.11 do not
  drop the PX PY term or use variances in place of standard deviations.
  • GFC direction: in May 2005 correlation fell and the long equity, short mezzanine trade lost on both legs; in
  2007 to 2009 correlations rose and hurt the super-senior tranches.
