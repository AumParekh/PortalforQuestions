  Revision notes
  • The specific: Credit VaR as a quantile minus EL (CR-11 c, S1). Malz: portfolio Credit VaR “is a quantile
  of the credit loss, minus the expected loss of the portfolio”. Because EL is always subtracted, Credit VaR
  can be negative: a single credit with π = 2% has a 95% Credit VaR of 0 − 20,000,000 = −20,000,000 (a
  gain), since the 95th percentile loss is zero.
  • Hull’s wording (CR-10 b; CR-12 g). Hull defines credit risk VaR “similarly to market risk VaR”: “the credit
  risk loss over a certain time period that will not be exceeded with a certain confidence level”. His Vasicek
  estimate L(1 − R)V (X, T ) is a loss quantile with no EL deduction (Example 12.5: USD 100 million retail
  book, PD 2%, recovery 60%, ρ = 0.1: WCDR 12.8%, 99.9% credit VaR USD 5.13 million). When a question
  says Credit VaR and gives EL or PD, apply the Malz definition (quantile minus EL); when it reproduces Hull’s
  L(1 − R)V (X, T ), it is asking for the loss quantile.
  • Market VaR versus credit VaR (CR-10 a). Definition: same concept (loss not exceeded at a confidence
  level); some credit models count only default losses, others also downgrades and spread changes. Horizon:
  market VaR often one day; credit VaR for instruments not held for trading usually one year (Basel: one
  year, 99.9%). Tools: historical simulation is the main market-risk tool; credit VaR needs “a more elaborate
  model”. Scenario PDs should be real-world; risk-neutral estimates are then used to value the portfolio on the
  horizon date.
  • Credit correlation (CR-10 a; CR-11 c). Defaults are not independent: in a downturn most companies
  become more likely to default. Credit correlation rises in stressed conditions and so does the risk of a credit
  portfolio. Malz: correlation “affects the volatility and extreme quantiles of loss rather than the expected
  loss”.
  • Two extremes of correlation (CR-11 c). Default correlation 1: the portfolio behaves as if n = 1 (all-or-
  nothing), no diversification. Default correlation 0: the number of defaults is binomial(n, π) and significant
  diversification is possible. Malz’s USD 1 billion book, π = 2%, zero recovery, n = 50: 95th percentile = 3
  defaults, loss USD 60 million, Credit VaR 60 − 20 = USD 40 million; n = 1,000: 28 defaults, Credit VaR
  USD 8 million.
  • Granularity, effect 1 (CR-11 h, S3). For a given default probability, Credit VaR “decreases as the credit
  portfolio becomes more granular”. The convergence is more drastic with a high default probability; con-
  versely “it is harder to reduce VaR by making the portfolio more granular, if the default probability is low”.
  Expected loss is unchanged by granularity (π× portfolio value in every panel of Malz’s Table 11.1).
  • Granularity, effect 2 (CR-11 d, h, S4). With independent credits the loss converges to EL “almost certainly”
  and Credit VaR goes to zero. In the single-factor model the granular portfolio’s loss rate converges to the
  conditional default probability p(m), so “portfolio returns are driven by the market factor”: idiosyncratic
  risk diversifies away, systematic risk does not, and Credit VaR stays positive. Malz Ch.9 adds that a more
  granular collateral pool lets a securitisation carry a larger senior tranche with no increase in Credit VaR (see
  D15).

  • Simulation of joint defaults (CR-11 g). Draw correlated standard normals (copula correlation), map each to
  a default time or rating outcome, compute the loss on each thread, repeat, and read Credit VaR off the sorted
  simulated losses (Malz, Ch.9, 22.3: the 10th largest of 1,000 losses for 99%, minus EL). Hull’s CreditRisk+
  Monte Carlo: sample an overall default rate, link each category’s rate to it, sample defaults and LGDs, total
  the loss, repeat, read VaR.
  • Rating transition matrices (CR-10 c). Historical one-year matrices (S&P 1981 to 2020: A stays A with
  92.61%, defaults with 0.05%). Assuming independence across periods, the n-year matrix is the nth power;
  six months needs the square root, three months the fourth root. Independence is not exactly true: recently
  downgraded companies are more likely to be downgraded again (ratings momentum).
  • CreditMetrics mechanics (CR-10 c; CR-12 g). Sample correlated normals xA , xB (copula correlation
  set to the equity-return correlation, e.g. 0.2), convert transition probabilities into thresholds with N −1 of
  cumulative probabilities, revalue every counterparty at the horizon; upgrades count as negative losses. See
  D20 for the model comparison.
  • Credit spread risk (CR-10 f). Trading-book credit products depend critically on spreads.√  Approach 1:
  historical simulation of 500 days of spread changes for a one-day 99% VaR, scaled by 10. Problems: a
  company alive today did not default in the past, so no future default is assumed (regulators add jump-to-
  default risk); spread data are not updated daily for all names. Approach 2: a 10-day transition matrix plus
  a distribution of spread changes per rating category, simulated; correlation through a Gaussian copula on
  ratings and near-perfectly correlated spread moves across categories.
  • Example 10.1 (CR-10 f). Two-year zero, USD 1,000 principal, yield 5%, price 907.03; worst outcome
  default (0.1%, value 400, loss 507.03); next worst B at 500 bp (0.267%, loss 44.17). VaR is 507.03 above
  99.9% confidence, 44.17 between 99.633% and 99.9%, and 2.92 at 99%.
  • Constant level of risk (CR-10 f). Rebalancing monthly back to BBB: default probability 12 × 0.0001 ×
  0.999911 = 0.0012 versus 0.0017 buy-and-hold; one downgrade to BB about 0.040 versus 0.0378. Fewer
  big losses, more small ones: VaR and ES are generally smaller under the constant level of risk strategy.

  Exam traps
  • Dropped term (EL): reporting the worst-case loss as Credit VaR. In Malz’s framework the EL is always sub-
  tracted, which is also why Credit VaR can be negative.
  • Quantile threshold: the 99% quantile is the smallest number of defaults whose cumulative probability is at
  least 99%, not the last row below 99%.
  • Wrong-input twin: recovery rate in place of LGD in both the worst-case loss and the EL.
  • Sibling swap: granularity changes the dispersion of losses, not the expected loss.
  • Polarity flip: granularity helps most when the default probability is high; with a low PD it is harder to reduce
  Credit VaR.
  • Absolute claim: “a granular portfolio has zero Credit VaR” is true only for independent defaults; with correla-
  tion the common factor remains.
